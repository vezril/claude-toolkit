## TL;DR: the eight things that matter

1. **Two parts, one cut.** A haiku sets two elements against each other across a pause (or flows as a single image). Never three independent parts. **High** [1][5][9]
2. **Show the cause, not the feeling.** Concrete sensory images; avoid words that interpret ("beautiful", "sad"). **High** (practitioner consensus) [8]
3. **Season word (kigo)** is the traditional anchor. Most Japanese haiku still use one; English haiku often omit it. **High** [1][2]
4. **5-7-5 English syllables is a misconception.** Japanese counts *on* (morae), not syllables. About 12 English syllables approximates 17 *on*. **High** [1][6]
5. **English conventions:** no title, almost never rhyme, little overt metaphor or simile, plain words, present tense. **Medium** (English-language norm, not Japanese law) [8][10]
6. **Direct personal experience is not required.** Bashō and Buson used imagination, fiction, and allusion. **Medium-High** (well argued, but contested in English haiku circles) [7]
7. **Nature and season point to haiku; human foibles and satire point to senryu.** The boundary is blurry. **Medium** [18][19]
8. **Haiku is a genre, not just a syllable form.** Targets (cut, kigo, objective imagery) matter more than counting. **Medium-High** [8][10]

## 1. What a haiku is

- Began as the **hokku**, the opening stanza of collaborative linked verse (renga / haikai no renga). By Bashō's time (1644 to 1694) hokku were appearing as independent poems. **High** [1]
- **Masaoka Shiki** (1867 to 1902) renamed the standalone hokku "haiku" in the late 19th century and cut it loose from linked verse. The term is now applied retroactively to older hokku. **High** [1]
- Japanese haiku are traditionally printed as **a single line**; English haiku are usually three lines, with variations. **High** [1]
- **HSA definition (2004):** a short poem using imagistic language to convey the essence of an experience of nature or the season, intuitively linked to the human condition. **High** (definition quoted in two secondary sources; original page only archived) [6]

## 2. Structure

### 2.1 Sound units: *on*, not syllables (**High**) [1]

| | Japanese *on* | English syllables |
|---|---|---|
| "haibun" | ha-i-bu-n = 4 | 2 |
| "on" | o-n = 2 | 1 |
| long vowel / doubled consonant / final -n | each counts as an extra *on* | not counted separately |

Bashō's frog poem scans as:

```
fu-ru-i-ke ya        (5)
ka-wa-zu to-bi-ko-mu (7)
mi-zu no o-to        (5)
```

Even classical masters broke the count; Bashō has 6-7-5 poems. **High** [1]

**Practical target in English:** roughly 10 to 14 syllables, short/long/short, readable in one breath. Welch estimates 95 to 98 percent of poems in leading English haiku journals are *not* 5-7-5. **Medium** (his estimate) [10]

### 2.2 The cut: *kire* and *kireji* (**High** for function, **Medium** for the list)

- A **kireji** ("cutting word") usually ends one of the three phrases, acting like spoken punctuation, a caesura, or a volta. **High** [1][6]
- **Paradox of the cut:** it both separates and joins, setting up an implied comparison, equation, or contrast between the two parts. **High** [5]
- **Position effects:**
  - **ya** mid-poem: holds up the first image (frog poem: *furuike **ya***). [1][12]
  - **kana / keri** at the end: exclamatory closure that sends the reader back to the start, a circular effect (Shirane). [5][12]
  - *ya* with *kana*, or *kana* with *keri*, are not normally combined. **Medium** [12]
- **The classical 18 kireji** (Shirane, *Traces of Dreams*, p.100, via secondary source): kana, mogana, zo, ka, yo, ya, keri, ran, tsu, nu, zu, ji, se, re, he, ke, ikani, shi. Lists vary by school; Bashō argued any *on* can cut if it functions as a cut. **Medium** [15][12]
- **English substitutes:** dash, ellipsis, colon, or simply the line break / syntax. **High** [1][6]

### 2.3 Two composition modes (**Medium-High**) [12][14]

| Mode | Japanese | What it is | Risk |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Combination** | *toriawase* (取り合わせ) | Two elements put together; reader bridges the gap. | Gap too small = redundant; too wide = random. |
| **Single object** | *ichibutsu jitate* (一物仕立て) | One subject flowing start to finish, no leap. | Needs an unusually fresh observation or it reads as a plain statement. |

- Bashō's disciples disagreed: **Kyoriku** called combination the core Bashō technique; **Kyorai** countered that Bashō also prized smooth single-object hokku. So neither mode is "the" rule. **Medium** (Shirane via secondary source) [14]
- **Terminology dispute:** "juxtaposition" is the common English gloss for toriawase, but Susumu Takiguchi argues "combination" is more accurate, since toriawase means joining into one whole rather than contrasting side by side. **Medium** [16]

### 2.4 Lineation in English (**Medium**) [1][9]

- Three lines is the effective norm; one-line haiku also exist.
- The line break carries weight: it is often your only "kireji".

## 3. Seasons and kigo

### 3.1 Definitions (**High**) [2][3][4]

- **Kigo**: a word or phrase tied to a season, used in renga, renku, and haiku. It buys economy: one word imports a whole field of associations (Higginson calls this *logopoeia*).
- **Saijiki**: a season-word almanac. Each entry: description, related words, example poems. A **kiyose** is the same without the example poems.
- Seasonal organization in Japanese poetry goes back to the *Man'yōshū* (8th c.) and *Kokinshū* (905). **High** [2]

### 3.2 The five seasons and seven categories (**High**) [3][4][13]

Seasons: **Spring, Summer, Autumn, Winter, New Year** (modern saijiki sometimes add a *muki* / no-season section). New Year became a separate season when saijiki moved to the solar calendar, to untangle "early spring" kigo from the old lunar New Year. **Medium** [13]

| Category | Japanese | Covers |
|---|---|---|
| The season | 時候 jikō | time, climate ("short night", "hot") |
| Heavens | 天文 tenmon | sky, weather, celestial |
| Earth | 地理 chiri | landscape, water, fields |
| Humanity | 生活 seikatsu | daily life, clothing, food, work |
| Observances | 行事 gyōji | festivals, holidays |
| Animals | 動物 dōbutsu | |
| Plants | 植物 shokubutsu | |

### 3.3 The traditional calendar (**High**) [2]

Seasons follow the old lunisolar reckoning, with solstices and equinoxes at **mid-season**, not at the start:

| Season | Approx. dates |
|---|---|
| Spring | 4 Feb to 5 May |
| Summer | 6 May to 7 Aug |
| Autumn | 8 Aug to 6 Nov |
| Winter | 7 Nov to 3 Feb |

Each season splits into early / middle / late. Kigo "sense" is calibrated to the Kyoto to Tokyo region. **High** [2]

### 3.4 Non-obvious kigo (**High** unless marked)

- **Moon** (tsuki) alone = **autumn** (clear nights, harvest moon). [2]
- **Frog** = **spring** (mating song). [2]
- **Pumpkin** = autumn harvest in Japan. [2]
- **"Flowers"** (hana) alone = **cherry blossoms, spring**. **Medium** (from memory, verify in a saijiki)
- **Homophone trap:** *tsuyu* 梅雨 (rainy season) = summer; *tsuyu* 露 (dew) = autumn. **Medium** (from memory)

### 3.5 Sample kigo by season (**Medium**, curated)

Items in **bold** appear in the sources cited here; the rest are standard saijiki entries from memory. Verify against Yamamoto's *Five Hundred Essential Japanese Season Words* [21] or Higginson's *Kiyose*.

| Season | Examples |
|---|---|
| Spring | haze (kasumi), plum blossom (ume), cherry blossom, bush warbler (uguisu), **frog**, spring rain, skylark |
| Summer | **short night**, **rainy season**, **evening downpour**, **cuckoo (hototogisu)**, **mosquito**, **peony**, **lotus**, cicada, firefly |
| Autumn | **moon**, **Milky Way (amanogawa)**, **autumn dusk (aki no kure)**, dew, insect voices, red dragonfly, chrysanthemum |
| Winter | **first winter shower (hatsushigure)**, snow, frost, withered field (kareno), winter seclusion |
| New Year | **kadomatsu**, **zōni**, **toso**, first sunrise (hatsuhi), first dream (hatsuyume) |

### 3.6 Working rules for kigo

- **Use one kigo per poem**, unless one clearly dominates. **Medium** (Welch) [8]
- **Subtle beats explicit:** naming "autumn" works, but a good season word implies it. **Medium** [8]
- **Regional ambiguity:** "dry grass" reads winter in New Jersey, summer in California. **Medium** [8]
- **Regional kigo lists exist** (e.g., Southern California Haiku Study Group: June gloom, jacaranda, wildfire). **High** [2]
- **No-season haiku (muki) are legitimate.** Japanese poets disagree on whether a kigo is required; writers outside Japan freely omit it. The Modern Haiku Association of Japan published non-season keywords in 2004. **High** [2][22]

### 3.7 Adapting kigo to Quebec (**Low**, my synthesis)

The Kyoto-calibrated calendar does not match Montreal's climate. Pick one convention (felt seasons, or astronomical) and stay consistent. Candidate local season words:

| Season | Candidates |
|---|---|
| Early spring | sugaring off / cabane à sucre, sap buckets, slush, ice breakup on the river |
| Late spring | black flies, lilacs |
| Summer | Saint-Jean bonfires (24 June), moving day (1 July), terrasse, fireflies |
| Autumn | red maples, first frost, apple picking, geese overhead |
| Winter | freezing rain (verglas), snow-clearing trucks at night, first snow |
| New Year | réveillon, tourtière |

## 4. Core motifs and aesthetics

### 4.1 Aesthetic terms

| Term | Rough meaning | Confidence | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **sabi** | faded or withered beauty; quiet, patinated loneliness | Medium | [17] |
| **wabi** | virtue in austerity and honourable poverty | Medium | [17] |
| **karumi** | lightness: everyday subjects and diction, no abstraction or posturing, relaxed, seemingly artless expression. Bashō's late ideal. | Medium-High | Shirane via [11]; [17] |
| **omomi** | heaviness (allegory, symbolism, logic) that karumi avoids | Medium | [11] |
| **shasei** | "sketching from life": Shiki's plein-air style, influenced by Buson and Western painting | High | [1] |
| **fueki ryūkō** | "the unchanging and the ever-changing": Bashō's late ideal of enduring truth expressed through the new | Medium | [7] |
| **honkadori / allusion** | echoing earlier poems; much of Bashō's and Buson's depth depends on it | Medium-High | [7] |
| **yūgen, mono no aware** | mystery / poignancy of transience | **Low** for haiku specifically | Broader Japanese aesthetics, often overapplied to haiku in popular writing |

### 4.2 Bashō's method

- **"Learn about the pine from the pine"**: recorded by his disciple Dohō in the *Sanzōshi* (1702). The poet sets aside self-preoccupation and enters the object until the poem arises from it. **Medium** (widely cited; read here via secondary sources) [20]
- Late Bashō, per Shirane: when a style grows heavy and falls into logic and intricacy, abandon it and write lightly, with ordinary words. **Medium-High** [11]

### 4.3 Recurring motif families (**Medium**, synthesis from [1][2][7])

- **Seasonal passage and transience**: the kigo's native job.
- **Small creatures**: frogs, crows, insects, sparrows (strongly associated with Issa's compassionate, humanist tone). [1]
- **Travel and wayfaring**: Bashō's travel diaries (*Oku no Hosomichi*) and haibun. [1]
- **Famous places (utamakura)**: poets travelled to sites made famous by earlier poems to share in that communal memory. [7]
- **Sound against silence, light against dark**: the frog poem's splash is the classic case.
- **Solitude against community**: haikai was historically a social, collaborative art.

## 5. Contested beliefs: challenge these

| Common belief | Status | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| "A haiku must be 5-7-5 syllables." | False for English; Japanese counts *on*, and masters broke the count. [1][10] | High |
| "A haiku must record a real, personal moment." | Contested. Shirane shows Buson wrote a widower's poem while his wife was alive (she outlived him by 31 years), and Bashō rewrote gender, place, and time. Welch also permits changing facts for poetic truth. [7][10] | Medium-High |
| "Haiku is Zen." | Overstated. Shirane attributes the Zen emphasis to English-language pioneers (Suzuki, Watts, the Beats). Shiki was agnostic and separated haiku from Buddhism. [1][7] | Medium-High |
| "Never use metaphor." | Overstated. Bashō and Buson reused images (Buson's rose, Bashō's beggar) as complex metaphors and symbols. English convention still discourages *overt* simile. [7][8] | Medium |
| "The haiku moment" is the essence. | An English-language concept (Kenneth Yasuda, 1957), not widely promoted in Japanese haiku. [1] | High |
| "Juxtaposition" is the Japanese technique. | A disputed English gloss of toriawase. [16] | Medium |

## 6. Haiku vs. related forms

| Form | What it is | Confidence |
|---|---|---|
| **Haiku** | Standalone short poem, nature/season linked to human condition, cut, usually kigo. [1][6] | High |
| **Senryu** | Same shape; focus on human nature, often ironic, satirical, or humorous; no kigo required. Named after Karai Senryū (1718 to 1790). The boundary with haiku is fuzzy; many English "haiku" without season reference are senryu. [1][18][19] | Medium-High |
| **Hokku** | Opening verse of renga/renku; now used mainly in that original sense. [1] | High |
| **Renga / renku** | Collaborative linked verse; seasons must progress; about half the stanzas carry no season. [2] | High |
| **Haibun** | Prose plus haiku, often travel journal (Bashō's *Oku no Hosomichi*, Issa's *Ora ga Haru*). [1] | High |
| **Haiga** | Painting combined with haiku; Buson was a master. [1] | High |
| **Tanka** | Older, longer related form (5-7-5-7-7 in Japanese). | High (general knowledge) |

## 8. Worked examples

### 8.1 Classical (Japanese originals are public domain; glosses are my own literal renderings)

**Bashō, frog**
```
furuike ya / kawazu tobikomu / mizu no oto
old pond ya / frog jumps-in / water's sound
```
- Kigo: *kawazu* (frog), spring. Kireji: *ya* after line 1. Mode: combination. [1][2]
- Craft point: frogs were conventionally praised for their song; Bashō chose the splash instead. [2]

**Bashō, crow**
```
kareeda ni / karasu no tomarikeri / aki no kure
on a withered branch / a crow has perched (keri) / autumn dusk
```
- Kigo: *aki no kure* (autumn dusk / end of autumn). Kireji: *keri* mid-poem. [12]

**Issa, Edo rain**
```
Edo no ame / nan goku nonda / hototogisu
Edo's rain / how many measures did you drink / cuckoo
```
- Kigo: *hototogisu* (cuckoo), summer. No kireji, but the first 5 *on* stand as a fragment against the remaining 12. 17 *on*, only 15 syllables. [1]

### 8.2 Original demonstrations (mine, written for this note)

**Combination (toriawase):**
```
first frost
the dog's breath lingers
at the gate
```
Two elements (season / breath) meet across the cut; the reader supplies the cold.

**Single object (ichibutsu jitate):**
```
sap bucket
each drip a little higher
in the tin
```
One scene, one flow, early-spring sugaring kigo.

**Senryu (human nature, and a boundary case):**
```
moving day
the same couch wedged
in every stairwell
```
"Moving day" could serve as a Quebec summer observance kigo, yet the poem's energy is human comedy. That is the haiku/senryu blur in practice.

**Bad draft to revision:**
```
The autumn leaves fall      →   empty swing
I feel so sad and lonely    →   the maple lets go
Nature is so sad            →   one more leaf
```
Removed: stated emotion, abstraction ("Nature"), padding to hit 5-7-5. Added: a concrete cause of the feeling, a cut, a human trace (the swing) paired with a seasonal image.

## 9. Procedure: writing a haiku

1. **Notice.** Collect a specific sensory detail: what you saw, heard, smelled, touched, tasted. Present tense.
2. **Anchor the season.** Choose one kigo, or deliberately none (then consider whether it is a senryu).
3. **Choose the mode.** Combination (two elements, one gap) or single object (one flowing image).
4. **Draft long, then cut.** Delete judging adjectives, "I feel", explanations, articles that add nothing.
5. **Place the cut.** Confirm exactly two parts. Test the gap: too close is redundant, too far is random.
6. **Lineate.** Short / long / short, roughly 10 to 14 syllables, one breath aloud.
7. **Strip ornament.** No title, no rhyme, no overt simile.
8. **Classify honestly.** Haiku or senryu? Label it.

## 10. Revision checklist

- [ ] Two parts, not one flat sentence and not three fragments?
- [ ] Any word that tells the reader what to feel? Cut it.
- [ ] Is the kigo doing work, and is there only one?
- [ ] Does the gap between parts create a spark, or does part two just restate part one?
- [ ] Are words concrete and plain (no Latinate abstractions)?
- [ ] Present tense?
- [ ] Would a stranger see the scene without your explanation?
- [ ] Does it survive being read aloud in one breath?
- [ ] Syllable count serving the poem rather than the other way round?

## 11. Agent instructions (when this note is used as a skill)

- Default to **modern English-language conventions** (sections 2 to 4). If the user explicitly asks for 5-7-5, comply, and mention once that English haiku practice rarely uses it.
- When writing: output the poem first; add a short annotation (kigo, cut, mode) only if asked or if teaching.
- When critiquing: work through the section 10 checklist; say which items fail and why; propose one revision, not five.
- Default kigo calibration to the **user's local climate** unless they ask for Japanese classical seasons.
- Never reproduce copyrighted modern haiku or modern translations verbatim; use public-domain Japanese originals with fresh literal glosses, or write original examples.
