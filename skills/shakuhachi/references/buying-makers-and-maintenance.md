## 6. Buying, prices and maintenance

### 6.0 A warning about the source base — the shakuhachi market is full of scraper shops

[V] A Yahoo! Japan search for 尺八 販売 地無し 価格 (fetched 2026-11-13) returned, inside the
first ten results, four listings on domains that are **not** musical-instrument retailers:
`wineandmorekenya.com` (offering a 一尺九寸 地無し延べ管 for ¥4,400), `basecampfm.com`,
`drift-sense.com`, and `dev.catchybeauty.com`. These carry Japanese product text scraped from
real auction listings at implausible prices (¥4,400–¥14,239 for instruments whose genuine
equivalents are ¥80,000+). **[V] that the domains and prices appeared in the search results;
[U] what they actually are** — I did not open them and will not treat them as price evidence.

The practical consequence for anyone writing a buyer's guide: **price data for shakuhachi must
come from named, identifiable shops**, and the prices below are therefore restricted to four
sources I opened myself. Scraped junk shops dominate general search results for this instrument.

**Observations that are themselves findings:**

1. **A handmade jinashi by a named maker costs ¥165,000–¥880,000 (≈US$1,065–5,680) at retail
   in Tokyo.** [V] That answers the question in the usual account directly, with a date and a source.
2. **Jinashi are long.** Every single one of the ten listed was **2尺2寸 or longer**; not one
   was the standard 1尺8寸. This matches Part A §4.6 (jinashi/hotchiku practice favours long
   instruments) and is here confirmed as a *market* fact, not only a repertoire claim. [V]
3. **Jinashi are not cheaper than jiari.** The commonly repeated English framing — jinashi as
   the "simple", "unlacquered", implicitly rougher instrument — is not reflected in price: the
   jinashi range *overlaps* the jiari range almost exactly. [V from the two tables]
4. **The shop explicitly warns that jinashi are a different kind of instrument.** [V] verbatim:
   「地無しは『独奏楽器』です。演奏の目的および吹き方が調律管とは異なります。」 — my
   translation: "A jinashi is a *solo instrument*. Its performance purpose and its blowing
   method differ from a tuned pipe (調律管)." Note the shop's own term for a jiari here is
   **調律管** "tuned pipe", which is a sharper contrast than the usual English gloss.
5. **地無し風** "jinashi-*style*" is a used category [V] — one instrument was sold under that
   label with the note 「地無し風尺八、上下管に分かれます。」 ("jinashi-style shakuhachi,
   separates into upper and lower pipes"), i.e. a two-piece instrument presented as
   jinashi-adjacent rather than as jinashi. Compare Part A §3.3 on 半地無し *han-jinashi*.

**Used instruments at Mejiro** [V] `.../products/pre-owned-bamboo-flutes`:

So **a used professional instrument from a reputable dealer runs ¥76,000–¥242,000** — roughly
**half** the new price for a comparable grade. [V] Note [V] the page carried **two different
prices for the same item number (5162455)** in two different page modules, which is a caution
about treating any single scraped price as authoritative.

[U] **Mejiro publishes no grading scale or inspection criteria for used instruments.** The only
assurance on the page is 【試聴音源あり】 ("audio sample available") on most listings and
「店内試奏について：ご購入前提の方限定です。」 ("in-store trial playing is limited to those
intending to purchase", by phone reservation). The absence of a published used-instrument
grading standard is worth stating plainly rather than inventing one.

### 6.4 Maintenance

#### 6.4.1 Cracking (割れ *ware*) is the dominant failure mode, and why

[V, COMMERCIAL — Tai Hei Shakuhachi FAQ-7, `shakuhachi.com/FAQ-7.html`, fetched 2026-11-13] The
maker's mechanical account, which is the most specific causal explanation I found anywhere:

1. **Bamboo is strong along the grain but the bonds *between* fibres are weak**, so it fails by
   longitudinal splitting rather than by breaking.
2. Its **columnar structure expands and contracts with temperature and humidity**.
3. **Shakuhachi are especially prone because madake's walls are thick** — and crucially,
   **the expansion of the inner and outer surfaces of the culm differs**. A wall thick enough to
   sustain a moisture/temperature *gradient* across it develops a stress differential; a thin-walled
   flute does not. [V as the maker's stated reason.]
4. The Q-Binding page restates it: "bamboo's inner and outer walls swell and contract at
   different rates, which can split the culm." [V]

**This is the key mechanism and it explains the rest of the maintenance regime.** Everything
below — humidity control, oiling, the bag, binding — attacks either the gradient or the hoop
stress it produces. [Mechanism [V]; the organising claim is mine.]

**The climate-shock figure, which is the one piece of hard experience data available** [V,
COMMERCIAL]: moving an instrument from Japan to the drier, centrally-heated North American
climate "can cause rapid cracking", and the maker describes **flutes that crack within a week or
two of being imported**. This is also given as the reason **shakuhachi are rarely stocked by
North American music stores at all**. [V as the maker's claim; **[U]** — no quantified failure
rate, no climate data, and he sells the solution.]

A Japanese maker gives a complementary failure estimate [V, COMMERCIAL]: 葛山幻海 Katsurayama
Genkai of 尺八修理工房幻海, `shakuhachi-genkai.com/tools/tools01.html`, fetched 2026-11-13, states
that with his storage method **about 80% of flutes will not crack**, and that the remainder are
simply unlucky. **So the baseline is roughly a 1-in-5 lifetime crack risk even with good care**,
per one maker's unquantified experience. [V that he says it; **[U]** as data.]

#### 6.4.3 Oiling — type, amount and frequency, from a maker

#### 6.4.4 After playing: 露通し / 露切り *tsuyu-tōshi* / *tsuyu-kiri*

The bore fills with condensation ("dew", 露) in use. Two sources, consistent [V]:

- 緑和堂, verbatim: 「演奏後はできるだけ速やかに、かつ慎重に露通しをかけます。」 — "After playing,
  run the *tsuyu-tōshi* through **as promptly and as carefully as possible**." Technique: **pull it
  straight down, not at an angle**, to protect the blowing edge; **repeat two or three times** until
  the bore is dry. [V]
- 葛山幻海: wipe all moisture from the bore after playing, **because it makes the lacquer peel**;
  the **mid-joint (中継ぎ) and the mouthpiece area are especially prone**. [V]

**So the swab protects the lacquer and the joint, and the risk of careless use is damage to the
utaguchi.** [V] A 露通し is included in the ¥27,500 Zen-On 悠 set [V, §6.3.1], which indicates it
is considered non-optional even on a plastic instrument.

#### 6.4.5 Storage — two incompatible philosophies, reported as such

**Where sources disagree, report the disagreement.** They do disagree, sharply.

**Philosophy A — the breathable box (緑和堂 / appraiser's view)** [V]:
「専用のケースに入れた上で、外気の影響を最小限に抑えてください。」 — keep it in a dedicated case
to minimise outside-air influence; use a **共箱 *tomobako*** (fitted wooden box), because
**paulownia (桐 *kiri*) has good humidity-regulating properties**; store **high and out of direct
sun** (e.g. a closet's upper shelf); avoid windows, cars and heaters.

**Philosophy B — the sealed bag "sauna" (葛山幻海 / maker's view)** [V]. His full procedure:

1. Wipe the bore dry after playing.
2. Oil the outside monthly (§6.4.3).
3. Put the flute in a **thin plastic umbrella bag** — explicitly the thin bags supermarkets provide
   for wet umbrellas, **because they seal well**; **double them** if worried; **thick bags are
   stiff and seal poorly**.
4. If dryness is a concern, **breathe lightly into the bag** so a faint mist forms inside, then
   squeeze out excess air.
5. **Twist the bag's mouth** closed so air cannot exchange. He describes the interior as a kind of
   **sauna**.
6. Tie it with a **rubber cord** — specifically the cord from Japanese confectionery packaging,
   **not a rubber band**, because rubber bands **degrade under UV and snap**.
7. Store out of direct sun and away from air conditioning, ideally inside a leather or cloth bag.
8. **Play it at least once every three months.**

A third voice sides with B [U, snippet only]: a Yahoo!知恵袋 answer (2012-07-10) advising that a
**new** shakuhachi be kept in a plastic bag **for about a year**, because it is most vulnerable to
temperature and humidity change when new.

**These are genuinely opposed.** Philosophy A wants a *buffered but breathing* enclosure (kiri
wood regulating humidity); Philosophy B wants a *sealed* enclosure at deliberately elevated
humidity. Both are explicitly aimed at preventing cracks. I cannot adjudicate: **[U]** no
controlled comparison exists, and the only outcome evidence is each author's own anecdote
(B claims no cracked flutes of his own, including on trips to Europe, Oceania and Asia; A offers
none). **The one thing both agree on is that the enemy is the *rate of change*, not the level.**

#### 6.4.6 籐巻き *tomaki* — rattan binding

**Terminology first, because the sources do not share a vocabulary.** [V] The Japanese sources use
**籐巻き** *tomaki* (e.g. GENKAI's product name 「二尺五寸管**化粧籐巻き**（in G）」 — "decorative
rattan-wrapped 2尺5寸 pipe"; Mejiro's repair line item 「**籐巻き**1箇所」 — "rattan binding, one
location"). [V] **Tai Hei's English pages never use the word *tomaki* at all** — they say
"binding", "standard binding", "inlaid binding", "traditional inlaid rattan". So the English and
Japanese literatures are describing the same thing under non-matching names, which is probably why
English accounts of it are thin.

**What it is and what it does** [V, COMMERCIAL — `shakuhachi.com/Q-Binding.html`, fetched
2026-11-13]: bands wrapped tightly around the tube at intervals, which **keep the bamboo from
expanding beyond its natural tolerance** — i.e. a hoop-stress restraint against the differential
inner/outer swelling of §6.4.1. The maker's position, quoted: *"only the time-tested method of
tightly binding the flute will insure against this possibility."*

**The grades, with real construction detail** [V] — this is the most specific description of
binding construction I found in any language:

| Grade | Construction | Material | Price (new instrument, whole flute) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Standard** | sits **on the surface** of the bamboo; **nine or ten** bands at "critical locations", evenly spaced | "high-tensile, small-diameter **woven** binding" — **fibre not named [U]** | **$175** (black) / $200 (lacquered dark red) |
| **Inlaid** | recessed in **shallow grooves cut into the bamboo**; greater tensile strength and stability; **flush**, so smoother under the fingers | same woven binding | $250 (black) / $300 (lacquered dark red) |
| **Traditional inlaid rattan** | a **channel is chiselled** into the bamboo, the binding wrapped **inside** it, the groove **glued and filled to within a fraction of a millimetre of the surface**, then **rattan inlaid over it** | **finely split rattan** | **$750**, and **Professional Root-End instruments only** |

Note the implication: **"standard binding" on Western-made instruments is not rattan** — it is an
unnamed high-tensile woven cord, with rattan reserved for the $750 traditional option, and the
"lacquered dark red" finish an *imitation* of urushi ("reddish brown to resemble Japanese
*urushi*" [V]). English writing that equates "bound shakuhachi" with "rattan-wrapped shakuhachi"
is therefore conflating three different constructions at three different prices. **This is a
correction.**

**Does binding affect tone?** [V] **The page does not address it**, and neither did any other
source I read. **[U] — genuinely open.** The stated objections to binding are purely
(a) aesthetic — the bands interrupt the look of the bamboo — and (b) tactile — raised standard
bands under the fingers, which is what the inlaid option solves. [V]

**Guarantee** [V, COMMERCIAL]: the maker states that **bound flutes are guaranteed for life
against becoming unplayable from splitting**, and that **none of the bound flutes made since 1987
has been returned unplayable**. The Q-Binding page says "three options" qualify for the guarantee
while listing five binding types — **an internal inconsistency on the page itself** [V], so the
exact scope of the guarantee is **[U]**.

**The cast-bore interaction, which is the clever part** [V]: even if the bamboo cracks, a
**precision-cast bore bonded to the inside of the bamboo keeps the instrument from leaking air**,
so it remains playable. So on a cast-bore instrument a crack is cosmetic/structural rather than
functional. That is a non-obvious consequence of the 1984 technology (§6.2.4) and it means
"cracked" means something different on a cast-bore instrument than on a traditional one.

**When a Japanese player chooses to bind** [U, snippet only]: Sound House staff blog
(`soundhouse.co.jp/contents/staff-blog/index?post=482`, 2018-08-14) states that rattan wrapping
tightens the bamboo to stop it cracking as it expands, and that **performers in dry climates often
have it wrapped from the start**.

### 7.1 Is 真竹 madake — *Phyllostachys bambusoides* — CITES-listed?

**Finding: almost certainly not, but I must present this as strong negative evidence rather than
as a read of the Appendices.** Part A §2.1 established the species identification; I use it here
without re-deriving it.

**The negative evidence, in order of strength:**

1. **[V] The USFWS-reviewed species list for musical instruments does not include bamboo, any
   *Phyllostachys*, or any grass.** Artifact: *"CITES Species Summary for Musical Instruments"*,
   League of American Orchestras, **dated March 19, 2024**, filename
   `Species-Summary-USFWS-Reviewed-3-19-24.pdf`, downloaded and text-extracted by me on

   "covers CITES-protected species **typically found in instruments**". **The complete list of

   (*Elephas maximus*), Brazilian rosewood (*Dalbergia nigra*), tortoiseshell (*Eretmochelys
   imbricata*), water monitor lizard skin (*Varanus salvator*), mother of pearl (stated as needing
   **no** CITES permit), and whalebone (e.g. *Balaena mysticetus*).** No bamboo, no *Phyllostachys*,
   no Poaceae. [V — I read the extracted text in full.]
2. **[V] The FIM CITES handbook does not mention bamboo once.** Artifact: *CITES Handbook*,
   **International Federation of Musicians (FIM)**, 2021 English edition,
   `fim-musicians.org/wp-content/uploads/fim-cites-handbook-2021-EN.pdf`, downloaded and

   [V]

   entirely** — see §7.2. A country that is both the range state and the exporter does not treat

4. **[U] Reasoning from the structure of CITES plant listings** (my inference, unsourced): CITES

### 7.2 Phytosanitary / plant-import controls — **this is the regime that actually bites**

CITES is the wrong worry for a bamboo flute. **Plant quarantine is the right one**, and it is a
completely separate legal regime with separate paperwork, separate authorities (agriculture
ministries, not wildlife authorities) and a different logic: it is about **pests and pathogens
riding on plant material**, not about species conservation.

#### 7.2.1 Japan, the export side — worked bamboo is explicitly exempt from import quarantine

**[V] The single most useful legal text I found in this entire section.** 農林水産省 植物防疫所
(MAFF Plant Protection Station), 「輸入植物検疫の対象とならない植物について」 ("On plants not
subject to import plant quarantine"), `maff.go.jp/pps/j/introduction/import/ifuyou/index.html`,
fetched 2026-11-13. Verbatim from the exempt list:

| Verbatim | My translation |
|---|---|
| 「製材、防腐木材、木工品、**竹工品**及び家具什器等の加工品」 | "sawn timber, preservative-treated timber, **wooden craft products**, **bamboo craft products** (竹工品), and processed goods such as furniture and household utensils" |
| 「**籐**及びコルク」 | "**rattan** and cork" |

**Two things follow and both matter.**

1. **A finished shakuhachi is a 竹工品 — a worked bamboo article — and is therefore in the class
   Japan's own plant-quarantine regime treats as not a regulated plant.** [V that 竹工品 is on the
   exempt list; **[U]** that a shakuhachi is legally classified as 竹工品 — no source I found says
   so explicitly, and classification is the inspector's call. This is **my** reading.]
2. **籐 rattan is separately exempt** — which matters because *tomaki* binding (§6.4.6) is rattan.
   A bound instrument does not thereby acquire a second regulated material. [V the exemption;
   the application is mine.]

**Direction caveat, important:** this is Japan's **import** exemption list. It tells you how Japan
classifies worked bamboo, which is strong evidence about the nature of the commodity, but it is
**not** an export authorisation and it does not bind any other country. [V] MAFF's own export page
(`maff.go.jp/pps/j/introduction/export/index.html`) states that export inspection exists to
confirm conformity **with the importing country's requirements** — i.e. Japan inspects on export to
satisfy the destination, so **the destination's rules govern**. **[U] I could not find a
shakuhachi- or bamboo-specific entry in MAFF's 輸出条件早見表 (export-conditions quick-reference
table).** That table exists at `zenshoku-kyo.or.jp/consultation/smarts/index/47/` [U, snippet only].

#### 7.2.3 The practical shape of the risk

My synthesis, flagged as such: the phytosanitary risk to a shakuhachi is **not** that the
instrument is contraband. It is that (a) it is **plant material of a species APHIS names on its
souvenir-pest list**, (b) **declaration is mandatory and protective**, (c) **the inspector has
final discretion and can confiscate**, and (d) the risk is **much higher for a raw, unworked,
untreated, bark-on, root-end culm or for bamboo-in-the-round stock** than for a finished,
cured, lacquered instrument. A maker importing **raw madake stock** is in a materially different
position from a player carrying a finished flute. [V for (a)–(c); (d) is my inference from the
pest logic and is **[U]**.]

### 7.3 Ivory *utaguchi* inlay — the one genuine CITES problem

Part A §1.2 established that the 歌口 blowing edge carries an inlay. **If that inlay is elephant
ivory, the instrument contains an Appendix I material and the legal position changes completely.**
Note the scale: an utaguchi inlay is a sliver — of the order of a gram or two, far under every
threshold below. **[U] I found no published weight for a shakuhachi utaguchi inlay**; §6.4.6's
alternative materials (water buffalo horn and plastic, per Mejiro's repair menu [V §6.4.7]) are the
modern substitutes.

#### 7.3.1 The CITES layer

[V, secondary quotation] From the **USFWS-reviewed** League of American Orchestras species summary,
2024-03-19, which I read in extracted text:

| Material | Rule, close paraphrase of the document |
|---|---|
| **African elephant ivory (*Loxodonta africana*)** | Items "**must have a CITES permit** which can be granted if the ivory content in the item was **removed from the wild prior to February 26, 1976**." The document adds: "(Note: the prior restriction on permits for items purchased after February 2014 is removed **effective July 6, 2016**.)" |
| **Asian elephant ivory (*Elephas maximus*)** | Permit grantable if **either**: the item is **over 100 years old**, the ivory **has not been repaired or modified on or after December 28, 1973**, and it was **imported through a CITES designated port or imported before September 22, 1982**; **or** the item is **under 100 years old**, **has not entered commerce since December 28, 1973**, and the ivory has not been repaired or modified on or after December 28, 1973 |

[V] **February 26, 1976 is the date the African elephant was first listed under CITES** — stated
explicitly in the USFWS Q&A I extracted (§7.3.2), which is why it is the pre-Convention date.
[V] **January 18, 1990 is the date the African elephant went into CITES Appendix I.** Both dates
come from the USFWS document, not from me.

[V] The EU guidance (§7.3.4) gives the Asian-elephant listing date as **1 July 1975** and the
African as **26 February 1976**, and says it uses the earlier (1975) date throughout "for the sake
of simplicity". So **1975, 1976 and 1990 are three different real dates** doing three different
jobs, and English accounts routinely blur them. **This is a correction.**

#### 7.3.2 The US national layer — the ESA 4(d) rule, and this one I have in primary text

**[V — and this is the strongest primary-grade artifact in §7.]** *"Revisions to the Endangered
Species Act (ESA) Special Rule for the African Elephant — Questions and Answers"*, **U.S. Fish and
Wildlife Service**, PDF at
`fws.gov/sites/default/files/documents/news-attached-files/questions-and-answers-african-elephant-4d-final-rule.pdf`,
fetched 2026-11-13 and text-extracted by me. (Direct `curl` was 403; the fetch tool retrieved it.)
This is **the agency's own explanation of its own rule** — not an advocacy paraphrase.

**The ESA antiques exemption, verbatim** — all four required:

> A. It is 100 years or older.
> B. It is composed in whole or in part of an ESA-listed species;
> C. It has not been repaired or modified with any such species after December 27, 1973; and
> D. It is being or was imported through an endangered species "antique port."

[V] And verbatim: "Under Director's Order No. 210, as a matter of enforcement discretion, items
imported prior to **September 22, 1982**, and items **created in the United States and never
imported** must comply with elements A, B, and C above, **but not element D**."

**The de minimis exception, verbatim** — "manufactured or handcrafted items must meet **all** of
the following criteria":

> (i) If the item is located within the United States, the ivory was imported into the United States prior to **January 18, 1990**, or was imported into the United States under a … CITES pre-Convention certificate with no limitation on its commercial use;
> (ii) If the item is located outside the United States, the ivory was removed from the wild prior to **February 26, 1976**;
> (iii) The ivory is a fixed or integral component or components of a larger manufactured or handcrafted item and is not in its current form the primary source of the value of the item, that is, **the ivory does not account for more than 50 percent of the value** of the item;
> (iv) **The ivory is not raw;**
> (v) The manufactured or handcrafted item is not made wholly or primarily of ivory, that is, the ivory component or components **do not account for more than 50 percent of the item by volume**;
> (vi) **The total weight of the ivory component or components is less than 200 grams;** and
> (vii) The item was **manufactured or handcrafted before the effective date of this rule**.

**And the document names musical instruments explicitly** [V, verbatim]: "The following types of
items may qualify for the de minimis exception: **many musical instruments** (including many

decorations, and **many bagpipes, bassoons and other wind instruments with ivory trim**)…" —
"However, to qualify for the de minimis exception, **all** of the above criteria must be met
(either A or B and C-G)."

**A shakuhachi with an ivory utaguchi inlay is squarely the kind of object this paragraph
describes: a wind instrument with ivory trim.** [My application of the verbatim text — the document
does not name the shakuhachi. [V] text, [U] application.]

**What the de minimis exception is NOT** [V]: it is "an exemption from prohibitions on **selling or
offering for sale in interstate and foreign commerce**". It is a *commerce* exception. It is not an
import/export authorisation — [V] "International trade in CITES specimens, including ESA antiques,

  and **does not change intrastate commerce, noncommercial movement within the US, or personal

**[U] US state-level bans: I could not verify any of it.** I know from the USFWS text that a state

#### 7.3.3 The UK layer — primary text, and a sharper threshold than the US

[V] **Ivory Act 2018, section 8 ("Pre-1975 musical instruments")**, read on
`legislation.gov.uk/ukpga/2018/30/section/8`, fetched 2026-11-13 — **primary legislation, read
directly.** Verbatim conditions:

> (1) An item that has ivory in it is exempt from the prohibition if—
> (a) the item is a **pre-1975 musical instrument**,
> (b) the volume of ivory in the instrument is **less than 20% of the total volume of the material of which the instrument is made**, and
> (c) the instrument is **registered under section 10**.

[V] Subsection (2): "musical instrument" **excludes** anything that, although capable of being
played as one, "was not made primarily for that purpose"; it **includes** "a bow, plectrum, or
other thing made for playing a musical instrument."

[V] Commencement: **1 February 2022** for specified purposes and **24 February 2022** for the
remainder, under **S.I. 2022/93**.

**So the UK threshold is 20% by volume plus registration; the US de minimis is 50% by volume AND
50% by value AND under 200 g.** These are **not the same test**, and a single instrument can pass
one and fail the other. **This is a correction to the common assumption that there is "a" de
minimis rule.**

#### 7.3.4 The EU layer — primary guidance, read directly

[V] **Commission Notice, *Revised Guidance Document — EU regime governing trade in ivory*** (2021),
PDF downloaded by me from
`environment.ec.europa.eu/document/download/6600ec4c-09cb-4423-91d1-194163179504_en` on
2026-11-13 and text-extracted. It interprets **Council Regulation (EC) No 338/97** and
**Commission Regulation (EC) No 865/2006**. Its five recommendations, verbatim:

> a) suspend the (re-)export and import of **raw ivory** items;
> b) suspend internal EU trade in **raw ivory**, except for **repairs of pre-1975 musical instruments** and pre-1947 antiques of high cultural, artistic or historical importance;
> c) suspend internal EU trade in **post-1947 worked specimens with the exception of pre-1975 musical instruments**;
> d) **restrict authorisation for import and (re-)export of worked ivory to pre-1975 musical instruments** and sales of pre-1947 antiques … to museums, and
> e) ensure a strict interpretation of EU law on the remaining authorised trade.

**The definition is the operative part and it is unusually demanding** [V, verbatim]:

> **pre-1975 musical instrument**: a musical instrument containing legally acquired pre-1975 ivory **which is, or has been until recently, used by a performing artist and is thus not merely a decorative object**. The CITES Convention has been applicable to African elephants since **26 February 1976**, and to Asian elephants since **1 July 1975**. For the sake of simplicity, this guidance document uses only the earlier date (1975) as a reference.

[V] And on evidence, verbatim: "the owner should **demonstrate that the item is, or has until
recently been, used as an instrument by a performing artist** and is thus not merely a decorative
object containing ivory."

**This is the trap for a shakuhachi.** An antique komusō flute with an ivory utaguchi, bought as a
collector's object and not played, **fails the EU definition** — not on date and not on quantity,
but because **it is not in use by a performing artist**. An EU player who actually performs on it
passes. **The EU test is about use; the US test is about weight and value; the UK test is about
volume and registration.** Three regimes, three incommensurable tests. [V all three texts; the
comparison is mine.]

[V] The guidance also notes that certificates for worked ivory are **not** issued except for
pre-1975 musical instruments and pre-1947 antiques, that a certificate "should describe the

past my extract). [V the fragment; [U] its completion.]

[V] **pre-1947 antique** is defined by reference to "worked specimens that were acquired more than

### 7.4 The Musical Instrument Certificate — what it is and, crucially, what it is not

[V, secondary quotation of a primary resolution] The MIC resolution itself
(`cites.org/.../E-Res-16-08-R17.pdf`) **refused me with HTTP 403**. I therefore rely on the music
sector's own explainer, which I downloaded and read in full: *"CITES Musical Instrument
Certificates"*, **April 2021**, signed by 19 organisations including the League of American
Orchestras, the American Federation of Musicians, FIM, NAMM, CAFIM, Pearle, Orchestras Canada,
Fender, Taylor, C.F. Martin, Paul Reed Smith and the International Association of Violin and Bow
Makers (EILA). Artifact: `americanorchestras.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Musical-Instrument-Certificate-ENG.pdf`,
fetched and text-extracted 2026-11-13.

**Origin, verbatim:** "At the 16th Conference of the Parties (CoP16), **Resolution Conf. 16.8** was
adopted on **Frequent Cross-Border Non-Commercial Movements of Musical Instruments**, creating a
multi-use Musical Instrument Certificate."

**The sentence that corrects the commonest misunderstanding, verbatim and emphasised in the
original:**

> **"The Musical Instrument Certificate (MIC) is not an exemption from the permit process. It is a consolidated permit."**

**What it covers** [V, verbatim/close paraphrase]:

| Aspect | Content |
|---|---|
| Function | Replaces the need for **multiple CITES export permits** when an instrument contains CITES material **and is not exempted** by an annotation or a personal-effects exemption |
| Process | Apply to **your own** CITES Management Authority for a **single** permit, which "must then be **credentialed by CITES enforcement officials at each designated port** through which an instrument travels" |
| Modes | hand-carried, checked luggage, **or cargo** for larger ensembles |
| Purpose codes | **P** for personal use by individuals; **Q** when a larger ensemble is treated as a **travelling exhibition** of multiple instruments on one permit |
| Validity | **three years** — and the sector is asking for ten [V] |
| Fee | **no fee in the UK** [V, gov.uk] |

**CoP17 changes, verbatim list** [V] — Resolution Conf. 16.8 (Rev. CoP17) was approved
**unanimously** and: "1. Clarifies that instruments **loaned** to musicians may qualify… 2.
Specifies that the non-commercial scope … includes using the permits when travelling for '**paid or
unpaid**' performances. 3. **Recommends** that CITES countries **not require permits** when
musicians are carrying certain instruments as **personal effects**."

**The sector's own verdict on how well it works, verbatim** [V]: "The process of issuing and
credentialing these permits is **extremely variable from country to country and is in urgent need
of harmonization**"; and "the required time and financial expense of obtaining and putting to use
the MIC has left no option but to **forgo travel with their best musical instruments**." Also:
"**Nearly all** orchestra musicians with CITES materials place their instruments in **cargo**
shipments to avoid multiple, unwieldy permits."

**Personal-effects exemption — the pitfall** [V] FIM handbook, verbatim: "The Personal Effects
Exemption applies to **private trips exclusively**. It is **not applicable if you are travelling
for professional purposes**." And: "**not all CITES parties recognise the Personal Effects
Exemption/Derogation**, which means a certificate may be required." [V] gov.uk's personal-effects
derogation list includes caviar, **rainsticks** and agarwood with quantity limits, plus clothing,
shoes, jewellery and antiques — **no musical-instrument line item**. [V]

**Does an MIC apply to a shakuhachi?** My answer, reasoned from the above and labelled as mine:

- **A plain bamboo shakuhachi with a non-ivory utaguchi: no MIC needed, because it contains no
  CITES-listed material.** [V] FIM's footnote states the test exactly: "If your instrument is
  exclusively made of species that are either exempt from permit requirements **or not protected by
  CITES**, you can travel safely without a permit. Be cautious though, as the presence of **one
  single protected species** may require a permit (MIC or TEC)."
- **A shakuhachi with an ivory utaguchi inlay: yes, this is exactly the MIC's case** — a small
  quantity of Appendix I material in an instrument crossing borders non-commercially. [V the
  framework; [U] the application.]
- **The relevant annotation exemptions do not help a shakuhachi**, because Annotation #15's
  "finished musical instruments" carve-out is attached to **Dalbergia and Guibourtia** (§7.5), and
  there is **no equivalent blanket carve-out for ivory**. [V] FIM: "Even if most of the instruments
  and bows contain a very small amount of ivory, **a CITES permit such as an MIC or TEC is
  required**."
- **TEC** = Travelling Exhibition Certificate, the MIC's parallel strand. [V] it is named in the FIM
  handbook as the alternative instrument. **[U]** I did not research its conditions.
- [V] FIM also records that some CITES authorities will issue a **"negative certificate"** —
  confirming an instrument contains **no** protected species, based on an expert statement. "Not
  all CITES authorities accept to issue such certificates, but some may accept to **validate expert
  certificates with a simple e-mail**." **For a shakuhachi this is the genuinely useful document**:
  a maker's or expert's written statement that the utaguchi is water buffalo horn or plastic, not
  ivory. [V the mechanism; the shakuhachi application is mine.]

### 7.5 Annotation #15, quoted verbatim from a secondary source — and why I include it

The briefing asked for the annotation text via an industry quotation. Here it is, and it is **also an
object lesson in what a shakuhachi does *not* get**.

[V, **secondary quotation of a primary instrument**] *"Musical Instruments and Appendix II
Annotation #15"*, music-industry statement, **April 2021**, 19 signatories as listed in §7.4;
`americanorchestras.org/wp-content/uploads/2021/05/Annotations-and-Musical-Instruments-ENG.pdf`,
downloaded and text-extracted by me 2026-11-13. It states that Annotation #15 applies to
***Dalbergia* spp. (except *Dalbergia nigra*), and *Guibourtia demeusei*, *Guibourtia
pellegriniana* and *Guibourtia tessmannii***, and reproduces it:

> All parts and derivatives, except:
> a) Leaves, flowers, pollen, fruits, and seeds;
> b) **Finished products to a maximum weight of wood of the listed species of up to 10 kg per shipment**;
> c) **Finished musical instruments, finished musical instrument parts, and finished musical instrument accessories**;
> d) Parts and derivatives of *Dalbergia cochinchinensis*, which are covered by **Annotation #4**;
> e) Parts and derivatives of *Dalbergia* spp. originating and exported from Mexico, which are covered by **Annotation #6**.

**The CITES interpretive definitions, verbatim** (these are the parts people get wrong):

> **Finished musical instruments** — A musical instrument (as referenced by the Harmonized System of the World Customs Organization, **Chapter 92**…) that is **ready to play or needs only the installation of parts to make it playable**. This term **includes antique instruments** (as defined by the Harmonized System codes **97.05 and 97.06**…).
> **Finished musical instrument accessories** — … separate from the musical instrument, and **specifically designed or shaped to be used explicitly in association with an instrument**, and that requires **no further modification** to be used.
> **Finished musical instrument parts** — … **ready to install** and specifically designed and shaped to be used explicitly in association with the instrument **to make it playable**.

**Provenance caveats the usual account asked me to state** [V]:

1. **This is a secondary quotation.** I did not read the CITES Appendices; cites.org gave me 403.
2. **The number 15 is CoP-dependent.** The document itself says revisions to Annotation #15 were
   "supported… at the **18th** Conference of the Parties… and have entered into effect", and that
   "The **original** Annotation #15 adopted at the 17th Conference of the Parties (CoP17) required
   substantial clarification… as agreed in **CITES Notification 2017/078**." So **#15 means
   different things pre- and post-CoP18**, and annotation numbers have been renumbered between CoPs.
   Treat any cited number as provisional.
3. The same document quotes **Annotation #10**, for *Caesalpinia echinata* / *Paubrasilia echinata*
   (pernambuco), as covering "**Logs, sawn wood, veneer sheets, including unfinished wood articles
   used for the fabrication of bows for stringed instruments**" — a *range-state-first* annotation,
   which is the model the sector argues for. [V]
4. It quotes **Resolution Conf. 11.21 (Rev. CoP17)**'s annotation principles: "1) controls should
   concentrate on those commodities that **first appear in international trade as exports from range
   States**; and 2) controls should include only those commodities that **dominate the trade and the
   demand for the wild resource**." [V]

**Why this matters for the shakuhachi, and it is a negative:** the "finished musical instrument"
exemption everyone cites is **an annotation on specific timber genera, not a general CITES
principle**. [V] FIM states the same for pernambuco, Madagascar ebony and *Cedrela*: each is
exempt "from permit requirements" **by its own annotation**. **There is no such annotation for
elephant ivory**, which is why an ivory-inlaid instrument still needs paperwork while a
rosewood-bodied guitar does not. **This is the single most common error in English writing about
instruments and CITES.**

**Scope discipline.** A separate researcher is covering the *history* of 一節切 and the gagaku

is Part A §1.3's: **which way is the blowing edge cut?** That single feature sorts this whole
family, and it is the feature English accounts most often get wrong.

### 8.3 The blowing edge is the dividing line — and it runs through this family, not around it

**This is the organological point of the whole section.** [V, ja.wikipedia 一節切 §構造, verbatim:]

> 歌口、すなわち吹き口は、管の上端の一部を**外側に**斜めに切り取った形をとる。この形は**古代尺八や
> 普化尺八と共通する**一方、**中国の洞簫や朝鮮の短簫とは異なる**。

My translation: "The *utaguchi*, i.e. the blowing mouth, takes the form of a portion of the upper
end of the tube cut away obliquely **towards the outside**. This form **is shared with the ancient
(gagaku) shakuhachi and the Fuke shakuhachi**, while it **differs from the Chinese *dòngxiāo* and
the Korean *tanso*.**"

**So the line is: Japanese shakuhachi lineage = outward cut; continental 簫 lineage = inward
cut.** [V] The Chinese Wikipedia article on 箫 confirms it from the other side and — crucially —
**gives the Chinese names for the three mouth types** [V, zh.wp 箫 §形制, verbatim]:

> U口／V口／唐口：吹口內削而切割成U字或V字則稱U口或V口。**唐口則是尺八的開口，外切。**

My translation: "U-mouth / V-mouth / **Táng-mouth**: when the blowing mouth is cut **inward**
(內削) into a U or V shape it is called U-mouth or V-mouth. **Táng-mouth is the shakuhachi's
opening: cut outward (外切).**"

**That is a genuinely valuable find.** Chinese organology has a dedicated term — **唐口 "Tang
mouth"** — for the outward-cut blowing edge, named after the Tang dynasty, i.e. acknowledging it
as the *archaic* form that Japan preserved and China abandoned. [V] ja.wp 尺八 states the
abandonment explicitly: 「その後中国では、歌口の傾斜が管の外側にあるタイプの縦笛は断絶し」 — "After
that, in China the type of end-blown flute with the bevel on the outside of the tube **died out**."
[V]

**Three terminological consequences, all of them corrections:**

1. **The shakuhachi's utaguchi is not sui generis** — it is the Tang-era blowing edge, retained in
   Japan, named 唐口 in Chinese. [V]
2. **"Shakuhachi = xiao" is wrong at the level of the mouth.** They are *inverse* bevels. [V]
3. **天吹 tempuku is NOT in the shakuhachi's blowing-edge lineage.** Despite looking like a tiny
   shakuhachi and sharing its 4+1 hole count, its mouth is **洞簫 type, cut on the inside**
   [V, GENKAI]. Every English description I have seen of the tempuku calls it a small shakuhachi
   or a shakuhachi relative. **On the one feature that actually sorts this family, it is on the
   other side of the line.** This is the single most surprising finding in §8.

### 8.4 一節切 *hitoyogiri* — organology only

[V] ja.wikipedia 一節切, plaintext via the API, fetched 2026-11-13. Footnoted there to 月渓恒子
(*国史大辞典*, 吉川弘文館; *日本大百科全書*, 小学館), 上参郷祐康 (*世界大百科事典*, 平凡社),
*日本国語大辞典*, and *デジタル大辞泉* — a properly referenced article.

- **Definition**: 「日本の尺八の一種で、リードを用いない竹製の縦笛」 — a *kind of* Japanese
  shakuhachi, a reedless bamboo end-blown flute; classified as **一節切尺八**. Also written
  一重切, 一簡切, 一節截; read either *hitoyogiri* or *hitoegiri*. [V]
- **The name is literal**: made from the **middle of a madake culm** containing **exactly one
  node**. [V]
- **Dimensions**: standard **1尺1寸1分 in 曲尺 ≈ 33.6 cm**, diameter **≈ 3 cm**. Note the unit:
  **曲尺 *kanejaku*** (the carpenter's shaku), not the 尺八's own reckoning — compare Part A §4.1.
  [V]
- **Holes**: 前面4孔、背面1孔の計5孔 — identical count and arrangement to the Fuke shakuhachi,
  **but 「孔間比は微妙に異なる」** (the inter-hole *ratios* differ subtly). [V] **This is a precise
  and useful distinction: same topology, different proportions.**
- **It is straighter and thinner** than the Fuke instrument, and **the end nearer the root becomes
  the blowing end** — 「根に近い方を歌口にする点でも普化尺八と異なる」, i.e. **the opposite
  orientation** from the Fuke shakuhachi, which puts the root at the *bottom* (Part A §1.7). [V]
  **This is a second structural inversion and it is not in the English literature I know of.**
- **A pre-17th-century family of five lengths named by their 筒音** (the all-holes-closed
  fundamental), [V] verbatim: **壱越切, 平調切, 双調切, 黄鐘切, 盤渉切** — Ichikotsu-giri,
  Hyōjō-giri, Sōjō-giri, Ōshiki-giri, Banshiki-giri, after the gagaku pitch names. **黄鐘切 became
  the standard.** [V] Compare Part A §4.7 on 正寸/正律: this is the *same naming logic* — length
  named by resulting pitch — applied two centuries earlier.
- **Performance limits, stated as the reason for its decline** [V]: 「基本奏法は普化尺八に近いが、
  複雑な技法には向かず、音量・音域も普化尺八に劣った」 — basic technique close to the Fuke
  shakuhachi, but **unsuited to complex technique, and inferior in both volume and range**. A
  second reason given: it was **unsuited to playing the 都節音階 (*miyako-bushi* scale)** that came
  to dominate Japanese music from the 17th century. [V] **That is an organological explanation for
  an extinction, and it is worth more than the usual narrative one.**
- **A 19th-century revival attempt**: 神谷潤亭, an Edo town physician, renamed it 「小竹」
  (*shōchiku*/"little bamboo") in the Bunka–Bunsei era and published 『糸竹古今集』 (1818); it
  failed, and transmission **ceased after the later 19th century**. [V]
- **Still made today** [V, §6.2.3]: Ôno Ranzan (France) and Dan Shinku (Japan) both list
  hitoyogiri; GENKAI prices them from **¥20,000** (§6.1.3). So the *instrument* is being built even
  though the *transmission* is broken. [V]
- [V] ja.wp 一節切 itself links tempuku: 「薩摩には、「天吹」（てんぷく）と呼ばれる小型の竹製縦笛が
  伝わっている。天吹は一節切と同じく竹製の縦笛であり、**形態上の近さから関連して言及されることが
  ある**」 — associated with the hitoyogiri "because of their **formal closeness**". Note the
  careful hedging: *mentioned in connection with*, not *descended from*. [V]
- **Named instrument**: 「乃可勢」 *Nokaze*, said to have passed through Nobunaga, Hideyoshi and
  Ieyasu. [V]

### 8.5 天吹 *tempuku* — the Satsuma instrument, and the one that breaks the pattern

**ja.wikipedia has no standalone 天吹 article** [V] — a request for 天吹 **redirects to 笛
("flute")**, where it gets exactly one line: 「天吹（てんぷく）- 薩摩地方に伝わる一節切に似た布袋竹製
の縦笛。」 — "an end-blown flute of *hōteichiku* bamboo, resembling the hitoyogiri, transmitted in
the Satsuma region." [V] So I went to specialist sources.

**Physical** (sources as in §8.2; the GENKAI page `shakuhachi-genkai.com/analysis/analysis10-tenpuku.html`
and `tenpuku.net/what/` both fetched 2026-11-13; Kagoshima Prefecture, Asahi and kotobank
**[U, snippet only]**):

| Feature | Value |
|---|---|
| Length | **≈ 30 cm** across all sources; GENKAI puts the **mean at ≈ 30.6 cm**, i.e. **one *shaku*** |
| Outer Ø | **≈ 22 mm** (天吹同好会); **1.8–2.5 cm** (GENKAI) |
| Bamboo | **布袋竹 hōteichiku**, locally **コサン竹**, explicitly **not** the swollen-node 亀甲竹 form; the part **nearest the root**; or near-root madake |
| Nodes | **3** |
| Holes | **4 front + 1 back = 5**, small; the **back hole is placed as on the 一節切, higher than on a shakuhachi**; the **end-node hole is also small**, deliberately, for 「音締め」 (tone-tightening) |
| Blowing edge | **洞簫 type: the inside is cut away** — opposite to the shakuhachi |
| Tuning | **not fixed**; varies by maker. The reference pitch is taken **from the third hole, not the open-tube note**, because the small end hole makes the lowest note hard to sound |
| Example scale | roughly **la–do–re–mi (or fa)–so–la (or si♭)** |

**[V] Lengths are cut to the player's own hand.** kotobank [U, snippet]: dimensions are set from
the player's finger widths, so each instrument differs. tenpuku.net [V]: 「長さや外径は一定ではなく、
音の高さも一本ずつ異なるため**合奏には向きません**」 — lengths, diameters and pitches all differ
instrument to instrument, **so it is unsuited to ensemble playing**, and **players as a rule cut
and make their own**. **This is a categorically different instrument-concept from the shakuhachi**,
whose entire 正寸/正律 apparatus (Part A §4.7) exists to make instruments interchangeable. [V both
sides; the contrast is mine.]

**Origin, as a live question rather than a settled fact** [V] — GENKAI's page lays out the
candidates: a modified **一節切**; a modified Chinese/Taiwanese/Korean end-blown flute (**洞簫** or
short flute); and notes the three-node build may point at a **三節切**. The author's own view is
that it came from something like the **洞簫 or 古代尺八** rather than from the shakuhachi, and he
**doubts** a Chinese or Korean origin on cultural and tuning grounds. [V as his argued position,
not as consensus.] **Given the inward-cut mouth, the 洞簫/古代尺八 hypothesis has a real
organological foundation** — my observation.

**Transmission history, from the preservation society** [V] `tenpuku.net/what/`:

| Date | Event |
|---|---|
| **1603** | Entry **"Tenpucu"** appears in the ***日葡辞書*** (the Jesuit *Vocabulario da Lingoa de Iapam*, Nippo Jisho) |

| Edo period | Played by **samurai-class youths within 郷中教育** (the Satsuma youth-group education system), **alongside the 薩摩琵琶** |

extinction and whose canon is seven pieces. **The 1603 Nippo Jisho attestation is the hard
terminus ante quem** and is the best-dated fact about any instrument in this section. [V]

### 8.6 The gagaku shakuhachi — organological facts only

[V] ja.wikipedia 尺八 §古代尺八（雅楽尺八）, fetched 2026-11-13:

| Fact | Verbatim / figure |
|---|---|
| Transmission to Japan | as a **gagaku** instrument, **late 7th to early 8th century** |
| Surviving instruments | 「東大寺の正倉院には**六孔三節**の尺八が**八管**収められている」 — **eight pipes** of the **six-hole, three-node** type held in the **Shōsōin at Tōdai-ji** |
| Mean length | **40 cm** |
| Finger-hole diameter | **2 cm** |
| Holes | **6**: **5 front, 1 back** (前5、後1) |
| Blowing edge | **outward** bevel — 「歌口の傾斜が管の外側にある」 |
| Extinction | ceased as a gagaku instrument; **gone by the mid-Heian period, the 10th century** |
| Playing method | **unknown** — 「古台尺八に関連する楽譜の記録がないため、演奏方法や音階などの詳細は不明である」: no notation survives, so technique and scale are **unknown** |
| Chinese origin story | the 『旧唐書』 "呂才伝" account: **呂才 Lü Cai (600–665)** devised it in the **貞観 era (627–649)**; the 一尺八寸 length was the one sounding **黄鐘**, China's standard pitch (= Japanese 壱越 = Western **D**) |
| Terminology | 宋 陳暘's 『楽書』 treats **尺八管 as an alternative name for 簫管** |

**The two most useful organological facts, both corrections:**

1. **Six holes, not five** (5+1 vs the modern 4+1). [V] The modern five-hole layout is **not**
   inherited from the gagaku instrument.
2. **40 cm, not 54.5 cm.** [V] Despite the name *shakuhachi*, the surviving Shōsōin instruments
   average **40 cm**. The *shaku* of the Tang/Nara period was shorter than the Edo *shaku* —
   ja.wp 尺八 warns about exactly this: 「時代と国によって「尺」の単位の実際の長さが違うので注意が
   必要」, "the actual length of the unit *shaku* differs by era and country, so caution is
   needed", and gives **≈54.5 cm** specifically for the **Edo-period Fuke shakuhachi**. [V] So
   **"shakuhachi" has meant two different physical lengths**, and the name is a *pitch* convention
   wearing a *length* costume. Compare Part A §4.2–4.3.
3. [V] ja.wp 簫 adds: 「日本の正倉院に伝わる**雅楽尺八も、洞簫の一種である**」 — the Shōsōin gagaku
   shakuhachi "is **also a kind of 洞簫**". Note the apparent tension with §8.3: ja.wp 尺八 says the
   gagaku instrument has the **outward** bevel, while ja.wp 簫 classes it under 洞簫 (whose defining
   bevel is **inward**). **I report this as a disagreement between two Wikipedia articles rather
   than resolving it** [V both statements]. The most likely reconciliation is that 洞簫 is being
   used in the *broad* sense ("single-pipe end-blown reedless bamboo flute") in one place and in
   the *narrow* Chinese-instrument sense in the other — but **[U]**, that is my guess.

### 8.7 The Chinese 尺八 / *chǐbā*, and 洞簫 / *xiāo* for contrast

[V] **zh.wikipedia 尺八**, plaintext via the API, fetched 2026-11-13 — short (948 chars) and
written from the Chinese side:

- Opening, verbatim: 「尺八原是**吴地**的一种木管乐器，原产自中国后传入日本。竹制，**外切口**，五孔
  （前四后一）…」 — "The shakuhachi was originally a woodwind instrument of the **Wu region**,
  produced in China and then transmitted to Japan. Bamboo, **outward-cut mouth**, five holes (four
  front, one back)…" [V] **Note the claim of a specifically 吴 (Wu, i.e. lower Yangtze) origin** —
  a regional attribution I did not see in any Japanese source. **[U] unsupported on that page.**
- It gives the length as **一尺八寸 ≈ 55 公分 (55 cm)** and distinguishes **雅乐尺八** (for court
  gagaku) from **普化尺八** ("what is meant by 尺八 today"). [V]
- Construction: 「由真竹的**根部**制成」 — made from the **root section of madake**; 「正面4个，背面
  1个指孔」. [V]
- [V] It describes the modern Chinese scene by listing external links to a **台灣尺八協會** (Taiwan
  Shakuhachi Association), 頻伽尺八館, and 映月尺八道館 — so **there is an organised contemporary
  Chinese-speaking shakuhachi world**, playing the *Japanese* instrument. **[U]** I did not open
  any of those sites and have no membership, size or date information.

[V] **ja.wikipedia 簫** (fetched 2026-11-13) is the single best organological source I found on the
continental side, and it is footnoted-encyclopedia in register:

- 簫 covers **two different instrument types**: the **single pipe** and the **panpipe** (排簫 /
  鳳簫). Before the Tang, 簫 alone meant the **panpipe**; the single pipe took the name later. [V]
  **English writing that treats 簫/xiao as simply "the Chinese vertical flute" is dropping half the
  word's referents.**
- **Single-pipe 洞簫 *dòngxiāo*, from the Song onward**: length **≈80 cm**, **5 front + 1 back**,
  mostly bamboo but also **jade and porcelain**; **2–4 出音孔 vent holes on the lower rear**. The
  mouth: 「歌口は、管壁の外側を削る尺八と異なり、管壁の**内側**を斜めにえぐっており、**えぐり方も
  小さい**」 — unlike the shakuhachi, which pares the **outside** of the tube wall, the 洞簫 gouges
  the **inside** obliquely, **and the gouge is smaller**. [V] The *shallower* cut is a second
  difference beyond direction, and it is a real acoustic variable (compare Part A §5.2 on the
  absence of a chimney).
- **Ancestry**: said to derive from the **羌笛** *qiāngdí*, a 3–4-hole reed-pipe (葦笛) of minority
  peoples in **Gansu/Sichuan**, called 篴 *teki* in the Han; **京房 Jīng Fáng added a back hole in
  the 1st century BC to make five**. [V] Qing court 雅楽 洞簫: **54 cm**, 5 front + 1 back. [V]
- **Three modern Chinese 洞簫 types with bore diameters** [V]: **鳳凰簫 Ø ≈2.2 cm, 6 holes**;
  **玉屏簫 Ø ≈1.05 cm, 6 holes**, made of pale-yellow bamboo with the nodes pared down, from
  southwest **Guizhou**; **琴簫 (雅簫) Ø ≈1.7–1.8 cm, 8 holes**, made of **紫竹** purple bamboo.
- **南曲 / 南管 (Fujian)**: a 洞簫 「尺八のように太い」 — **as thick as a shakuhachi** — length
  **≈56 cm**. [V] **This is the single closest continental relative of the shakuhachi by
  dimension**, and it is the instrument zh.wp calls **南簫**.
- [V] zh.wp 箫 on 南簫 specifically: it is the **粗短型** ("thick-and-short type"), 「為傳統古洞簫，
  或稱「南簫」」 — the *traditional old* 洞簫; **前五孔、後一孔**; **十目九節** (ten internodes, nine
  nodes); **吹口為V字形** (V-shaped mouth, hence inward-cut); made of **桂竹, 孟宗竹, 石竹**;
  「音色渾厚深沉」 (thick, deep timbre); and played with the arms raised 「鳳凰展翅之勢」, "in the
  posture of a phoenix spreading its wings". [V] **Note 孟宗竹 = *Phyllostachys edulis* (mōsōchiku),
  a different *Phyllostachys* from madake** — [V] the name; the species equation is **mine** and
  **[U]**.
- **細長型 "slender-and-long"** = **琴簫**, also called **北簫** (northern xiao), developed in
  northern China by **彭祉卿**, who published 〈新制雅箫圖說〉 in 《今虞琴刊》 and **invented the
  8-hole 雅箫/琴箫** for playing with the *qin*; this is the ancestor of the modern 8-hole xiao and
  is **tuned to 12-tone equal temperament** for ensemble use. [V] Also: traditional 洞簫 is
  **一體成形** (made in one piece), whereas the 琴簫 introduced a **metal tuning ferrule between
  the mouth and the first finger hole**, splitting the tube in two "like a clarinet or recorder" —
  and later a **three-section** version with a second ferrule below the first finger hole, made
  for **Cantonese-opera musicians in Hong Kong in the 1980s** for portability. [V]
  **Compare Part A §1.6 on the 中継ぎ: the shakuhachi and the xiao arrived at a mid-joint for
  different reasons** — tuning/portability in the Chinese case, and (per Part A) a different
  rationale in the Japanese. **A real comparative point.**
- **全音箫 vs 半音箫** [V]: a 6-hole xiao with **evenly spaced** holes is a 全音箫 ("whole-tone
  xiao"); a 半音箫 ("semitone xiao") **sets out 8 equal-tempered hole positions and then does not
  drill holes 2 and 6**, so the spacing *looks* uneven. **This is the same design problem as Part A
  §5.5's cross-fingering discussion, solved the opposite way.**

### 8.8 Korean 퉁소 *tungso* and 短簫 *tanso*

[V] **ko.wikipedia 퉁소**, fetched 2026-11-13:

- Classified as a **당악기 *tang'akki*** — a **Tang-derived** instrument, in the 죽부 (bamboo)
  category. [V] So Korea classifies it the same way Japan classifies the gagaku shakuhachi: as a
  Tang import.
- Origin story: **구중 *Gu Jung*, a musician under Han Emperor Wu**, is said to have improved a
  **강족 (Qiang people)** wind instrument. Records of use in Korea date from the **고려 Goryeo**
  period. [V] **Note this is the same 羌 ancestry ja.wp 簫 gives for the 洞簫** — the two
  encyclopedias agree independently. [V]
- Material **황죽 黃竹** (aged/yellow bamboo). Classical specification: length **1자 8치 2푼**,
  inner diameter **2푼**. Modern: **length 55 cm, inner Ø 2 cm** — explicitly 「단소보다 굵고
  크게」, thicker and larger than the 단소. [V]
- Construction: a **부서 *puseo*** is formed at the head for the breath; **one back hole about
  4 치 down from the head**, with **five front holes below it**. [V]
- **The 청공 *cheonggong* distinction is the important one** [V]: the 정악 (court/orthodox-music)
  퉁소 has **no 청공**; the folk 통소/퉁애 has a **청공 about 3 치 down from the head, covered with
  a reed membrane (갈대청)** to give a buzzing timbre. **A mirliton membrane** — ja.wp 簫 reports
  the same for Korea: 「清孔をつけてそこに竹紙を貼り、しびれた音色を出す」 (a 清孔 with bamboo
  paper applied, giving a "numbed"/buzzing timbre). [V both] **No shakuhachi has anything like
  this**, and it is the sharpest timbral divergence in this whole family.
- Current use: formerly in 제례악/연례악 (ritual and banquet music); now a **향악 solo instrument**
  and used in folk music as **퉁애**. [V]

[V] **ko.wikipedia 단소**:

- Classified **향악기** (native/*hyang* category), and said to be **a modification of the 퉁소**.
  [V] Not in the 악학궤범 (*Akhak kwebŏm*, 1493) or later sources, so **its origin is unknown**;
  one account (함화진, 『한국음악소사』) claims a Qing-dynasty introduction under **King Sunjo**
  but with **no firm basis**. Current view: a **late-Chosŏn modification of the 퉁소**. [V]
- **Length ≈ 43 cm** (varies with the bamboo); open at the bottom. **Holes: 4 front + 1 back, as
  on the 퉁소**, but **unlike the 퉁소 the lowest hole is rarely used**. [V]
- **Material: mainly bamboo, but plastic for educational use** — and ja.wp 簫 notes it is "popular
  enough to be taught in South Korean primary schools". [V both] **Compare §6.3: the plastic
  student instrument is a pan-East-Asian phenomenon, not a Japanese novelty.**
- **Range**: 협종 (G♭) to 중청중려 (A♭). Takes the **high register** in traditional music, with
  very elaborate ornamentation. [V] 단소 산조 was created by **전용선** and others, overcoming the
  narrow 요성 (vibrato) width imposed by the **small blowing aperture** [V] — **note that this is
  the exact inverse of the shakuhachi's situation**, where the wide, unloaded mouth gives *huge*
  pitch-bending range (Part A §5.3, §5.7). **The blowing-edge geometry has direct consequences for
  available ornamentation**, and the two traditions developed opposite ornamental vocabularies as a
  result. [V both premises; the causal link is my inference and **[U]**.]
- North Korea developed a **고음단소** (high-register tanso) with an extended range for its
  배합관현악 (blended orchestra). [V]

### 8.9 One more neighbour worth a line

[V] ja.wp 簫 §パンパイプ型: the **排簫 panpipe** branch has a documented archaeology the single-pipe
branch lacks — **oldest extant example c. 433 BC from the tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng (曾侯乙墓),
Suixian, Hubei**; Han-dynasty 『爾雅』 distinguishing a large 23-pipe 䇾 (pipe length 1尺4寸,

whose tubes show **traces of tuning by inserted packing**, and which was **wrongly repaired in the
Meiji period into two separate pieces and reunited in 2019 by dissolving the animal glue with an
enzyme**. [V] Included because it establishes that **the Shōsōin's end-blown 尺八 pipes sit beside

## 9. Corrections to the commonly repeated English account

Numbered as the usual account asked. Each states the correction, then where the evidence is.

1. **"A jinashi is the cheap, simple, unlacquered shakuhachi."** False as a market claim. At
   Mejiro on 2026-11-13, new jinashi ran **¥165,000–880,000** and new jiari **¥165,000–1,100,000**
   — overlapping ranges. A handmade jinashi by a named maker is a **premium** object. The shop's
   own framing is that a jinashi is 「独奏楽器」, a *solo instrument*, whose purpose and blowing
   method differ from a 調律管 ("tuned pipe"). (§6.1.1)

2. **"Jinashi come in the standard 1尺8寸 length."** Not in the market. **All ten** new jinashi
   listed at Mejiro were **2尺2寸 or longer**; none was 1尺8寸. (§6.1.1)

3. **"There is a student / intermediate / professional ladder you can walk up at a specialist
   shop."** There is not. Mejiro's cheapest *new* instrument of the standard length is ¥165,000,
   and its "for beginners" category contains **no instruments at all** — only books, DVDs and one
   adapter. The entry tiers live at general retailers, at endorsement-branded synthetics, and at
   Western makers' published grades. (§6.1.1, §6.1.2, §6.3.3)

4. **"The plastic shakuhachi is an unbranded curiosity."** The brand is **全音 Zen-On**, a
   sheet-music publisher; the model is **悠 "Yū" DK-01** at **¥27,500 incl. tax**, in **ABS**,
   endorsed by the player **神永大輔 Kaminaga Daisuke**; there is a **second** model, **玄 "Gen"
   DK-G01** at **¥24,200**, in **30% glass-fibre-reinforced resin**, sold specifically on the claim
   that **「割れやコンディションの変化も起こりにくく」** — cracking and condition change are less
   likely. The synthetic is marketed against the bamboo instrument's **dominant failure mode**, not
   merely on price. (§6.3.1, §6.3.2)

5. **"Shakuhachi makers are individual artisans."** Many are *multi-generational named family
   workshops* with the same social form as swordsmiths: **荒木古童 (6 generations, from the late
   Edo period), 遠藤 (4 generations, founded 1912), 北原精華堂 (3 generations)**, and a making
   lineage with its own school name, **直簫流 Jikishō-ryū**. There is also an **official 都山流
   list of 8 makers** with addresses, and the 銘 on a retail instrument is traceable to it.
   (§6.2.1, §6.2.2)

6. **"Western shakuhachi making is a handful of people."** The **European Shakuhachi Society lists
   31 makers** across France, Italy, Spain, the UK, the USA, Australia, New Zealand, Russia,
   Ukraine, Brazil, Colombia and Japan, working in madake, *Arundo donax*, black bamboo, French
   bamboo, Chinese bamboo, local Colombian bamboo and **PVC** — and **roughly 24 of the 31 make
   jinashi or hotchiku**, the inverse of the Japanese retail market's weighting. (§6.2.3)

7. **"Bound shakuhachi are rattan-bound."** Three different constructions get called "binding" at
   three prices: **standard** (an unnamed "high-tensile, small-diameter woven" cord lying on the
   surface, **nine or ten bands**, **$175**), **inlaid** (same cord recessed in shallow grooves,
   **$250**), and **traditional inlaid rattan** (a chiselled channel, filled to within a fraction
   of a millimetre, rattan laid over, **$750**, professional instruments only). The "dark red
   lacquered" option is an **imitation** of urushi. Also, **the Japanese term 籐巻き *tomaki* does
   not appear on the English maker pages at all** — the two literatures use non-matching names for
   the same thing. (§6.4.6)

8. **"A crack ruins the instrument."** On a **precision-cast-bore** instrument the cast bore is
   **bonded to the bamboo**, so a cracked tube **still does not leak** and the instrument stays
   playable; the maker guarantees bound flutes for life against becoming unplayable, and states
   none made since **1987** has come back unplayable. "Cracked" therefore means something
   materially different depending on how the bore was made — which is a buying-decision fact.
   (§6.4.6)

9. **"You can get a crack repaired at a good shop."** As of 2026-11-13, **Mejiro — the largest
   specialist retailer — does not accept crack repair or rattan binding at all** (「現在承って
   おりません」), accepts repairs **only** for instruments bought there, and refers cracks to
   another workshop. The only priced service is a loose mid-joint, **¥6,000–12,000 + tax**,
   1–2 weeks. Repair capacity for the dominant failure mode sits with individual workshops that
   publish no prices. (§6.4.7)

10. **"Store a shakuhachi in a case at stable humidity."** Two incompatible recommended practices
    exist and should be reported as such: a **breathing, buffered 共箱 of paulownia** (the
    appraiser's view), versus a **sealed thin plastic umbrella bag deliberately humidified by
    breathing into it, twisted shut and tied with a confectionery cord** (the maker's view, who
    calls the interior "a kind of sauna" and adds: play it at least once every three months). The
    only point both agree on is that **the enemy is the rate of change, not the level**. (§6.4.5)

11. **"Keep it at the right humidity" — there is finally a number, and it is weak.** The only
    numeric target I could find anywhere is **50–60% RH**, with **≥50%** given for spring/autumn
    and **≥50% maintained with a humidifier** in winter — and it comes from an **antiques dealer's
    marketing column** supervised by an **appraiser**, not from a maker, a school, a museum or a
    conservation body. Cite it as indicative, attribute it, and note that the risk is asymmetric:
    every source treats **drying** as the cracking risk and **damp** as a mould/lacquer risk. No
    source gives a target **temperature**; the only temperature figure in this research is **20 °C
    / 68 °F as a tuning reference**. (§6.4.2)

12. **"Oil the bamboo to feed it."** The maker's stated mechanism is a **vapour barrier**: a very
    thin film that **slows moisture loss**, applied **about once a month**, **camellia or olive
    oil** (walnut gives gloss but less protection), wiped off to leave almost nothing — because
    **too much makes it slippery and old oil goes sticky**. (§6.4.3)

13. **"Madake is *Phyllostachys bambusoides* and might be CITES-restricted."** The species name is
    right (Part A §2.1); the CITES worry is misplaced. The USFWS-reviewed music-industry species
    list (March 2024) names only ivory, *Dalbergia nigra*, tortoiseshell, water monitor lizard,
    mother of pearl and whalebone; the FIM handbook does not contain the word "bamboo" once; and
    Japan's own plant-quarantine law puts **竹工品 (worked bamboo articles)** on the list of things
    **not subject to plant quarantine**. **The right worry is phytosanitary, not CITES.** (§7.1,
    §7.2)

14. **"CITES exempts finished musical instruments."** It does **not**, as a general principle. The
    "finished musical instruments" carve-out is **text inside specific annotations attached to
    specific taxa** — Annotation #15 for *Dalbergia* spp. (except *D. nigra*) and three *Guibourtia*
    species, Annotation #4 for *D. cochinchinensis*, #6 for Mexican *Dalbergia*, #10 for pernambuco.
    **There is no such annotation for elephant ivory**, which is exactly why an ivory-inlaid
    instrument still needs paperwork while a rosewood guitar does not. (§7.5)

15. **"The Musical Instrument Certificate exempts you."** Verbatim from the music sector's own
    explainer: **"The Musical Instrument Certificate (MIC) is not an exemption from the permit
    process. It is a consolidated permit."** It must still be **credentialed at each designated
    port**, it is valid **three years**, and the sector's own assessment is that the process is
    "extremely variable from country to country and in urgent need of harmonization". (§7.4)

16. **"There is a de minimis ivory rule."** There are at least **three mutually incommensurable
    tests**: **US** — all seven criteria including **<200 g total ivory**, **≤50% of value**,
    **≤50% by volume**, not raw, made before the rule's effective date, plus a 1990 or 1976 date
    depending on where the item is; **UK** — **<20% ivory by volume** plus **registration** under
    s.10 of the Ivory Act 2018; **EU** — a **use test**: the owner must demonstrate the instrument
    "is, or has until recently been, **used by a performing artist** and is thus not merely a
    decorative object", on a **pre-1975** ivory basis. An instrument can pass one and fail another.
    (§7.3.2–7.3.4)

17. **"Pre-CITES ivory is 1947" / "1975" / "1976" — these are three different dates doing three
    different jobs.** **1 July 1975** = CITES applicable to Asian elephants (and the EU's chosen
    simplification date); **26 February 1976** = CITES applicable to African elephants, hence the
    US pre-Convention date for items outside the US; **18 January 1990** = African elephant moved
    to Appendix I, hence the US date for items inside the US; **3 March 1947** = the EU's "antique"
    cutoff, which the FIM handbook notes **is not harmonised across CITES parties**. (§7.3.1,
    §7.3.4)

18. **"If I buy an antique shakuhachi in Japan I can take it home."** Not if it has ivory and you
    lack documentation. Verbatim from 環境省: **「ワシントン条約の適用前に取得したことを証明する
    書類がない象牙製品は、外為法により国外持ち出しが禁止されています。」** — ivory products without
    documentary proof of pre-Convention acquisition are **prohibited from being taken out of
    Japan** under the Foreign Exchange and Foreign Trade Act. Japan also requires **business
    registration (特別国際種事業者)** for anyone dealing in ivory products **even free of charge**,
    with registration since **July 2018**, 5-year validity, management slips and 5-year records.
    (§7.3.5)

19. **"The utaguchi is always ivory on good instruments."** Mejiro's own repair menu offers
    utaguchi replacement in **water buffalo horn/plastic** or **silver-wrapped** — no ivory option
    at all. And the genuinely useful CITES document for a shakuhachi player is not a permit but a
    **"negative certificate"**: an expert statement that the instrument contains **no** protected
    species, which some CITES authorities will issue or will validate by e-mail. (§6.4.7, §7.4)

20. **"The shakuhachi's blowing edge is unique."** It is the **archaic Tang-era** geometry, retained
    in Japan after China abandoned it, and Chinese organology has a **name** for it: **唐口
    "Táng-mouth", 外切 (outward-cut)**, as against **U口 / V口**, which are **內削 (inward-cut)**.
    (§8.3)

21. **"Tempuku is a small shakuhachi."** On the feature that actually sorts this family it is on
    the other side of the line: its 歌口 is **洞簫 type, cut on the INSIDE**. It also differs in
    being cut to the individual player's hand, having a deliberately small end-node aperture, taking
    its reference pitch from the **third hole** rather than the open tube, having **no fixed
    scale**, and being **unsuited to ensemble playing**. (§8.2, §8.3, §8.5)

22. **"Hitoyogiri is an earlier shakuhachi."** Same hole count (4+1) and the same outward bevel,
    but: **1 node not 7**, **≈33.6 cm not 54.5**, **mid-culm not root-end**, **subtly different
    inter-hole ratios**, and — the striking one — **the end nearer the root becomes the blowing
    end**, the **opposite orientation** from the Fuke shakuhachi. Its decline is explained in
    organological terms: inferior volume and range, unsuited to complex technique, and unsuited to
    the **都節 *miyako-bushi*** scale that came to dominate Japanese music. (§8.4)

23. **"The gagaku shakuhachi is the early form of the shakuhachi."** Grove gives them **separate
    headwords** (Henry Johnson, 2015). The gagaku instrument has **six holes (5 front + 1 back)**,
    **not five**; the Shōsōin's **eight** surviving pipes are **六孔三節** and average **40 cm**,
    not 54.5; and its playing method and scale are **unknown** because no notation survives. The
    name *shakuhachi* has denoted two different physical lengths because the *shaku* itself changed
    length. (§8.1, §8.6)

24. **"Xiao is the Chinese shakuhachi."** 簫 denoted the **panpipe** before the Tang and still
    covers both panpipe (排簫/鳳簫) and single pipe. The single-pipe 洞簫 is ≈**80 cm** with
    **5+1 holes plus 2–4 rear vent holes**, and its mouth is cut on the **inside** and
    **shallower**. The closest continental object to a shakuhachi by dimension is the **Fujian
    南管 洞簫 / 南簫** at ≈**56 cm**, "as thick as a shakuhachi", **十目九節**, **V-mouth**. (§8.7)

25. **"Only the shakuhachi has the two-piece mid-joint."** The Chinese **琴簫** acquired a metal
    tuning ferrule between mouth and first hole, splitting it in two "like a clarinet or recorder",
    and a **three-section** version was made for **Hong Kong Cantonese-opera musicians in the
    1980s** for portability. Different motive, same solution. (§8.7)

26. **"Nothing in this family has a membrane."** The **folk Korean 퉁소/퉁애** has a **청공
    *cheonggong*** covered with a **reed membrane (갈대청)** — a mirliton — to give a buzzing
    timbre; the court 정악 퉁소 has none. Both the Korean and the Japanese encyclopedias record it.
    (§8.8)

27. **"The plastic student instrument is a Japanese novelty."** The Korean **단소** is made in
    plastic specifically for school use and is taught in South Korean primary schools. The
    synthetic student end-blown flute is a regional pattern. (§8.8, §6.3)

**On legality (§7)**

17. **I never read the CITES Appendices.** cites.org (403), the Species+ API (401), the Species+
    checklist endpoint (200 but empty for positive controls), EUR-Lex (TLS failure), eCFR
    (redirect), Kew (403) and METI (403) all refused. **Every CITES claim in §7 is secondary.**
18. **I cannot exclude that some bamboo somewhere is CITES-listed** — particularly in Appendix III,
    which a single Party populates unilaterally and which instrument-industry documents would never
    mention. The negative evidence is strong for *madake* and only suggestive for "bamboo".
19. **US entry conditions for a bamboo handicraft from Japan are unknown.** APHIS's ACIR database
    has the right commodity category ("Timber — Fully Processed Wooden Articles and Handicrafts",
    whose handicraft definition includes bamboo) but `acir.aphis.usda.gov` failed TLS verification.
    **This is the gap that would matter most to a real traveller.** APHIS's own published guidance
    is **China-specific**; nothing Japan-specific was found.
20. **Which part of 7 CFR 319, if any, covers worked bamboo articles** — unresolved (eCFR blocked).
21. **Whether ISPM 15 reaches bamboo** — unresolved. Relevant to anyone shipping an instrument in a
    bamboo or wooden crate.
22. **The EU phytosanitary import position for worked bamboo is entirely unverified** (EUR-Lex
    blocked). Do not assume it is permissive.
23. **Whether a finished shakuhachi is legally classified as 竹工品** under Japan's exemption list.
    The category is verbatim-verified; the classification of this object into it is **my** reading,

24. **US state-level ivory bans: no list, no statute, no threshold verified.** I know from USFWS's
    own text that a stricter state layer exists; I obtained nothing about its content.
25. **The weight of a shakuhachi's ivory utaguchi inlay** is unpublished, so the de minimis
    arithmetic cannot actually be done. By inspection it is grams, far under 200 g — but that is
    eyeballing, not evidence.
26. **The TEC (Travelling Exhibition Certificate)** is named in the FIM handbook as the MIC's
    parallel strand; I did not research its conditions.
27. **The EU pre-1975 musical-instrument certificate's restriction sentence** ("This certificate is
    only valid for a single sale to a…") is cut off in my extract and its completion is unknown.
28. **Whether an ATA carnet currently helps** — the sector was still *asking* for carnet-based

29. **The 20% ivory-by-volume threshold the EU advocacy sources mention** could not be located in
