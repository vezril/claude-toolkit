# The Shakuhachi: Origins, the Fuke Sect, Suizen, and the 1871 Rupture

Research notes for a reference skill. Not a skill file.

**Scope.** Origins and discontinuities; the Fuke sect and the forgery problem; *suizen*;
1871; the schools; the ensemble tradition; the 20th century; the naming question.
Deliberately **excluded** (other researchers hold these): the instrument as a physical
object, the Dokyoku school in depth, technique and notation as such. Notation appears
only where it distinguishes one school from another.

## 1. Origins and the instrument's several discontinuous lives

### 1.1 The headline: at least four instruments, three of them dead

`[V]` **Japanese reference works treat "shakuhachi" as a name applied to several
historically distinct instruments, not as one continuous object.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』
(S2, Kamisangō): 「日本の音楽史上に現れた広義の尺八には，古代尺八，天吹（てんぷく），一節切（ひとよぎり）尺八などもあるが，現行するのは普化（ふけ）尺八のみである」
— "the shakuhachi in the broad sense as it appears in Japanese music history includes
the ancient shakuhachi, the *tempuku*, and the *hitoyogiri* shakuhachi, but the only one
currently in use is the *fuke* shakuhachi" (my translation). Nipponica (S1, Tsukitani)
gives the same taxonomy plus 多孔尺八 (multi-hole shakuhachi) as a fifth, modern member.

| "Shakuhachi" | Period in use | Holes | Standard length | Fate |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 古代尺八 gagaku / Shōsōin shakuhachi | transmitted from Tang; in use Nara → c. 10th c. | 6 (5 front, 1 back), 3 nodes | 1 shaku 8 sun by Tang measure | **extinct**; dropped from gagaku c. 10th c. |
| 一節切 hitoyogiri | 14th/15th c. → died out 19th c. | 5 (4 front, 1 back), 1 node | 1尺1寸1分 ≈ 33.6 cm | **extinct**; brief 19th-c. revival attempt failed |
| 天吹 tempuku (Satsuma) | "flourished among Satsuma samurai in the later 16th c." (hedged) | `[U]` | `[U]` | marginal survival `[U]` |
| 普化尺八 fuke shakuhachi | late 16th c. → present | 5 (4 front, 1 back), 7 nodes | 1尺8寸 ≈ 54.5 cm | the modern instrument |
| 多孔尺八 multi-hole | Taishō/early Shōwa inventions | 6, 7, 9 | as fuke | 7-hole has a minority following |

(Table assembled from S1, S2, S3; see the subsections for each cell's source.)

### 1.2 The gagaku shakuhachi

`[V]` **Name and derivation.** Nipponica (S1): 「尺八の名称は、中国唐代の律尺による1尺8寸（約43.7センチメートル）に由来する」
— the name derives from 1 *shaku* 8 *sun* **by the Tang pitch-measure, c. 43.7 cm**.
Tsukitani attributes the name to the 唐書 account of the musician 呂才 (Ryosai / Lü Cai),
who in the mid-7th c. "improved and regularised the existing vertical flutes and made
twelve pipes of lengths matching the twelve *lü*", the longest of which — the pipe whose
fundamental was 黄鐘 *kōshō*, the Chinese standard pitch — was 1 shaku 8 sun;
「尺八の名称が生まれたらしい」, "the name shakuhachi seems to have arisen" from this.
Note the hedge **らしい**.

`[V]` **A real disagreement about which Tang history, and how long a Tang shaku was.**
- Nipponica (S1) cites 『唐書』 and gives the Tang measure as **≈ 43.7 cm**.
- 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S3) cites 〔新唐書‐呂才伝〕 (*Xin Tangshu*, "New Tang History") and
  computes 1尺8寸 from the Tang shaku as **≈ 56 cm**: 「黄鐘律(こうしょうりつ)の長さ九寸の二倍、一尺八寸（唐尺から換算すると約五六センチメートル）」.
- ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7) cites 『**旧**唐書』列伝「呂才伝」 (*Jiu Tangshu*, "Old Tang History"),
  sourced to 久保田敏子 (1990), 『よくわかる箏曲地歌の基礎知識』, p. 212, and dates Lü Cai's
  work to 貞観 627–649, giving his dates as 600–665.

So: **Old Tangshu vs New Tangshu, and 43.7 cm vs 56 cm — a ~28% discrepancy in the
derivation of the instrument's own name.** I could not resolve this; both figures are in
print in major Japanese reference works. `[U]` on which is right. (The underlying problem
is that the Tang had both a 律尺/小尺 and a 大尺; 43.7 cm implies ~24.3 cm/shaku and 56 cm
implies ~31 cm/shaku. That reconciliation is my inference, `[U]`.)

`[V]` **A Song-dynasty gloss.** ja.wikipedia (S7) quotes 陳暘『楽書』巻148 (Chen Yang,
*Yueshu*): 「簫管之制、六孔旁一孔。加竹膜焉。足黄鐘一均声。或謂之尺八管、或謂之竪籧、或謂之中管。尺八、其長数也。」
— i.e. the *xiaoguan* "is also called the *chiba* (shakuhachi) pipe … *chiba* is its
length-number." Useful: in Chinese the term is a **descriptive length-name for a class of
vertical pipe**, not a proper instrument name. The quotation is given with an
archive.org link to the text; I did not open the Yueshu itself, so `[V]` for
"ja.wikipedia quotes this", `[U]` for the quotation's accuracy against the original.

`[V]` **Arrival and use in Japan.** Nipponica (S1): transmitted as a gagaku (*tōgaku*)
instrument **from the later 7th century onward**; ja.wikipedia (S7) says 7th c. end to
early 8th c. Six finger-holes (5 front, 1 back), three nodes, longest pipe 1尺8寸
(黄鐘管) with others of various lengths. Materials in the surviving set include not only
bamboo but **jade (玉), stone (石) and ivory (牙), each carved to imitate bamboo's three
nodes** (S1).

`[V]` **What survives.** Nipponica (S1) is explicit and is the most precise source I found:
「日本に伝わったうち、正倉院に8管、法隆寺に1管（現在は東京国立博物館にある）、計9管が現存する」
— **8 pipes in the Shōsōin, 1 at Hōryū-ji (now in the Tokyo National Museum), nine
extant in total.** Britannica Japan (S3) and ja.wikipedia (S7) both say 8 in the Shōsōin;
only Nipponica adds the Hōryū-ji pipe. **The common English claim "the Shōsōin holds the
shakuhachi" should be "eight of them, plus a ninth elsewhere."**

`[V]` **Named Shōsōin pipes.** 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S3) quotes the 東大寺献物帳
(Tōdai-ji Kenmotsuchō, inventory of goods presented to the temple), dated 天平勝宝八年
(756) 六月二一日, as the first attestation of the word: 「玉尺八一管 尺八一管 樺纏尺八一管 刻彫尺八一管」
— "one jade shakuhachi, one shakuhachi, one birch-wrapped shakuhachi, one carved
shakuhachi." ja.wikipedia (S7) cites the Imperial Household Agency Shōsōin treasure
database for four of them by name — 彫石尺八 (id 0000010081), 玉尺八 (0000010053),
樺纏尺八 (0000010055), 刻彫尺八 (0000010056), accessed by the article's editors
2023-10-19. **I did not open the Kunaichō database pages myself** — `[V]` that
ja.wikipedia cites these four IDs, `[U]` on the database records themselves.
Note the inventory names map onto the surviving objects, which is why the 756 date
matters: it is a **dated documentary attestation, not an inference from the objects**.

`[V]` **The extinction, and the textual proof of it.** Nipponica (S1): used in the gagaku
*kangen* ensemble 「10世紀ごろまで」, then **dropped from the instrumentation**; used
outside gagaku until about the end of the Heian period. 『世界大百科事典（旧版）』 (via the
古代尺八 kotobank page, fetched 2026-11-13) dates the end to 「平安時代中期(10世紀末ごろ)」.
ja.wikipedia (S7): 「平安時代中頃の10世紀には絶えた」.

The decisive piece of evidence is lexical. 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S3) cites 『今鏡』 (Ima
kagami, 1170), book 3: 「尺八といひて、吹き絶えたる笛、はじめてこのたび吹き出したると承りしこそ、いとめづらしき事なれ」
— "it is a most remarkable thing to hear that the flute called the shakuhachi, **whose
playing had died out**, has this time been sounded for the first time [again]" (my
translation). **A 12th-century Japanese source already describes the shakuhachi as an
instrument no longer played.** This is the single best citation for the first
discontinuity and it is rarely given in English accounts.

`[V]` **And why nothing musical survives.** Nipponica (S1): 「楽譜などの記録を残さなかったため、奏法や音階など音楽についてはまったくわからない」
— no notation or comparable record survives, so **the playing technique and scales are
completely unknown**. ja.wikipedia (S7) agrees. Any English text that describes how the
gagaku shakuhachi *sounded* or was *played* is speculating.

`[V]` **Measurements of the surviving pipes.** ja.wikipedia (S7), citing kotobank:
average length 40 cm, finger-hole diameter 2 cm, 6 holes (5 front, 1 back).
Britannica Japan (S3): 管長 40 cm前後, 指孔径 2cm余, 指孔6, 8 pipes in the Shōsōin.
Note that 40 cm agrees with neither 43.7 nor 56 — the objects are shorter than either
reconstruction of "1 shaku 8 sun". `[U]`: nobody I read addresses this directly.

`[V]` **China's side of the discontinuity.** ja.wikipedia (S7), citing Tsukitani in
『日本の伝統芸能講座』 (2008) p. 384: in China the type of vertical flute with the blowing
edge bevelled on the **outside** of the tube 「断絶し」 — died out. The modern Chinese
*xiao* is bevelled inward. So the gap is not only Japanese.

### 1.3 Hitoyogiri 一節切 — a different instrument with its own repertoire and its own death

This is the part flattened hardest in English. The Heibonsha entry (S4, by Kamisangō
Yūkō) is detailed enough to reconstruct a whole independent musical culture.

`[V]` **The object.** 「正しくは一節切尺八といい，一簡切，一節截などとも書く」 — properly
*hitoyogiri shakuhachi*, also written 一簡切 / 一節截. Made from the **middle** of a madake
culm, thinner and shorter than the fuke shakuhachi, with **exactly one node, about a
third of the way down**. 5 finger-holes (4 front, 1 back) — the same count and layout as
the fuke shakuhachi, 「孔間比は微妙に異なる」 (the inter-hole ratios differ subtly). Smaller
volume, narrower range (S4). Nipponica (S4) adds: standard length 1尺1寸1分 ≈ 33.6 cm;
the node sits **between the 4th and the 5th (back) hole**; and — the orientation point —
「根に近いほうを上にする」, the end **nearer the root is up**, the opposite of the fuke
shakuhachi. ja.wikipedia (S7) adds outer diameter ~3 cm.

`[V]` **Origin: legend only.** Kamisangō (S4) is blunt: 「起源については，15世紀ころにロアン（朗庵などと書かれる）なる外国人僧侶が伝来したという伝説があるのみで，よくわからないが，遅くとも14世紀ないし15世紀には行われはじめたものらしい」
— "as for the origin, there is **only a legend** that a foreign monk named Roan (written
朗庵 etc.) transmitted it around the 15th century; it is not well understood, but it
**seems** to have begun to be played at the latest in the 14th or 15th century." Note the
double hedge 「伝説があるのみで」 + 「ものらしい」.

ja.wikipedia (S7) gives a *different* name for the same legend: 「一説によると、一節切は室町時代に中国から日本に渡った禅僧・蘆安がもたらしたもの」
— the Zen monk **蘆安** (Roan, different characters), sourced to 久保田敏子 (1990) p. 213.
『デジタル大辞泉』 (S4) states it flatly with no hedge at all: 「室町中期に中国から伝来」.
『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S4) hedges: 「室町中期ごろに、南中国から伝来したといわれる」 —
"**is said to have been** transmitted from South China around the mid-Muromachi period."
Nipponica (S4): 「伝来は室町時代中期と伝えられ」 — "is handed down as".
**Four Japanese reference works, four different levels of commitment to the same claim,
and two spellings of the monk's name.** Worth reporting as-is.

`[V]` **First attestations.** 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S3) gives 『申楽談儀』 (Sarugaku dangi,
1430) as the first attestation of 尺八 in the hitoyogiri sense: 「内にての音曲には、坐断し、右に扇を持て、左にはしゃく八を番(つが)ゑられしが」.
Britannica Japan (S4) notes 「当初は単に尺八と称した例も多い」 — **early on it was often
called simply "shakuhachi"**, which is why pre-17th-century occurrences of the word
cannot be assumed to mean the modern instrument.

`[V]` **The 16th-century popular song that dates its popularity.** Kamisangō (S4):
「16世紀ころに一般化していたことは，〈尺八のひとよきりこそね（音）もよけれ，君とひとよ（一夜）はね（寝）も足らぬ〉などという小歌によっても知られる」
— the *kouta* punning on 一節 / 一夜 ("one node" / "one night") and 音 / 寝 ("tone" /
"sleep"): roughly "the shakuhachi's *hitoyogiri* has the finest tone — but one night with
you is not enough sleep." 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S4) sources the song to 隆達節歌謡
(Ryūtatsu-bushi kayō, 1593–1611). This is both a dating datum and the earliest clear
evidence of the sexual punning on 尺八 (see §8).

`[V]` **Tuning and the standardisation of length.** Kamisangō (S4): originally made in
various lengths matched to the twelve *lü*, distinguished by fundamental as 壱越切
*ichikotsu-giri*, 平調切 *hyōjō-giri* etc.; **after the 宗左流 Sōsa-ryū lineage arose in the
later 16th century, 黄鐘切 *ōshiki-giri* (fundamental ≈ A4, length ≈ 34 cm) became the
standard**, 「その管長が当時多用された壱越調の律音階に適したためであろう」 — probably because
that length suited the then-prevalent *ritsu* scale on *ichikotsu*. Note 「であろう」 —
Kamisangō's own conjecture.

`[V]` **Lineages, masters and printed method books.** Kamisangō (S4):
- 宗左流 Sōsa-ryū, later 16th c. — the lineage that standardised the length.
- **大森宗勲 Ōmori Sōkun (1570–1625)**, out of Sōsa-ryū, "wrote many notation books" in
  the early 17th c. and spread the instrument as **宗勲流 Sōkun-ryū**. (ja.wikipedia (S7)
  gives the same dates, calling him 大森宗勲 and 「名手といわれた」.)
- Later 17th c.: his follower **指田一音 Sashida Ichion** and the **指田流 Sashida-ryū**.
- Three **printed self-instruction manuals**, which Kamisangō reads as evidence of a
  genuine amateur market: 中村宗三『糸竹初心集』(*Shichiku shoshinshū*, **1664**);
  村田宗清『洞簫曲』(*Dōshō no kyoku*, **1669**); anon. 『紙鳶』(*Ikanobori*, **1687**,
  collected in 『糸竹大全』).

**This matters for the whole argument.** There were named shakuhachi *ryū* with printed
methods and a lay market **in the 17th century — for the hitoyogiri, not for the fuke
shakuhachi.** The Edo "shakuhachi was a Zen monopoly" story applies to a different
instrument than the one that had the publishing boom.

`[V]` **Its repertoire and its genre categories.** Kamisangō (S4): up to the first half of
the 17th century the music was mainly **solo pieces, called 手 *te***; in the second half
of the century accompaniment of 流行歌 *hayariuta* and dance songs and **ensemble with
koto and shamisen** became common, these being called **乱曲 *ranky0ku***. So the
hitoyogiri had already done what the fuke shakuhachi is usually credited with doing only
after 1871: joined the koto/shamisen ensemble. (Confirmed independently by
『世界大百科事典（旧版）』「日本音楽」, quoted on the 一節切 kotobank page: the hitoyogiri was
「一般庶民の楽器として，箏や三味線と合奏されたり，流行歌や民謡を吹くこと」.)

`[V]` **Why it died — a musicological, not a political, reason.** Kamisangō (S4): from the
18th century it was hardly played; 「急速な衰微の原因は，音量，音域など性能の点で普化尺八に劣ったこと，なかんずく17世紀以後の日本音楽で支配的になった都節音階（半音を含む5音音階）の吹奏に適さなかったことに求められる」
— the causes were (a) inferior volume and range next to the fuke shakuhachi and
**(b) above all its unsuitability for the 都節 *miyako-bushi* scale** (the
semitone-containing pentatonic) that became dominant in Japanese music after the 17th
century. **This is the single best-argued causal claim I found anywhere in the sources and
it is essentially absent from English accounts.**

`[V]` **The failed revival, with a name and a date.** Kamisangō (S4): in the early 19th
century the Edo town physician **神谷潤亭 Kamiya Juntei (1783?–?)** took up the barely
surviving instrument, **renamed it 小竹 *kotake***, added his own new pieces to the
transmitted ones, and published 『糸竹古今集』 (*Shichiku kokonshū*, **1818**) and many other
notation books — but 「一部の好事家の関心を集めただけで，19世紀後半以後，一節切はまったく絶えてしまった」,
he drew only the interest of a few dilettantes, and **after the later 19th century the
hitoyogiri died out entirely.** Nipponica (S4) agrees: 「19世紀初期に小竹(こたけ)と改名して一時再興されたものの、同中期以後は伝承者を失った」
— lost its transmitters after the mid-19th century.

`[V]` **Date range disagreements.** Britannica Japan (S4): 「15世紀中頃から18世紀初頭までに行われた」
(mid-15th to early 18th c.) and 「19世紀中頃以後は絶滅した」. 『デジタル大辞泉』 (S4): flourished
Momoyama to early Edo, 「幕末に衰滅」 (died out at the end of the Edo period).
ja.wikipedia (S7) and Britannica on the 尺八 page (S3): peak **late 17th century**,
near-extinct in the 19th. **Range of stated peaks: 17th c. "second half" / "late" /
"end"; range of stated deaths: Bakumatsu / mid-19th c. / after the later 19th c.**

`[V]` **A notational connection to the modern schools.** Nipponica (S1) on notation:
**フホウ (fu-ho-u) notation was used for the hitoyogiri in the 17th century**, and among
fuke-shakuhachi lines survived in the 尾崎真龍 (1820–1888) lineage, 明暗真法流 and 宗悦流
(both now defunct), 「宗悦流の末流、竹保流にのみ現行される」 — surviving in practice today
**only in Chikuho-ryū**. Meanwhile ロツレ notation 「一節切（小竹）にも一時使われた」, was also
briefly used for the hitoyogiri/kotake, before becoming the Kinko-ryū system from the
18th century. So the two live notation families both pass through the hitoyogiri world.

### 1.4 Tempuku 天吹 of Satsuma

`[V]` **It is in the Japanese taxonomy and it is hedged.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 (S2)
lists 天吹（てんぷく） among the broad-sense shakuhachi. 『山川 日本史小辞典 改訂新版』 (S3)
is the most informative line I found: 「16世紀後半に薩摩武士の間で流行した**という**天吹(てんぷく)」
— "the *tempuku*, **said to have** become popular among Satsuma samurai in the later 16th
century." Note the bare 「という」: the standard Japanese historical dictionary will not
assert even the popularity, let alone an origin.

`[V]` **Nipponica lists it as a cross-reference target** (S1's 参照項目 includes 天吹), so
『日本大百科全書』 has a dedicated 天吹 entry. **I could not open it:** kotobank's search
interface is JavaScript-rendered and returned no hrefs to `curl`, and the numeric
kotobank IDs I guessed resolved to unrelated articles (a dermatology entry and a

### 1.5 The fuke shakuhachi — and the honest Japanese position on its origin

`[V]` **The single most important sentence in these notes.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 (S2,
Kamisangō): 「普化尺八の起源や伝来はまったく不明で，古代尺八との関係もわからない。ただ14世紀ごろに5孔の尺八の祖型が存在したことは確かで，それがのちに一節切尺八，普化尺八などに分化したものらしい。」

My translation: "**The origin and transmission of the fuke shakuhachi are completely
unknown, and its relationship to the ancient shakuhachi is also unknown.** It is certain
only that a prototype five-holed shakuhachi existed around the 14th century, and it
**seems** that this later differentiated into the hitoyogiri shakuhachi, the fuke
shakuhachi, and so on."

Three things follow, and they are the spine of section 2:
1. The modern instrument's descent from the Nara import is **not established** — the
   Japanese standard encyclopedia says 「まったく不明」, *completely unknown*.
2. The common ancestor is a **14th-century five-hole prototype**, which is also the
   hitoyogiri's ancestor. The hitoyogiri is therefore a **parallel, not a predecessor**.
3. The hole count changed: 6 (gagaku) → 5 (the 14th-c. prototype and everything after).
   The 6→5 transition is unexplained in every source I read. `[U]`

`[V]` **When the thing is first actually documented.** Kamisangō (S2):
「普化宗と普化尺八の存在が確認できるのは17世紀からである」 — "**the existence of the Fuke sect
and of the fuke shakuhachi can be confirmed only from the 17th century.**"

`[V]` **Earliest lexical attestation of 普化尺八-as-instrument.** 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S3)
gives for sense ①(ハ) (today's ordinary shakuhachi): 「我いにしへ諸国執行(しゅぎょう)の心さしありて、尺八の僧と成つつ」,
from the *ukiyo-zōshi* 『近代艷隠者』 (1686). So the earliest dictionary citation for the
modern instrument's sense is **1686** — and it is a work of popular fiction about someone
*becoming* a "shakuhachi monk," i.e. the social role was already a recognisable type.

`[V]` **Dating of the object's appearance.** Britannica Japan (S3): 「16世紀末頃出現」
(appeared around the end of the 16th century). ja.wikipedia (S7) follows this:
「16世紀末の日本で開発され」. 『世界大百科事典』 (S2) will not give a date at all, only the
14th-century prototype and 17th-century documentation. **The "late 16th century" date is
Britannica's, and the most authoritative Japanese source declines to endorse it.**

`[V]` **What the name means and Tsukitani's preferred alternative.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』
「普化尺八」 (S5, Kamisangō): the term exists only to disambiguate; it comes from the
instrument's status as a 法器 (ritual implement) of the Fuke sect in the Edo period, when
only komusō were supposed to play it. Nipponica (S1, Tsukitani) adds a judgement:
「普化尺八の名が一般化しているが、歴史的には虚無僧尺八とよぶほうが適切である」 — "*fuke shakuhachi*
is the generalised name, but **historically it is more appropriate to call it the komusō
shakuhachi**." Tsukitani's reason is implicit but follows from §2: the *sect* is a thin
and late institution; the *mendicants* are the documented reality.

`[V]` **Second sense of the term.** 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 (S5): 普化尺八 also means
**the repertoire** — 「普化宗の虚無僧が吹く尺八曲のこと。元来は宗教的な曲であったが、のちには芸術的な曲も吹かれた。「古典本曲」ともいわれる。」
So "fuke shakuhachi" denotes both the instrument and the *koten honkyoku* corpus;
Nipponica (S1) notes the same double use. An English source using the term without
saying which sense is in play is ambiguous.

## 2. The Fuke sect, the komusō, and the forgery problem

### 2.1 Two things called "the history of the Fuke sect"

There are two accounts and they must be kept apart:

- **(A) The order's own founding narrative**, whose written vehicle is the
  『虚鐸伝記国字解』 *Kyotaku denki kokujikai* (1795) and which is still published today by
  Myōan-ji itself. Chain: Puhua/Fuke → Zhang Bo → (sixteen generations) → Zhang San →
  Shinchi Kakushin → Kyochiku/Yorichiku and the dream at Asama-yama → Tengai Myōfu and
  Myōan-ji → Kusunoki Masakatsu as the first komusō.
- **(B) The documented institutional history**, which begins in the **17th century**, is
  about privileges, travel papers, rōnin control and temple administration, and contains
  no verifiable link to China or to Kamakura.

Japanese reference works keep these apart and label (A) as legend. English accounts
routinely run them together.

### 2.2 The *Kyotaku denki kokujikai* — the decisive citation

`[V]` **The full Britannica Japan entry** (『ブリタニカ国際大百科事典 小項目事典』,
「虚鐸伝記国字解」, via kotobank.jp/word/虚鐸伝記国字解-53423, fetched 2026-11-13):

> 尺八の文献。山本守秀著。安永8 (1779) 年起稿，寛政7 (95) 年京都にて刊行。「虚鐸」とは尺八の異称。『虚鐸伝記』なる漢籍に国文の注解を施した体裁であるが，**原典は存在しない**。普化宗の起源・歴史，虚無僧の起源・系譜，普化尺八曲の由来などを記す。**大部分は伝説的で史実とは認めがたい。**

My translation: "A shakuhachi text. By **Yamamoto Morihide**. Begun **An'ei 8 (1779)**,
published in **Kyoto in Kansei 7 (1795)**. *Kyotaku* is an alternative name for the
shakuhachi. It takes the form of a **vernacular Japanese commentary on a Chinese-language
text called the *Kyotaku denki* — but that original text does not exist.** It records the
origin and history of the Fuke sect, the origins and genealogy of the komusō, the
derivation of the fuke shakuhachi pieces, and so on. **The greater part is legendary and
cannot be accepted as historical fact.**"

**This is the forgery adjudication, in a mainstream Japanese encyclopedia, in two
clauses.** Note what it says precisely: the text presents itself as a *kokujikai* — a
vernacular gloss on a classical Chinese original — and **the original it glosses does not
exist**. It is not merely unreliable; its evidentiary frame is fictitious.

| 虚鐸傳記國字解 3巻 | 山本守秀 解註 | 升屋庄兵衞 | 1795 |

`[U]→[V]-ish` **The history of the forgery argument.** A Yahoo! Japan search snippet
(fetched 2026-11-13) from a note.com essay series (「明暗寺系創始文献『虚鐸伝記』を読み解く」
by コムジョ, an amateur researcher) states: 「『虚鐸伝記』の偽書の可能性は栗原広太が「尺八史孝」[sic, 考] 大正7年（1918）の中で指摘し、断定したのは中塚竹禅「琴古流尺八史観」昭和54年（1979）」
— the *possibility* that the Kyotaku denki is a forgery was **raised by Kurihara Kōta in
『尺八史考』 (1918)**, and was **asserted as settled by Nakatsuka Chikuzen in
『琴古流尺八史観』 (1979)**.

I could not open that essay, and the attribution of the argument is therefore `[U]`.
But **both books are real and I verified them**: NDL Search (fetched 2026-11-13) returns
栗原広太 (**1877–1955**), 『尺八史考』, 筑摩書房 **1918** and 竹友社 1918 and 1975; and
中塚竹禅 (**1887–**), 『琴古流尺八史観』, 日本音楽社 **1979**. ja.wikipedia's 尺八
bibliography (S7) gives the 1979 Nihon Ongakusha edition with
`|gyear=1932年` — i.e. **the 1979 book is a reprint of a 1932 original**, which would put
Nakatsuka's verdict in **1932, not 1979**. `[U]` on which year Nakatsuka's argument
actually dates from; the 1932 original date is the more likely.

**If this attribution holds, the forgery case is over a century old (1918) and was
considered settled within Japanese shakuhachi scholarship by the 1930s.** That is the
chronology to check against Sanford 1977 (below), who in English is often treated as
where the question was decided.

`[V]` **The companion forgery: the 慶長掟書.** The sect's other foundational document is
the 『慶長掟書』 / 「慶長之掟書」 of 慶長19 (**1614**), the bakufu grant said to have
established komusō privileges. **The Japanese sources split on it:**

- ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8) rejects it: 「『慶長掟書』（けいちょうじょうしょ）には「武者修行の宗門と心得て全国を自由に往来することが徳川家康により許された」との記述があるが，**原本は徳川幕府や普化宗本山である一月寺、鈴法寺にも存在しないため、偽書ではないかと疑問視されている**」
  — the *original does not exist* in the Tokugawa archives, nor at Ichigatsu-ji, nor at
  Reihō-ji, the sect's own head temples, **so it is questioned as a forgery**.
  Note the same argumentative move as for the Kyotaku denki: *the original is missing.*
- ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8) hedges differently: 「1614年（慶長19）に江戸幕府より**与えられたとされる**「慶長之掟書」により…」 — "said to have been granted".
- 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 「普化宗」 (by **竹貫元勝 Takenuki Genshō**, a Zen historian;
  via kotobank.jp/word/普化宗-124229, fetched 2026-11-13) **treats it as real and reads it
  politically**: 「1614年普化宗の僧徒である虚無僧は，勇士浪人の一時の隠れ家であるとなし，虚無僧取立ては武士に限るとするなどの掟書を出し，武門の正道を失うことなく，武者修行の宗門であると規定していて，**幕府の浪人取締策として普化宗を公認するという側面をもった**」
  — the *jōsho* declared komusō status a temporary refuge for brave rōnin, restricted
  recruitment to samurai, and defined the order as one of "warrior training"; **it had the
  aspect of the bakufu licensing the Fuke sect as a rōnin-control measure.**
- Myōan-ji's own site quotes the clause it relied on: 「普化禅宗門の儀は武家の隠れ家にして不入守護の宗門也」
  — "the Fuke Zen order is a refuge for warrior families and an order under protection
  from entry [by officials]" — and says the temple used it 「をたてに、治外法権的な立場に立って」,
  **as a shield to occupy a quasi-extraterritorial position**
  (myouan-doushukai.org, fetched 2026-11-13).

**Report this as an unresolved disagreement, not a settled forgery.** The Heibonsha
encyclopedia's Zen historian analyses the 1614 document's *policy function* without
disputing its existence; Japanese Wikipedia reports a missing-original argument against
it. I found no source that reconciles them. `[U]`

### 2.3 The legend, as the sources actually give it — and where they disagree

`[V]` **Nipponica** (by **末木文美士 Sueki Fumihiko**, a major historian of Japanese
Buddhism; kotobank 普化宗 page, fetched 2026-11-13) tells it most fully:

> 普化は馬祖道一(ばそどういつ)の門人盤山宝積(ばんざんほうしゃく)より法を受けたが、狂態を装い、つねに鐸(たく)を人の耳辺に振っていた。張伯(ちょうはく)という者が弟子になろうとしたが許されず、鐸の音をまねて竹管を吹いたところから、竹管吹奏の風がおこった。

My translation: Fuke received the dharma from Panshan Baoji, a disciple of Mazu Daoyi;
**he feigned madness and would constantly shake a hand-bell (鐸 *taku*) beside people's
ears.** A man named **Zhang Bo (張伯)** sought to become his disciple and was refused, and
**imitated the bell's sound on a bamboo pipe** — from which the practice of blowing bamboo
pipes arose.

> 日本では、心地覚心(しんちかくしん)が1249年（建長1）に入宋(にっそう)して張参(ちょうさん)より竹管吹奏の奥義(おうぎ)を受け、帰国後、紀州（和歌山県）由良(ゆら)に興国寺を建てて広めた。

Kakushin entered Song China in **1249** and received the *ōgi* (inmost secrets) of pipe-
blowing from **Zhang San (張参)**, then on returning built Kōkoku-ji at Yura in Kii.

**Important clarification that resolves an apparent contradiction.** There are **two
different Zhangs**: **張伯 Zhang Bo**, the Tang-era would-be disciple who invented the
*kyotaku*, and **張参 Zhang San**, the Song-era master from whom Kakushin learned. The
chain runs Zhang Bo → (16 generations) → Zhang San. ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8) calls
Zhang San 「中国普化宗16代目張参」, the 16th-generation head; Myōan-ji's own site says
「張伯十六世の孫、張参」, "Zhang San, descendant in the 16th generation from Zhang Bo."
**English accounts that give only one Zhang, or that call Kakushin's teacher "Zhang Bo,"
have collapsed the chain.** ja.wikipedia's own 尺八 article (S7) makes exactly this slip:
it names only 張伯 and omits Zhang San.

| Source | The four 居士 |

| ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8) | 「宝伏」ら4人 |
| **Myōan-ji official site** | **国作, 理正, 宗恕, 法普** |

Only 理正 is common to both lists. 宝伏 vs 法普 and 国佐 vs 国作 and 僧恕 vs 宗恕 look like

`[V]` **Kakushin's identity and dates.** He is 心地覚心 = **無本覚心 Muhon Kakushin** =
**法灯国師 Hottō Kokushi**. 『世界大百科事典（旧版）』「虚無僧」 (quoted on the kotobank 尺八
page) names him 「無本覚心（むほんかくしん）(法灯国師（ほつとうこくし）)」.
Dates in the sources:
- Entered Song: **1249** — agreed by Nipponica 「1249年（建長1）」, Britannica Japan
  「建長1 (1249) 年」, Heibonsha 「1249年入宋した」, Myōan-ji 「建長元年の春」 (spring of 1249).
- Returned: **1254 (建長6)** — Heibonsha, ja.wikipedia 虚無僧, ja.wikipedia 尺八.
- 『デジタル大辞泉』 refuses a year and gives a range: 「建長年間（1249～1256）に伝来」.
- 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 adds a further step: returned 建長6 (1254), then in
  **正嘉2 (1258)** converted 西方寺 into a Zen temple named 興国寺 and spread the teaching.

**`[V]` A dating error to flag:** ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8) writes 「[[1249年]]（建長6年）」.
**This is wrong on its face** — 建長6 is 1254, not 1249. The article then correctly says he
returned in 1254. An internal inconsistency in a much-copied article.

`[V]` **Kakushin's temple affiliation is also disputed.** 『百科事典マイペディア』 (kotobank
普化宗 page) calls him 「東福寺の覚心」, Kakushin **of Tōfuku-ji**; every other source
associates him with Kōkoku-ji at Yura and with Kennin-ji/Rinzai lineage generally.

dharma-grandson, founded Ichigatsu-ji at Kogane in Shimōsa under the patronage of:
- **北条経時 Hōjō Tsunetoki** — Heibonsha (竹貫), Nipponica (末木), Britannica Japan,
  ja.wikipedia 普化宗.
- **北条時頼 Hōjō Tokiyori** — 『百科事典マイペディア』.

Four sources to one, but note that Tsunetoki (r. 1242–46) and Tokiyori (r. 1246–56) are
consecutive regents, so the slip is easy and the dating is loose either way. `[U]`

`[V]` **The dream at Asama-yama, from the sect's own account.** Myōan-ji's official site
(myouan-doushukai.org, fetched 2026-11-13) gives the episode the user asked about:
Kakushin transmitted the piece 「嘘鈴」 [*sic*; normally 虚鈴 *Kyorei*] to his dharma-brother
**寄竹了円 Yorichiku Ryōen** (later **虚竹禅師 Kyochiku Zenji**); Yorichiku then
「伊勢の朝熊山の…に夜通し参籠して夢中に感得する処あり「虚空」「[霧海篪]」の二曲を作曲し、明暗開宗根元としての「三虚霊」を完成して」
— kept an **all-night vigil at Asama-yama in Ise**, received a revelation **in a dream**,
and composed **two pieces, 《虚空》 *Kokū* and 《霧海篪》 *Mukaiji*** [the second title was
truncated in the page markup; the standard third member of the set is 霧海篪, so `[U]` on
the exact character string as rendered on the page], thereby **completing the 「三虚霊」
*san-kyorei*, the three *kyorei*, as the root of the Myōan school's founding.**
He was then called 「今普化」, "the present-day Fuke."

Two further details from the same page, both striking:
- He built a hermitage at **Uji in Yamashiro** and named it **「朗庵」 Rōan**, spending his
  days in 「吹禅三昧」; died **1298 (永仁6), 7th month 28th day**, three months before his
  master Hottō Kokushi. His grave is called **普化塚** or **亀塚**.
  **「朗庵」 is the same name as the legendary transmitter of the *hitoyogiri*** (§1.3,
  where 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 gives 「ロアン（朗庵などと書かれる）なる外国人僧侶」).
  **I have not seen anyone connect these. It may be coincidence, a shared toponym, or
  a sign that the two legends draw on one stock of names.** `[U]` — flagging it as a
  genuinely open question.
- His dharma-heir **天外明普 Tengai Myōfu** built a temple in Kyoto named
  **虚霊山明暗寺**, installed Kyochiku as its founder, proclaimed the Fuke sect, made
  pipe-blowing the liturgy, and 「**読経を為さず**一枝の竹管を以って市中を行化托鉢して」
  — **recited no sutras**, begging through the city with a single bamboo pipe.

`[V]` **Where 「明暗」 comes from.** Myōan-ji's own page quotes Fuke's gatha, which the
monk is said to have chanted while shaking the bell:
「**明頭**来也**明頭**打、**暗頭**来也**暗頭**打／四方八面来也旋風打、虚空来也連架打」
— "If it comes in the light, I strike in the light; if it comes in the dark, I strike in
the dark…" **The temple name 明暗 Myōan is taken from 明頭/暗頭 in this verse.**
This also explains the 明暗 inscription on komusō alms-boxes (see §2.5). `[U]` on the
gatha's source text — it is in the *Linji lu* / Fuke's hagiography, which I did not open.

`[V]` **Kusunoki Masakatsu and the origin of the word *komusō*.** Myōan-ji's site:
「後世の虚無僧の起源は**楠木正勝**公が南朝衰微後この宗門に入り「**虚無**」と号し、常に天蓋を冠り、諸国を遊行し、戸々に尺八を吹いて、南朝復興の機を計ったので、世人は「虚無者」と呼びその風儀が始まりだ**と云い伝えられています**」
— Kusunoki Masakatsu (grandson of Kusunoki Masashige) is said to have entered the order
after the Southern Court's decline, taken the name **虚無 Komu**, worn the *tengai* hood
always, wandered the provinces playing shakuhachi door to door **in order to plot the
Southern Court's restoration**, and been called 「虚無者」 — whence the practice. Note
「と云い伝えられています」: **the temple itself labels this as transmitted lore.**
ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7) gives the same figure with the date 「1400年ごろ」 and the flat
verdict 「検証された史実ではない」 — **not verified historical fact**. ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」
calls it 「伝説がある」.

**Note what this does: it supplies a politically useful origin.** The komusō's defining
features — the face-concealing hood, the right to wander, the door-to-door playing — are
all explained in the legend as **covert resistance work for a defeated imperial cause**.
That is exactly the story an Edo order of armed, hooded, freely travelling ex-samurai
would find convenient.

`[V]` **The word's actual etymology is different and more mundane.** 『国語大辞典』
(新装版, 小学館, 1988), cited by ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8): 「古くは、「こもそう（薦僧）」ということが多く、もと坐臥用のこもを腰に巻いていたところからという」
— the older form was *komosō* 薦僧, **from the 薦 *komo* (rush mat) for sitting and
sleeping that they wound round the waist.** 『世界大百科事典（旧版）』「虚無僧」 lists the
alias set: 菰僧, 薦僧, 梵論字, 梵字, 暮露, 普化僧. So **虚無僧 ("monk of emptiness") is a
re-etymologising respelling of 薦僧 ("mat monk")** — and the Kusunoki legend is a further
re-etymologising of that. This is a clean, citable example of the whole pattern.

### 2.4 The documented institutional history

`[V]` **The sect is late.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 「普化宗」 (竹貫): 「中世には薦僧（こもそう），暮露（ぼろ）などと称され，普化宗の徒は漸増したとみられるが，**普化宗を公称し，一宗として展開をみるのは近世に入ってからである**」
— in the medieval period they were called *komosō*, *boro* etc. and their numbers
**seem (とみられる)** to have gradually grown, "**but it is only in the early modern period
that it publicly called itself the Fuke sect and developed as a sect.**" ja.wikipedia
「普化宗」 (S8) says the same in almost the same words.
Compare §1.5: 「普化宗と普化尺八の存在が確認できるのは17世紀からである」.

`[V]` **The medieval mendicants, pre-dating the sect.** 『世界大百科事典（旧版）』「虚無僧」:
- 吉田兼好『徒然草』 (Yoshida Kenkō, *Tsurezuregusa*, c. 1330) has 〈ぼろぼろ〉 and
  〈ぼろんじ〉, 「我執深く闘争を事にする卑徒としている」 — treating them as **low fellows,
  deep in self-attachment, who make a business of fighting**. (A 14th-century source
  already connects these mendicants with violence — the "shakuhachi as weapon" theme is
  not an Edo invention.)
- 『三十二番職人歌合』 「尺八を吹いて門戸にたち托鉢することをもっぱらの業としたとする」 —
  their sole occupation was standing at gates blowing the shakuhachi and begging.

**So: shakuhachi-playing mendicants are documented from the 14th century; the Fuke *sect*
is documented from the 17th.** Roughly three centuries separate the practice from the
institution. This is the central structural fact of section 2.

`[V]` **The temples and the administrative structure** (Heibonsha 「普化宗」, 竹貫):
- **一月寺 Ichigatsu-ji**, 金龍山梅林院, at **小金 Kogane, Shimōsa** (now Kogane, Matsudo
  city, Chiba), head temple of the **金先派 Kinsen-ha** (also written 靳全派).
- **鈴法寺 Reihō-ji**, 廓嶺山虚空院, founded at **武蔵藤袴** (Fujibakama, now Satte city,
  Saitama) by 括総了大居士, head temple of the **括総派 Kassō-ha** (also 活総派 / 火下派).
  **In 1614 (慶長19) it was relocated to 青梅 Ōme by 吉野織部** and became a training hall.
  — **This resolves an apparent contradiction**: ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8) locates
  Reihō-ji at Fujibakama/Satte; ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7), 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』 and
  『デジタル大辞泉』 locate it at **武蔵青梅** Ōme. Both are right — **Fujibakama is where it
  was founded, Ōme is where it moved in 1614.**
- **明暗寺 Myōan-ji**, 虚霊山, in Kyoto, head temple of the **寄竹派 Yoritake-ha**
  (派祖 天外明普).
- **普化宗六派 the six branches of the Fuke sect**, per Heibonsha: 金先派 (Ichigatsu-ji),
  括総派 (Reihō-ji), 寄竹派 (Myōan-ji, Kyoto), **小笹派/司祖派** (理光寺, 上野太田),
  **梅地派/梅士派** (慈高寺, 高崎), **小菊派/夏漂派** (心月寺, 常陸下妻).
- **Governance:** Ichigatsu-ji and Reihō-ji were designated **触頭 *furegashira***,
  supervising the branch temples of the Kantō region; **Myōan-ji and others handled
  Kansai and westward.** Myōan-ji's own site specifies that from the **延宝 era (1673–81)**
  it became the 興国寺末虚無僧宗門本寺, the head temple governing komusō **west of Kansai**.
- ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8): the two Kantō head temples were 触頭 over
  **「普化宗末寺120あまり」** — some **120+ branch temples**.
- **Myōan-ji's own figure is much larger:** 「江戸初期には**十八派、百四十ヶ寺**に激増し」 —
  by the early Edo period it had swollen to **18 branches and 140 temples.**
  **Disagreement to report: 6 branches / 120 temples (Heibonsha, ja.wp) vs 18 branches /
  140 temples (Myōan-ji's own site).** ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 also notes
  「普化宗諸流には16派があると伝えられている。**しかし，諸説がある。**」 and prints **two
  different 16-branch name lists** that overlap only partially. So the branch count
  itself is contested: **6, 16 (two versions), or 18.** `[U]` on which is right.

`[V]` **The *honsoku* / travel-document system.** Heibonsha (竹貫): 「宗徒となるときは**入宗証文**を提示し，宗門に帰依する趣旨をいい，弟子となることを誓約するなどし，その後住持から**尺八や天蓋（てんがい）が手渡された**」
— on becoming an adherent one presented a **入宗証文 *nyūshū shōmon*** (certificate of
entry), stated one's intent to take refuge in the order and swore to become a disciple,
after which **the abbot handed over a shakuhachi and a *tengai* hood**.
**Note: the shakuhachi was institutionally issued, as a licence token.**

`[U]` **I could not source the specific term 本則 *honsoku*** for the travel pass in any
of the Japanese reference works I read. The documents I *did* find named are:
入宗証文 (entry certificate, Heibonsha) and **吹簫行化証 *suishō gyōke shō*** (the Meiji-era
itinerancy seal issued by the 明暗教会 from 1890, per Myōan-ji's own site). The Myōan-ji
page also mentions a 「吹禅行化誓願文 *suizen gyōke seigan-mon*」 — a slip handed to donors
in return for alms, stating the purpose of the itinerancy (seen in a Yahoo! Japan snippet
of myouan-doushukai.org; `[U]` on its date and wording). **If a reference skill is to use
the word *honsoku*, it needs a source I did not find.**

`[V]` **Governance of the temples.** Heibonsha: the **本寺住持職** (head-temple abbacy) was
filled by **election in a council** of the head and branch temples' disciples; branch-
temple abbots were chosen by that temple's disciples in consultation, subject to the head
temple's approval. **The abbot was tonsured; those in other ranks, such as 看主 *kanshu*,
wore hair** (住持は剃髪し，他の看主などの位階にある者は有髪であった).
This matches ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8): the komusō were 「「僧」と称していながら剃髪しない半僧半俗の存在」
— **half-monk, half-lay, calling themselves monks but not tonsured.**

### 2.5 The rōnin question, the privileges, and the weapon

`[V]` **Entry restricted to samurai, and the criminal immunity.** 『精選版 日本国語大辞典』
「普化宗」: 「**武士以外の入宗を許さず**、江戸時代には、**罪を犯した武士がこの宗にはいれば罪をのがれることができた**」
— entry by non-samurai was not permitted; in the Edo period **a samurai who had committed
a crime could escape punishment by entering this sect.** ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8) says
the same: 「罪を犯した武士が普化宗の僧となれば、刑をまぬがれ保護された」.
『旺文社日本史事典 三訂版』: the restriction made it 「**復讐や仕官の目的の浪人や，無頼の徒の隠れ場**」
— a hiding place for rōnin bent on revenge or on getting office, and for ruffians.

`[V]` **And the predictable consequence.** ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8): 「江戸時代中期以降には、**遊蕩無頼の徒が虚無僧姿になって横行**するようになり、幕府は虚無僧を規制するようになった」
— from the mid-Edo period dissolute ruffians went about **in komusō costume**, and the
bakufu began to regulate komusō. Heibonsha (竹貫): 「しだいに復讐や仕官を目的とする者や無頼の徒がふえたため，その悪行を禁令によって厳しく取り締まっている」.
Nipponica (末木): 「やがて浮浪の徒が多く尺八を吹いて行脚するようになり、**普化僧と混同をきたした**」
— vagrants came to be **confused with** Fuke monks.

**A 17th–19th-century impostor problem is thus documented by the sources themselves.**
ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 adds: 「江戸期においても**偽の虚無僧が横行**していたが、偽虚無僧も皇室の裏紋を用いていたようである」
— fake komusō were rife in the Edo period and apparently also used the imperial reverse
crest.

`[V]` **A revocation of privileges BEFORE 1871 — easy to miss.**
『百科事典マイペディア』 「普化宗」 (kotobank, fetched 2026-11-13): 「江戸時代には浪人の多くがこの姿をとり，弊害が生じたので，**天保(てんぽう)のころ宗派としての特権を剥奪(はくだつ)された**」
— because many rōnin took this guise and abuses arose, **the sect's privileges as a sect
were stripped around the Tenpō era (1830–1844).** **No other source I read mentions
this.** `[U]` on corroboration — but if true it materially changes the 1871 story: the
sect would have been politically dismantled a generation before it was legally abolished.
**This is the single most important thing in these notes that I could not corroborate.**

`[V]` **The espionage claim, and how heavily it is hedged.** The sources differ sharply in
confidence, which is itself the finding:
- ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8): 「諸国通行の自由など種々の特権を持っていたため**隠密の役も務めたとも言われる**」
  — *double* hedge (とも + 言われる): "is also said to have".
- Nipponica (末木): 「江戸時代には浪人の集団ともみなされ、**密偵機関として利用された**」
  — flat indicative: "was **used** as an espionage apparatus." A Buddhist-studies scholar
  stating it outright.
- ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8) has a whole section on 「時代劇における虚無僧」 — komusō **in
  period drama** — noting that the deep hood makes them useful as assassins, *onmitsu*
  and disguised samurai, and describing the stock scene of a band of komusō who attack as
  they pass. **The sources are self-aware that the spy image is partly a jidaigeki
  convention.**

`[V]` **The weapon.** 『国語大辞典』 via ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」: komusō 「多く小袖に袈裟を掛け、深編笠をかぶり**刀を帯した**」 —
**wore a sword**. Combined with the samurai-only entry rule and the *Tsurezuregusa*
characterisation (§2.4), the armed-mendicant picture is documented. **What I did *not*
find in any Japanese reference source is the claim that the shakuhachi itself was used
as a club.** `[U]` — the thick root-end *jinashi* pipe is physically a cudgel, and the
claim is ubiquitous in English, but I could not source it. Treat as unverified folklore.

`[V]` **The regulated costume** (ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」, S8; translation mine, condensed):
for *takuhatsu*, an unpatterned garment in indigo or grey; the *obi* tied in front; **a
spare shakuhachi in a bag at the waist**; a bag at the neck; the *kesa* over the back; on
the head the deep woven hood called **天蓋 *tengai***; five layers of straw sandals; the
shakuhachi in hand. For travel: indigo cotton, *kyahan*, *kōgake*, straw sandals.
Originally a plain *amigasa* and white robe; the above was fixed by the Tokugawa bakufu.

`[V]` **The 明暗 alms-box is a late-Meiji prop, not an Edo artifact.** ja.wikipedia
「虚無僧」 (S8) and 「明暗寺」 (S8) both say it: 「よく時代劇で用いられる「明暗」と書かれた**偈箱**（げばこ）は、**明治末頃から見受けられるようになったもので、虚無僧の姿を真似た門付芸人が用いたもの**である（因みに「明暗」に宗教的な意味合いはなく、「私は明暗寺（みょうあんじ）の所属である」という程度の意味である）。**江戸時代には、皇室の裏紋である円に五三の桐の紋が入っており、「明暗」などと書かれてはいなかった。**」

My translation: "The *gebako* (alms box) inscribed 明暗 so often used in period dramas
**only begins to be seen from around the end of the Meiji era, and was used by
door-to-door entertainers imitating komusō** (incidentally 明暗 has no religious meaning;
it means roughly 'I belong to Myōan-ji'). **In the Edo period it bore the imperial reverse
crest, the 五三の桐 in a circle, and was not inscribed 明暗.**"

**This is a first-rate correction.** The single most recognisable visual sign of the
"Edo komusō" is a late-Meiji invention by street buskers, and the actual Edo marking was
an **imperial** crest — which supports the privilege/protection reading of the order, and
incidentally supports the Southern-Court legend's usefulness.

## 3. 吹禅 *suizen* and 本曲 *honkyoku*

### 3.1 What the Japanese sources actually say about *suizen*

The user's suspicion was that the modern framing owes more to the 20th century than to
the Edo sect. **The evidence is mixed and the picture is more interesting than either
"ancient" or "invented."**

`[V]` **The term is in the standard encyclopedia, as one of three uses of the
instrument.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 「尺八」 (S2, Kamisangō):

> 普化宗での尺八吹奏には，（1）法要の一環，（2）托鉢，（3）禅の手段（**座禅に対して吹禅（すいぜん）という**）の三つの場合があり，そこから当時の尺八曲の禅的・冥想的性格が生じた。

My translation: "Shakuhachi playing in the Fuke sect had three occasions: (1) as part of a
Buddhist service, (2) *takuhatsu* (mendicant begging), and (3) as a means of Zen — **as
against *zazen* (sitting Zen) this is called *suizen* (blowing Zen)**. From this arose the
Zen-like, meditative character of the shakuhachi pieces of the period."

Two things to notice. **First, *suizen* is one of three uses, and the other two are
liturgical and economic.** The 20th-century framing promotes (3) to the whole. Second,
Kamisangō presents the word as a **coinage by contrast with 座禅** — 「座禅に対して…という」 —
which is a note about *how the word is formed*, not an assertion of its age.

`[V]` **A different term is what the encyclopedias actually give for the doctrine.**
Nipponica 「普化宗」 (末木): 「尺八の吹奏をもって煩悩(ぼんのう)を断じ、虚無の境地に至る**虚無吹断**を禅の究極とする」
— the sect holds that **虚無吹断 *komu suidan*** — cutting off the defilements by blowing
the shakuhachi and reaching the state of emptiness — is the ultimate of Zen.
Britannica Japan 「普化宗」: 「典拠とする**経典はなく**，尺八による**虚無吹奏**を禅の至境とした」
— "**there is no scripture it takes as authority**; it held blowing into emptiness on the
shakuhachi to be Zen's highest state."
『百科事典マイペディア』 「普化宗」: 天外明普 「京都白川で**尺八の吹奏による禅**を鼓吹」 —
propagated "Zen by means of shakuhachi playing."

**So the encyclopedias' preferred vocabulary for the doctrine is 虚無吹断 / 虚無吹奏 /
「尺八の吹奏による禅」, and 吹禅 appears once, in an aside about word formation.** If 吹禅
were the Edo sect's own established technical term, one would expect it to be the headword
and 虚無吹断 the gloss, not the reverse.

`[V]` **吹禅 has no entry in either of the obvious reference works.** I checked:
- **kotobank has no 吹禅 entry.** The numeric ID I tried resolved to an unrelated article;
  no 吹禅 headword appears in the 尺八 or 普化宗 entry's 関連語 lists.
- **ja.wikipedia has no 吹禅 article** (verified: `action=raw` returns the
  "Wikimedia Error" page, 3,456 bytes, the signature of a non-existent title).
  The word appears in ja.wikipedia only *inside* the 尺八 article, in the 本曲 section:
  「もともとの本曲は、普化宗で**吹禅に使われた曲**を指していたが…」 (S7).

**That is a meaningful absence.** A term with the cultural weight English sources give
"blowing Zen" has no headword in the Japanese encyclopedias or in Japanese Wikipedia.

`[V]` **But it is alive and institutional in the Myōan line today.** On Myōan-ji's own
site (myouan-doushukai.org, fetched 2026-11-13):
- The hermitage episode: Kyochiku 「**吹禅三昧**の日を送り」 (spent his days in *suizen*
  absorption) — i.e. the temple projects the term back onto its 13th-century founder.
- An event series: 「第３７回『四国明暗尺八**吹禅**会』」 — the **37th** Shikoku Myōan
  Shakuhachi Suizen Meeting. A 37-run series implies the term has been in organisational
  use for decades. `[U]` on the start year.
- A document title: 「**吹禅行化誓願文**（すいぜんぎょうけせいがんもん）」, a slip handed to
  donors in return for alms, 「行化の目的を明示」 (stating the purpose of the itinerancy).
  `[U]` on its date — if this is an 1890s document it would push *suizen* back into the
  Meiji revival; if it is postwar it supports the 20th-century reading. **This is the
  single highest-value unresolved item in section 3.**

**My reading, stated as a hypothesis and not as a finding.** `[U]` The *concept* —
shakuhachi playing as Zen practice, the instrument as 法器 not 楽器 — is Edo and is well
documented (§3.3). The *word* 吹禅 is unlexicalised in Japanese reference works, is
presented by the standard encyclopedia as a back-formation against 座禅, and is today
chiefly an institutional term of the **Myōan / Taizan line** — the line that was
*reconstructed* from 1890 onward (§5.4). The most likely shape is therefore: Edo concept,
**Meiji-revival-or-later terminological crystallisation**, 20th-century export. But I did
not find a first attestation for the word and **cannot date it.** Anyone writing a
reference entry should say "the term's history is undocumented in the standard Japanese
sources" rather than assert either an Edo or a modern origin.

### 3.2 一音成仏 *ichion-jōbutsu*

`[V]-weak` **The reading is unsettled, which is itself a datum.** From Yahoo! Japan search
snippets (fetched 2026-11-13) of several Japanese pages: the term is read **いっとんじょうぶつ
*itton-jōbutsu*** on some pages and **いちおんじょうぶつ *ichion-jōbutsu*** on others, with
one page giving both: 「（いっとんじょうぶつ：いちおんじょうぶつ）」. The gloss given is
「一本の竹の管から出る一音が、そのまま悟りの境地に通じる」 — a single tone from one bamboo
pipe leads directly to the state of awakening. Another page (虚無僧寺オンライン): 「ひとつの音を吹く、或いは聴くことによって成仏する境地を意味しているものと**思われ**」
— note the hedge **思われ**, "is thought to mean": **a shakuhachi-world website is not sure
what its own slogan means.**

A Kokugakuin University page (kokugakuin.ac.jp, snippet only) quotes a player:
「尺八には"[一音成仏]"という言葉があります。一音を吹くことで悟り…」.

`[V]` **It has no kotobank entry and no ja.wikipedia article.** Checked by the same
methods as 吹禅.

`[V]` **A related doctrinal link, from the Fuke sect's own apologetics.** ja.wikipedia
「普化宗」 (S8) carries an external link, archived 2014-04-24, titled
「**普化宗の「一音成仏」と「音声説経」について**」 — "On the Fuke sect's *ichion-jōbutsu* and
*onjō sekkyō*". I did not open it (`[U]`), but the pairing with 音声説経 ("preaching by
vocal sound") locates the slogan in a **Buddhist sound-doctrine** frame, and one snippet
connects it to 「草木国土悉皆成仏」 (the Mahāyāna formula that grasses, trees and the land
all attain Buddhahood) — a *Tendai/hongaku* formula, not a Rinzai one. `[U]`

**Assessment.** `[U]` I could not establish when 一音成仏 enters the shakuhachi vocabulary,
whether it is attested in Edo Fuke documents, or even its settled reading. **It should be
presented in a reference as a slogan of the modern honkyoku world whose documentary
history is not established**, and specifically **not** as a doctrine of the Edo sect.
I can neither confirm nor refute an Edo origin.

### 3.3 法器 — the one piece of this complex that is solidly Edo

`[V]` This is where the sources are confident. 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 (S2):
「普化宗では尺八を**法器**と呼び，**楽器とはみなさず**，虚無僧以外の一般人の尺八吹奏や，遊芸としての吹奏，他楽器との合奏を**許さないと主張していた**」
— "In the Fuke sect the shakuhachi was called a *hōki* (ritual implement) and **was not
regarded as a musical instrument**; the sect **asserted** that it did not permit playing
by laypeople other than komusō, playing as entertainment, or ensemble with other
instruments."

Note **「主張していた」** — *asserted*, i.e. this was the sect's claim, not necessarily the
state of affairs. Kamisangō immediately undercuts it:
「しかし18世紀中ごろになると，虚無僧たちの尺八吹奏も音楽的な向上をみせはじめ，**表向き禁止といいつつも一般人の尺八吹奏や箏・三味線との合奏もかなり広まった**」
— "but by the mid-18th century the komusō's own playing began to show musical
improvement, and **while nominally prohibited, lay playing and ensemble with koto and
shamisen had already spread considerably.**"

ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7) agrees: 「建前上は一般の者は吹いてはならなかったが、**実際には尺八をたしなむ者はいた**」
— officially laypeople were not to play it, **but in fact there were people who did**.

`[V]` **An alternative name worth recording.** 『世界大百科事典（旧版）』「宗教音楽」 (quoted
on the kotobank 尺八 page): 「普化宗の伝統で尺八が〈**法竹（ほつちく）**〉と呼ばれる」 — in the
Fuke tradition the shakuhachi is called ***hotchiku*** (法竹, "dharma bamboo"). The entry
uses it as a comparative for the Jewish shofar, which it says might better be called a
法器 than an instrument. (Today *hotchiku* usually denotes a long unlacquered *jinashi*
pipe; `[U]` whether that narrowing is historical or 20th-century.)

**So the defensible claim is:** the Edo sect's doctrinal position was that the shakuhachi
was a ritual implement and not an instrument, and that position was already failing in
practice by the mid-18th century. Everything about *suizen* as a named practice is
shakier than that.

### 3.4 本曲 / 外曲 and how 1871 manufactured the ambiguity

`[V]` **The definitions.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7), citing Tsukitani in
『日本の伝統芸能講座』 (2008) p. 394: 本曲 = 「その楽器のみによる楽器本来の楽曲」 (music
original to the instrument, for that instrument alone); 外曲 = 「他種目の旋律をその楽器用に編曲した楽曲」
(melodies of other genres arranged for this instrument). 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 (S2):
本曲 = 「尺八本来の曲」つまり「尺八だけで奏する曲」; 外曲 = 「他種の音楽に尺八を添えて奏する曲」.

`[V]` **The category did not exist before it was needed.** Nipponica (S1, Tsukitani) is
explicit and this is the sharpest formulation of the whole 1871 argument:
「**普化宗時代はすべて本曲であったため、この分類すら不要だった**が、廃宗後三曲合奏に仲間入りしたことで区別が生じた」
— "In the Fuke-sect period everything was *honkyoku*, **so even this classification was
unnecessary**; the distinction arose when, after the sect's abolition, the shakuhachi
joined the sankyoku ensemble."

`[V]` **And 1871 made "honkyoku" ambiguous.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7): 「もともとの本曲は、普化宗で吹禅に使われた曲を指していたが、**1871年の普化宗廃止後は宗教音楽とは無縁な尺八のみの独奏曲や重奏曲も本曲と呼ばれるようになった**。これらの比較的新しい本曲と普化宗で吹奏された狭義の本曲を区別するため、後者を特に**古典本曲**と呼ぶことがある。」
— so **古典本曲 *koten honkyoku* is the unambiguous term, and it exists only because 1871
created the ambiguity.**

Tsukitani (S1) traces the subsequent inflation: 「また近代流派の流曲を「都山流本曲」などとよんだことから、古典本曲と近代本曲に区別され、さらに演奏形態で近代本曲を**独奏本曲と合奏本曲**に分けるなど、**用語の拡大化**がみられる」
— because the modern schools called their own pieces "Tozan-ryū honkyoku," a
koten/kindai split appeared, and then modern honkyoku were further split by performing
format into solo and ensemble honkyoku: "an **expansion of the terminology**."

**Resulting taxonomy** (my synthesis of S1, S2, S7):

| Term | Means |
|---|---|
| 古典本曲 koten honkyoku | the Fuke-period komusō repertoire, mostly anonymous, mostly solo |
| 近代本曲 kindai honkyoku | newly composed school pieces (Tozan-ryū honkyoku etc.) |
| ├ 独奏本曲 | solo modern honkyoku |
| └ 合奏本曲 | ensemble modern honkyoku — **Nakao Tozan's innovation** (§5.3) |
| 外曲 gaikyoku | jiuta/sōkyoku (and some nagauta) with a shakuhachi part added |
| 新曲 shinkyoku | in the sankyoku world, Meiji-to-prewar pieces incl. Miyagi (§6.3) |

`[V]` **Repertoire counts.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7): the honkyoku blown by Edo komusō,
including the Kinko-ryū honkyoku, number 「**150曲あまりが伝承されている**」 — **more than
150 pieces transmitted**, 「これらは宗教音楽として成立し、**作者、作曲年代ともに基本的に不詳**」
(composers and dates basically unknown). Transmitting centres named: 弘前の**根笹派錦風流**,
浜松の普大寺の流れをくむ名古屋の**西園流**, 京都明暗寺の**明暗真法流**と**明暗対山流**,
**博多一朝軒**, **越後明暗寺**, 東北の**布袋軒**, **松巖軒**.

`[V]` **The same-title-different-piece problem.** ja.wikipedia (S7): 「全国の寺院で伝承される本曲には**同名異曲が多く**ある。『鈴慕』『三谷』『鶴の巣籠』などは本曲の代表的な曲名であるが、**曲によっては10種類以上の旋律の異なるものが伝承されている**」
— more than ten melodically different versions under one title for some pieces.
『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 (S2) agrees: 「古典本曲には流派や芸系あるいは**奏者個人による差異が大きく，同名異曲も多い**」.

**Consequence for a reference work: a *koten honkyoku* title is not a work-identifier.**
"Reibo" or "Sanya" names a family of pieces, and the school/lineage must be specified.

`[V]` **The 虚鈴/虚霊 case, worked out.** 『世界大百科事典（旧版）』「虚鈴(虚霊)」 (via
kotobank.jp/word/虚鐸-1302321, fetched 2026-11-13) gives the variants by school:
琴古流《**真虚霊**》; 西園流・明暗対山流《**虚鈴**》; 明暗真法流《**真虚鈴**》《**行虚鈴**》《**草虚鈴**》
(shin/gyō/sō — the calligraphic triad). And on the origin legend:
「普化尺八起源伝説によれば…その音を慕った者が竹管で鐸音を模して吹奏し，その曲を《虚鐸》と名づけたのが…起源といわれるが，**史実としては不明というほかはない**。中世末期ないし近世初期に何らかの形の原曲が存在し，伝承系統により多くのバリアンテを生じた可能性は考えられるが，現行の各曲は流派差･個人差がかなり大きいので，**推断は難しい**」
— "as historical fact there is no option but to call it unknown"; it is **conceivable**
that some original existed in the late medieval or early modern period and produced many
variants by transmission line, "but inference is difficult."

**That is the model paragraph for how to write about honkyoku origins.**

## 4. 1871 and the rupture

### 4.1 The year, the instrument and the legal form

`[V]` **The year is 1871 (明治4) and the instrument was a 太政官布告 *Dajōkan fukoku*
(Grand Council of State proclamation).** ja.wikipedia 「虚無僧」 (S8):
「明治4年（1871年）、明治政府は幕府との関係が深い普化宗を廃止する**太政官布告**を出し、**虚無僧は僧侶の資格を失い、民籍に編入された**」
— the Meiji government issued a Dajōkan proclamation abolishing the Fuke sect, which had
deep ties to the bakufu; **the komusō lost their clerical status and were entered in the
commoner register (民籍).**

`[V]` **Myōan-ji's own account gives the month and the scope.**
(myouan-doushukai.org, fetched 2026-11-13): 「**1871(明治4)年10月**、大政官布告[sic]によって普化宗は**廃宗**、**虚無僧托鉢は禁止**、更に**全国普化宗寺院は廢毀**され」
— **October 1871**: the sect abolished, **komusō mendicant begging prohibited**, and
**Fuke temples nationwide demolished**. `[U]` I did not locate the proclamation's number or
text in the 法令全書 / NDL digital collections; **the month (October) rests on Myōan-ji's
own page alone.**

`[V]` **The stated reason.** ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8): 「**江戸幕府との繋がりや身分制度の残滓が強かったため**、明治になって政府により1871年に解体され、宗派としては失われている」
— because its ties to the Edo bakufu and the **residue of the status system** within it
were strong. Nipponica (末木): abolished 「**官命により**」, by government order.
『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』 (S2): 「**維新政府の命令で**廃止され」.

`[V]` **A competing framing worth flagging.** ja.wikipedia 「明暗寺」 (S8) attributes the
1871 event to **廃仏毀釈** (*haibutsu kishaku*, the anti-Buddhist movement):
「明治4年（1871年）に**廃仏毀釈により**廃宗廃寺となった」. **The other sources frame it as
targeted bakufu-affiliate dismantlement, not general anti-Buddhism.** These are different
causal claims and a reference work should not pick one silently. My reading (`[U]`): the
sect-specific framing is better supported, because the proclamation named this one sect
while Rinzai Zen as a whole was untouched — but *haibutsu kishaku* is the enabling
environment. The two are not exclusive.

### 4.2 What happened to the temples

`[V]` ja.wikipedia 「普化宗」 (S8): 「その後**一月寺は日蓮正宗の寺院となり、鈴法寺は廃寺となった**」
— Ichigatsu-ji became a **Nichiren Shōshū** temple; Reihō-ji was **abolished outright**.
(Note the site of Reihō-ji survives as 鈴法寺公園, a park in Ōme, per the article's
wikilink.) **Both head temples of the sect are gone as Fuke institutions.**

`[V]` **Myōan-ji's own, much more specific account** of what a dissolution looked like on
the ground (myouan-doushukai.org, fetched 2026-11-13). Under the 34th *kanshu*, 自笑昨非:
the founder-image 虚竹禅師像, the 虚霊山 temple plaque, and the **memorial tablets (霊牌) of
thirty-three generations** of abbots, deputies and *kanshu* were **entrusted to 爾瓊林和尚,
abbot of [善慧院] within Tōfuku-ji**, with whom 昨非 was on good terms; 昨非 himself
**returned to lay life** under the name 明暗覚昨. 「ここに普化宗としての明暗寺の歴史は一応の終止符を打つ事となったのです」
— "thus the history of Myōan-ji as a Fuke-sect temple came, for the time being, to a
full stop."

**This is the mechanism by which the Myōan line survived 1871: as deposited objects in
someone else's temple.** The institution ended; the *regalia* were warehoused. Everything
in §5.4 follows from that.

`[V]` **A Bakumatsu detail from the same source that explains the vulnerability.** The
33rd *kanshu*, **玄堂観妙**, and his disciple **素行** were active in imperial-loyalist
politics, were implicated in the **蛤御門事件** (the Hamaguri-gomon / Kinmon incident), were
arrested by bakufu officials, and 玄堂観妙 was **beheaded in the 11th month of 1866
(慶応2)**; 素行 died of illness after release. **So the head of the Kyoto head temple was
executed five years before the abolition.** `[U]` on corroboration outside Myōan-ji's own
page — but it is a concrete, dateable, checkable claim and it is the kind of thing that
would explain why the order had no protector left.

### 4.3 What happened to the komusō and to the repertoire

`[V]` **Occupationally, they became teachers.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7):
「普化宗は政府により1871年に解体され、**虚無僧は尺八の師匠などに転じた**」 — the komusō
**converted into shakuhachi teachers** and the like.

`[V]` **A documented personal trajectory, which is the type case.** 2代荒木古童 (Araki Kodō
II, 竹翁, **1823–1908-01-17**), ja.wikipedia 「荒木古童」 (S13): born 荒木半三郎, third son
of 荒木亀三郎, a retainer of **Minakuchi domain in Ōmi**; first studied with 横田五柳 of
一閑流, then **became a komusō** and entered the school of 豊田古童 of Kinko-ryū; on
Toyoda's death in **1850** he inherited the name 古童 and became a pupil of 久松風陽;
「**普化宗の廃止後も楽器としての尺八の復興に力を注いだ**」 — after the sect's abolition he
devoted himself to the revival of **the shakuhachi as a musical instrument**; later gave
the name 古童 to his eldest son and took the name 竹翁.

**That sentence is the hinge: 楽器としての尺八 — "the shakuhachi *as a musical instrument*."
The re-legitimisation was explicitly a reclassification from 法器 to 楽器.**

`[V]` **The reclassification as the encyclopedia states it.** 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』
(S2, Kamisangō): 「普化宗は1871年（明治4）に維新政府の命令で廃止され，**尺八も存亡の危機に面した**が，**荒木古童など当時の琴古流の指導者の尽力により普化宗を離れた楽器としての存続が認められ**，以来，**琴古流では本曲よりも外曲に力を注ぐようになり**，東京を中心としつつ全国的に普及していく。」

My translation: "The Fuke sect was abolished in 1871 by order of the Restoration
government, and **the shakuhachi too faced a crisis of survival**; but **through the
efforts of the Kinko-ryū leaders of the day, such as Araki Kodō, its continuation as a
musical instrument separated from the Fuke sect was recognised**, and thereafter
**Kinko-ryū came to put more effort into *gaikyoku* than into *honkyoku***, spreading
nationwide from a Tokyo base."

**Three consequences in one sentence, all important:**
1. Survival was **not** automatic — 存亡の危機, a crisis of survival.
2. The permission was obtained by **the Kinko-ryū leadership lobbying**, i.e. the
   instrument's legitimacy was *negotiated* in the 1870s, not inherited.
3. The price was **repertoire inversion**: Kinko-ryū shifted its weight from *honkyoku*
   to *gaikyoku*. **The school most identified in English with the classical solo
   repertoire deliberately de-emphasised it after 1871.** That is a direct correction.

`[V]` **The instrument itself was modified in the same movement.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」
(S7) on Araki Kodō II, citing kotobank: 「**尺八の指孔位置や歌口を改良した**ことでも知られ、東京を中心に全国に普及させた」
— known also for **improving the finger-hole positions and the blowing edge**. The physical
object is another researcher's lane, but the *fact that re-legitimisation involved
redesigning the instrument* belongs here: the lacquered, pitch-accurate 地塗り bore that
makes *gaikyoku* possible (§1.3 / Nipponica S1) is part of the same post-1871 package.

`[V]` **The repertoire survived by lineage, not by institution.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7):
「宗教音楽としての本曲は、各地の本曲を収集した黒沢琴古の琴古流本曲、西園流を学び明治期に明暗対山流を興し、明暗教会の再興に尽力した**樋口対山（1856年 - 1915年）**の系統をはじめ、**各地において明治維新後も伝承されたものが現代においても血脈を保っている**」
— the honkyoku-as-religious-music survived through Kurosawa Kinko's collected Kinko-ryū
honkyoku, through the line of **Higuchi Taizan**, and through local transmissions that
continued after the Restoration. §3.4 lists the surviving centres (Kinpū-ryū at Hirosaki,
Seien-ryū at Nagoya, Myōan Shinpō and Myōan Taizan at Kyoto, Itchōken at Hakata, Echigo
Myōan-ji, Hoteiken and Shōganken in Tōhoku).

**So the shape of the rupture is: institution destroyed, costume criminalised, repertoire
preserved in provincial lineages, instrument reclassified and physically redesigned, and
the whole thing re-entered public life inside the koto/shamisen ensemble.**

### 4.4 The Meiji music-education context

`[V]` **The state music apparatus.** ja.wikipedia 「音楽取調掛」 (fetched 2026-11-13): the
**音楽取調掛** (Music Investigation Committee), a Monbushō music-education body, existed
**1879–1887** and in 1887 was reorganised into the **東京音楽学校** (Tokyo Music School,
later the music faculty of Tokyo University of the Arts). The 1872 学制 had already
prescribed 唱歌 (singing) and 奏楽 as school subjects but, lacking trained teachers, marked
them 「当分之ヲ欠ク」 — *omitted for the time being*. **伊沢修二 Isawa Shūji** and
**目賀田種太郎** submitted a proposal in 1878; the committee opened **1879-10-07**; Isawa
became its head **1881-10-26**; on **1881-10-30** he submitted the 「音楽取調ニ付見込書」
adopting the policy of 「**東西両洋の音楽を折衷して**唱歌を作成する」 — creating school songs
by **blending the music of East and West**.

`[U]` **The user's "1883" I could not pin down.** The most likely referent is the second
volume of 『小学唱歌集』 (*Shōgaku shōka shū*, the first state school-song collection),
whose volumes are usually dated 初編 1881 / 第二編 1883 / 第三編 1884 — **but I did not
verify those dates** and the 音楽取調掛 article I read did not give them. **Flagged.**

`[V]` **The shakuhachi's one real point of contact with that apparatus, and it is a
person.** **上原六四郎 Uehara Rokushirō (1848-12-28 – 1913-04-01)**, ja.wikipedia
「上原六四郎」 and 「俗楽旋律考」 (fetched 2026-11-13):
- Born a retainer of **Iwatsuki domain**; studied French at the 開成学校 from 1869.
- **From 1882 he worked in the Monbushō's 専門学務局 音楽取調掛** — i.e. inside the state
  music-education body — and later taught at 東京職工学校, 東京商業学校, 東京美術学校 and
  東京工業学校; **in 1891 became a professor at the 東京音楽学校.**
- 「上原は、音楽理論研究家であるとともに**荒木古童門下の尺八の妙手**でもあった」 — he was a
  music theorist **and an accomplished shakuhachi player in Araki Kodō's school.**
- His 『**俗楽旋律考**』 (*Zokugaku senritsu kō*), 金港堂, **August 1895**, published at the
  urging of 村岡範為馳, principal of the Tokyo Music School: it analysed Japanese *zokugaku*
  modes and scales into **都節音階 (陰旋法)** and **田舎節音階 (陽旋法)**, then applied the
  analysis to the shamisen, koto **and shakuhachi**, explaining their tunings and
  modulation 「楽理上」 — in music-theoretical terms. It argued **against Isawa Shūji**, who
  had assimilated Japanese scales to the ancient Greek Dorian and Phrygian; Uehara held
  the two were fundamentally different.

**This is the re-legitimisation mechanism made concrete.** A pupil of Araki Kodō II sat
inside the Ministry of Education's music bureau from 1882 and became a professor at the
national music school in 1891, and wrote the book that put the shakuhachi into Japanese
*music theory*. ja.wikipedia 「尺八」 (S7) closes the loop: **初代川瀬順輔 Kawase Junsuke I
(1870–1959)**, 「近代尺八の祖のひとりと言われ」 (said to be one of the fathers of the modern
shakuhachi), studied under **both Araki Kodō II and Uehara Rokushirō, professor at the
Tokyo Music School**, and opened a dōjō in Tokyo in **1902**.

`[V]` **A dark counterpoint the Japanese sources do not suppress.** ja.wikipedia 「尺八」
(S7), citing 齋藤桂『1933年を聴く：戦前日本の音風景』 (NTT出版, 2017), ch. 1, 「尺八奏者・野村景久による殺人 音楽の合理化と精神論」,
pp. 16–45: **野村景久 Nomura Kagehisa**, a rising young shakuhachi player-composer who had
performed with 古賀正男 and others, appeared on radio and written widely, **murdered a
family of four and robbed them in 1933 and was executed**. The article's reading:
「虚無僧の怪しさから来る悪いイメージを払拭し近代化を進めてきた尺八界を揺るがせ、**かつて「法器」であった尺八の精神性を見直す気運を生んだ**」
— it shook a shakuhachi world that had been trying to dispel the shady komusō image and
modernise, and **generated a mood of re-examining the spirituality of the shakuhachi as a
former 法器.**

**Read that carefully: a 1933 murder is credited with re-spiritualising the instrument.**
That is direct evidence that the "Zen instrument" framing was *re-asserted* in the 1930s,
after four decades of deliberate secularisation — which bears directly on the *suizen*
dating question in §3.1. Note Nomura Kagehisa is also a named participant in
新日本音楽 (§7.1); the modernising and re-spiritualising currents run through one person.
`[V]` for ja.wikipedia's citation of Saitō; `[U]` for Saitō's argument, which I did not read.
