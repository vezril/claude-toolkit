# The Shakuhachi

The **shakuhachi** (尺八) is an end-blown bamboo flute with **five finger holes** — four front, one back — and no mouthpiece: the player's lower lip and chin form the embouchure directly against an **outward-bevelled blowing edge** (歌口 *utaguchi*) cut into the top of the tube. It is made from **madake** bamboo, usually in two pieces joined at a mid-joint, and the standard instrument is **one shaku eight sun** long with a fundamental of **D4**.

This skill covers the instrument and the history. [[shakuhachi-performance]] covers how it is played, notated and taught; [[shakuhachi-dokyoku]] covers the Watazumi Dōso → Yokoyama Katsuya lineage.

## The headline: there are four instruments under this name, and three of them are dead

This is the single most important correction, because the English account routinely presents one continuous thousand-year tradition.

1. **The gagaku shakuhachi** — a Tang import, six holes, in the Nara court. **It died out by the 10th century.** A Japanese source of **1170** (『今鏡』) already calls it 「吹き絶えたる笛」, *a flute whose playing had ceased*. Eight survive in the **Shōsōin** plus a ninth from Hōryū-ji — some of **jade, stone and ivory**, carved to imitate bamboo. The word is first attested in the **東大寺献物帳 of 756**. **No notation survives**, so any account of how it was played is speculation.
2. **The hitoyogiri (一節切)** — **a sibling, not an ancestor.** It and the Fuke shakuhachi both descend from a 14th-century five-hole prototype. It was a complete independent musical culture: named schools (宗左流, 宗勲流, 指田流), a named master with dates (**大森宗勲, 1570–1625**), **three printed self-instruction manuals (1664, 1669, 1687)**, a solo repertoire and an ensemble repertoire with koto and shamisen — and it **died**, with a dated failed revival (1818). Its death had a musicological cause: **it could not play the *miyako-bushi* scale** that came to dominate Japanese music after the 17th century.
3. **The tempuku (天吹)** of Satsuma — a separate regional instrument.
4. **The Fuke shakuhachi**, the ancestor of the modern instrument — and *its* origin is openly unknown. 『改訂新版 世界大百科事典』: 「普化尺八の起源や伝来はまったく不明で、古代尺八との関係もわからない」 — *the origin and transmission of the Fuke shakuhachi are completely unknown, and its relation to the ancient shakuhachi is unknown.*

## Two documentary problems the English account skates over

**The Fuke sect is documented only from the 17th century**, though shakuhachi-playing mendicants are attested from the **14th** (『徒然草』, 『三十二番職人歌合』) — three centuries before the institution. The Chinese transmission through Kakushin is 「傍証がないので史実とはみなし難い」, *without corroborating evidence and hard to regard as historical fact*.

**The *Kyotaku denki kokujikai* is not merely unreliable — its source text does not exist.** Britannica Japan: 「『虚鐸伝記』なる漢籍に国文の注解を施した体裁であるが、原典は存在しない…大部分は伝説的で史実とは認めがたい」. By **Yamamoto Morihide**, begun **1779**, published Kyoto **1795**. And the forgery case is old: raised by **栗原広太, 『尺八史考』, 1918** and settled by **中塚竹禅, 1932** — decades before Sanford's 1977 *Monumenta Nipponica* article, the usual English reference point. (A *second*, separate forgery question around the 1614 *Keichō jōsho* is **not** settled — the Japanese sources disagree, and this skill reports the disagreement rather than resolving it.)

## There were no shakuhachi schools in the Edo period

This is the second structural correction, and it reframes the whole school map. Nipponica's 尺八 entry, by **月溪恒子 Tukitani Tuneko**, states flatly: 「流派のような組織は存在しなかった」 — *organisations of the kind called* ryū *did not exist* — and 「家元組織としての流が確立するのは普化宗廃止（1871）後」, *the* ryū *as an iemoto organisation became established only after the abolition of the Fuke sect in 1871*. Edo-period teaching ran through the Fuke temples' 尺八指南役 and 吹合.

So **"Kinko-ryū, founded in the 18th century" is shorthand at best.** Kurosawa Kinko collected a repertoire; the *school* as an institution is a post-1871 construction. Two consequences the English account misses:

- **Kinko-ryū deliberately de-emphasised honkyoku after 1871** — 「琴古流では本曲よりも外曲に力を注ぐようになり」 — which was the price of getting the instrument reclassified from 法器 (religious implement) to 楽器 (musical instrument).
- **The Myōan/Taizan line is the *most recently edited*, not the purest and oldest.** Higuchi Taizan collected the dispersed repertoire from **1890**; **小林紫山 notated 32 pieces and standardised them (定型化) between 1914 and 1938**; the lineage was **suspended twice** (1871–90, 1938–49), and the 1949 succession was installed by a committee **that included the other schools' heads**.

Even the famous number is contested, and by the same scholar: the **Kinko honkyoku count is 36** in one Kamisangō publication and **「30余り」** ("thirty-odd") in his own encyclopedia entry.

## 1871 manufactured the ambiguity in the word "honkyoku"

The Meiji abolition of the Fuke sect is the hinge, and its cleanest consequence is terminological. **本曲** originally meant the pieces used for **吹禅 *suizen*** in the Fuke sect. After 1871, religiously unconnected shakuhachi solo and ensemble pieces also came to be called honkyoku — so the narrow Fuke repertoire is now distinguished as **古典本曲 *koten honkyoku***. **"Honkyoku" is ambiguous; 古典本曲 is not.** Over **150** pieces of the Edo komusō repertoire survive in transmission.

## The Living National Treasures, and the mechanism English gets wrong

Designation is **各個認定** under 文化財保護法 art. 71(2), and the crucial mechanism is that **the designation of 尺八 itself lapses when its holder dies**. The Agency for Cultural Affairs' 2022 答申 therefore *re-designates* 尺八 while certifying Nomura — which means **there was no shakuhachi Living National Treasure between 2014 and 2022.**

All seven holders, with years: **納富寿童 1967** · **島原帆山 1982** · **山口五郎 1992** · **青木鈴慕 II / 鈴翁 1999** · **山本邦山 2002** · **野村峰山 2022-07-22** · **善養寺惠介 2025-07-18**. (And 青木鈴翁 **is** Aoki Reibo II, 1935-10-04 – 2018-08-21, who took the name in 1975, was certified in 1999, and renamed himself 鈴翁 in 2018 — a question two passes had to settle.)

**Every one is Kinko or Tozan** — the two schools with licensing systems. [[shakuhachi-dokyoku]]'s Yokoyama Katsuya is not on the list.

## Two more claims to stop repeating

- **"The shakuhachi replaced the kokyū in the sankyoku ensemble."** Japanese Wikipedia calls this 「全く根拠のない発言」 — *an utterly groundless statement*. Both instrumentations survive, and most Edo *nishiki-e* show the kokyū. (A second pass also found that **the shakuhachi was not originally in 三曲 at all**.)
- **"吹禅 *suizen* is the ancient Fuke term for blowing Zen."** 吹禅 has **no kotobank headword and no Japanese Wikipedia article**. The encyclopedias' doctrinal terms are **虚無吹断 / 虚無吹奏**; 吹禅 appears once, as a back-formation against 座禅. The *concept* is Edo — 法器 is solidly attested — but **the word's history is undocumented, so say that rather than dating it.**

## Things about the instrument that are usually got wrong

- **Madake is *Phyllostachys reticulata* (Rupr.) K.Koch.** *P. bambusoides* is a **synonym**, not the accepted name — and it is certainly not *P. edulis* (moso). Verified against GBIF's taxonomic backbone.
- **"1.8 shaku" on a modern instrument is a pitch name, not a measurement.** The distinction is 正寸 (exact length) versus **正律 (exact pitch)**, and 正律 is now mainstream. This is nearly absent from English writing, and the cause of divergence is the bore — i.e. the jinashi/jiari axis again.
- **54.5 cm vs 43.7 cm is a units problem, not a contradiction.** Nipponica's smaller figure is 1.8 shaku in the **Tang 小尺** (~24.6 cm); 54.5 cm is the modern **曲尺** (exactly 10/33 m, so 1尺8寸 = 545.45 mm).
- **The bore is an inverted cone**, roughly 20 mm at the top narrowing to ~15 mm at the foot. The root end's external flare is not a bore flare.
- **Two-piece construction is for bore adjustment and material selection.** Portability is explicitly the side-effect — the Japanese source gives the reasons in that order.
- **籐巻き *tomaki* rattan binding is primarily a crack repair, not ornament** — a working shop lists it as 「割れ止めの基本仕上げ」, included in the crack-repair price.
- **半地無し *han-jinashi* is a real third category** (ji only in the node depressions). And *ji* is classically urushi plus 砥の粉, but **gypsum (石膏) is commonest today**.
- **No Japanese source claims jinashi sounds better.** The reference framing is that jinashi *does not give accurate pitch and the player must correct it*, with per-instrument individuality offered as the compensating virtue in classical honkyoku playing. The jinashi-superiority claim is a Western accretion — and Riley Lee argues against it in print from inside the lineage most associated with it.
- **Madake is outside CITES**, verified positionally rather than assumed: **no Poaceae appears in any appendix**. But **ivory utaguchi inlay is Appendix I**, and for US travel there is **no route at all for Asian elephant ivory** — the personal-effects exemption excludes Appendix-I specimens except worked African elephant ivory pre-dating **26 February 1976**.
