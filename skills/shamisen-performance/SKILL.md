# Shamisen Performance Practice

How the **shamisen** is held, struck, stopped, tuned, pitched, notated and taught. This is the companion to [[shamisen]], which covers the instrument as an object and the music's history and genre map, and to [[tsugaru-shamisen]], which covers the one tradition with its own technique.

The reason this exists as a separate skill is that **the English-language account of Japanese scales and tuning is unusually unreliable**, and correcting it takes more room than a paragraph. The sources here are the Japanese reference works and the Japan Arts Council's own educational material, read in Japanese; the paywalled Anglophone scholarship (Johnson, Malm, Garland, Grove, Koizumi) could not be read and **nothing is attributed to it**. The bibliographic records for those works, and the two journal articles that would settle the open questions, are in [[shamisen]]'s `references/sources.md`.

## The five things worth knowing before anything else

- **There is no fixed reference string.** The three tunings are usually taught as intervals above the first string, but Nipponica explicitly rejects that framing: going from *sansagari* to *niagari* is done by **lowering string 1** a tone. The relative-interval-from-the-bottom picture is a Western convenience, not how the instrument works.
- **Absolute pitch is not fixed, but it is not arbitrary either.** The first string is tuned to the **singer's voice**, or referenced to a **shakuhachi or shinobue**. Pitch is a property of the occasion.
- **The voice deviates from the shamisen, not the reverse.** Nipponica's formulation is that the shamisen line is **規範** (normative) in learning, and the song or narration is "technically and aesthetically heightened as a *deviation* (ずれ) from the shamisen melody." That inverts the usual English framing of accompaniment.
- **The yō/in framework is held to be wrong by Japanese musicology itself** — not merely mislabelled by Westerners. Heibonsha's *Mypedia* says so in both entries: Japanese traditional music has **scales, not modes**, and the "yō" basket conflates two genuinely different pitch sets (*min'yō* and *ritsu*). See `references/scales-notation-and-pedagogy.md` §5.3.
- **Fretlessness has a specific purpose.** Beyond expressive bending, it lets melody notes be tuned into exact harmonic agreement with the open first string so that the **sawari rings** — which Nipponica names as an explicit criterion of good playing.

## Technique, in outline

The right hand does nearly everything. The large flat **撥 bachi** strikes downward (**打ち** *uchi*) with the body of the stroke, and the instrument's characteristic sound comes from the plectrum hitting the **skin as well as the string**. The upstroke scoop (**スクイ** *sukui*) and the left-hand pull-off (**ハジキ** *hajiki*) and slide (**スリ** *suri*) supply the ornament vocabulary. Left-hand positions are **勘所 kandokoro** — "intuition places" — learned as a body map rather than read off markers, since there are no frets.

A real pedagogical divide runs through this: some articulations are **written** in the notation and some are **expression** that notation does not record, and schools disagree about which is which. The reference file keeps the two apart and flags the terms it could not verify rather than guessing at them.

## Notation: there is no single system, and that is the point

The shamisen was transmitted for its first century with **no notation at all** — the expedient was to sing the melody using mouth-syllables and memorise it. Then, from the Meiji period, too many systems arrived at once:

- **文化譜 bunkafu** — the dominant modern tablature. String-and-number, with `0` = open and semitone steps, so `0`–`10` spans an octave. NDL catalogue records credit **杵家彌七 Kineie Yashichi** (not 杵屋, as Nipponica has it); publisher 邦楽社.
- **研精会譜 kenseikai-fu** — a **solfège** notation, not tablature. It encodes orthogonal information to bunkafu: pitch rather than hand position.
- **口三味線 kuchi-jamisen** — the mouth-syllable system (*ten / ton / chin / shan*…). Two reference works **flatly contradict each other** about what the syllables denote, and gidayū differs again, so any single canonical table is overstated.
- **Western staff notation** — used, but it loses the hand position, the sawari, and the rhythmic feel.
