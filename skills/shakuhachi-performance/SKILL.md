# Shakuhachi Performance Practice

How the **shakuhachi** is held, blown, fingered, bent, notated, and taught. This is the companion to [[shakuhachi]], which covers the instrument as an object and the history; the Watazumi → Yokoyama lineage has its own skill at [[shakuhachi-dokyoku]].

The main reason this is a separate skill is that **the English-language account of shakuhachi technique and notation is unreliable in a specific way**: it homogenises across schools that disagree, and it inherits mystical register from a tradition the Japanese reference literature treats with more care. Every claim here is tagged verified-against-a-named-artifact or explicitly flagged.

## The three things worth knowing before anything else

- **There are two note-name systems in common use, and they are not interchangeable.** **Kinko: ロ ツ レ チ リ** (ro–tsu–re–chi–**ri**). **Tozan: ロ ツ レ チ ハ** (ro–tsu–re–chi–**ha**). Same five pitches on 1.8 shaku (D F G A C), different last syllable. And **ヒ / イ / ハ denote different pitches in different schools**, so a bare syllable without a school name is ambiguous. (A third system, **フホウ fuhō**, is older than ro-tsu-re, was used for the hitoyogiri, and **survives today in 竹保流 Chikuhō-ryū**.)
- **Meri and kari are acoustics, not mystique.** Tilting the chin down lowers the pitch and changes the timbre by changing the geometry of the air stream against the blowing edge; Wolfe and Smith's title concedes it directly: *"Some acoustics of the shakuhachi — and of the shakuhachi player's face."* **The player's face is part of the instrument.** Working ranges, measured: **meri ~2 semitones, kari ~1 semitone**, maximum over **4 semitones**, widening toward the all-open fingering. The 2:1 asymmetry is almost never stated in English. And **meri notes have a different timbre, not just a different pitch** — which is musically exploited rather than corrected for.
- **Notation is a late-19th-century reform instrument, not a thousand-year tradition.** The **ro-tsu-re tablature was devised by Araki Kodō after 1871**, to make the flute notatable for ensemble playing with koto and shamisen — i.e. **Kinko and Tozan notation are cousins out of the same reform, not ancient rivals.** The hitoyogiri's older **フホウ** system is the thing they both replaced.

## The corrections that will most save you from a confident wrong statement

- **Kinko's メ mark and Tozan's small-glyph メ do not correspond by any single rule.** Kinko's メ is two semitones on ツ and リ but one on チ and ロ; Tozan's small glyph is two on ツ and ハ but one on レ, チ and ロ; and Tozan's メ *shallows* the meri on a full-size glyph while *deepening* it on a small one. This is the single likeliest place for an English reference to be quietly wrong, and it is in `references/technique-and-notation.md` §2.5 with the schools' own charts.
- ***Yuri* is executed differently by school.* **Tozan shakes the head; Kinko shakes with the hand.** That one sentence proves the broader rule: never describe a shakuhachi technique without naming the school.
- **玉音 names two different techniques.** The throat-gargle *tamane* that Tukitani describes, and a **tongue-flutter タマ音** that the Utsugi & Moriyama measurement paper describes. Same reading, different mechanism — never write "tamane" unqualified.
- **"Free rhythm" is rejected from inside the tradition, not just by critics.** Kakizakai Kaoru: 「自由リズム」は「非常に誤解を招きやすく、問題があります」 — *extremely misleading and problematic* — and 「本曲の「間」には最善の「間」があるはずです」, *there must be a best* ma.
- **Honkyoku notation is not rhythm-free either.** Kinko has 間拍子 dots and duration lines; what differs is **the time-value conventions differ across every school**. Kinko even **prints the disagreement**: the mark **「或」** carries alternative *hand positions and alternative held-lengths*, 「因為持たれるのを長さは、流儀によって異なる」 — *because the length held differs by studio*.
- **A lineage-internal teacher states there is no definition distinguishing なやし / かむり / 振り**: 「はっきりした使い分けは無さそうだ」. English sources that offer three crisp definitions are inventing precision the tradition does not claim.
- **オトシ is not a pitch drop.** 落し is a run of **finger strikes that accelerates and decelerates**. The waterfall association belongs to the piece section **瀧落シ**.
- **込み吹き is not a blowing technique.** 葛山幻海: 「息を込める意味に取られがちだが、ユリの一種」 — commonly mistaken for a blowing technique but actually a kind of *yuri*, and 錦風流-specific.
- **"At least one major lineage forbids vibrato in classical honkyoku"** — 「古典本曲ではビブラートはつけません」. Flat statement, not a nuance.
- **Five commonly-cited English ornament terms are not in any Japanese reference work read for this skill:** **あたり** (as a term distinct from 打ち), **ネヤシ** (almost certainly a mis-transcription of ナヤシ), **ケシ** (the verb 消す is a real score instruction, but 切 and 韻 cover the territory), **大ユリ** (Tukitani's triad is 縦/横/**斜**, not 縦/横/大), and **半音 as a technique name** (it appears in Japanese only in the sense "semitone"; the real vocabulary is 半開/四半開/4分の3開/4分の1開/かざし). The skill flags these rather than glossing them. And **nine years of Kakizakai's columns contain zero instances of 大ユリ, ネヤシ, オトシ, カラカラ, ケシ or 込み吹き** — which is itself the finding.
- **There were no shakuhachi *ryū* in the Edo period at all.** The iemoto licensing economy is Meiji and later. Kinko's rank vocabulary is attested; **Tozan's eight named grades** are 初伝・中伝（→竹号）・奥伝・皆伝 ｜ 指南・准師範（検定試験, 開軒）・師範（≥3 years later, →山号）・大師範 (promotion, not an exam). But **the "Kinko licensing system" does not exist as a unified system** — the school has no single body and each 会派 runs its own.
- ***Ma* is not the "pregnant silence" of the English literature.* In Heibonsha it is the **ordinary 邦楽 word for rhythm, metre and tempo**, synonym **間拍子** — which is also the name of Kinko's rhythm dots; **表間 and 裏間** are beats 1 and 2 of a bar. The temporal-interval sense is one sense among three.
- **The Geidai shakuhachi department was founded only in 1977**, with **Yamaguchi Gorō (Kinko)** as first lecturer and **Yamamoto Hōzan (Tozan)** later joining — one professor from each of the two schools. (Yokoyama Katsuya's own professorship was **Tokyo College of Music** (名誉教授), not Geidai; see [[shakuhachi-dokyoku]].)
- **The biggest modern pedagogical change was state policy.** From the **1998** 学習指導要領 revision, **effective 1 April 2002**, a 和楽器 became **compulsory in Japanese compulsory education** (中学校第2章第5節 第3の2(4), 「3学年間を通じて1種類以上の楽器を用いること」). It names no specific instrument.
