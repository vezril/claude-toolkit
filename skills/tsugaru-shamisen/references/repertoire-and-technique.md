One methodological warning that recurs below. **Almost the entire popular history of this tradition
descends from one man's reconstruction**: Daijō Kazuo (大條和雄, b. 1928), a Hirosaki writer, not an
academic. Japanese Wikipedia's own 津軽三味線 article states it bluntly: Daijō was essentially the
first to collect and write down the oral traditions, and current understanding of the history "owes
much to his research results" — *but* "it too is no more than a reconstruction of oral tradition,
and because it contains a great deal of fictional element, its value as a historical source is low"
(「それもまたあくまで伝承を再構築したものに過ぎず、多分にフィクション的要素も含まれているため、

## 4. The repertoire

### The canon is startlingly small — quantify it first

**[V]** McGoldrick surveyed **66 albums / 860 tracks** and found **only 32 distinct pieces**. The
frequency distribution:

- **Tsugaru jongara bushi — 190 appearances, almost one quarter of all tracks on its own.**
- Tsugaru yosare bushi — 93
- Tsugaru aiya bushi — 75
- **generic "kyokubiki" — 75**
- Tsugaru ohara bushi — 58
- Tsugaru sansagari — 48

**Those six account for over 60% of all tracks.** Second tier (15–38 appearances each): *Yasaburō
bushi* (38), *Tsugaru ondo* (37), *Tosa no sunayama* (37), *Ajigasawa jinku* (36), *Tsugaru jinku*
(25), *Ringo bushi* (22), *Tsugaru tanto bushi* (18), *Kuroishi yosare bushi* (17), *Tsugaru waiha
bushi* (15), *Tsugaru shiogama jinku* (15). A third tier of fifteen pieces appears ten times or
fewer, **eight of them only once.** *McGoldrick 2017, 66–67.*

His conclusion: "To an outsider, this body of recordings is likely to appear extremely repetitive…
but players are also expected to present new versions (improvisations and/or compositions) of the
standard repertoire… **Fans of the music are interested to hear how a particular player interprets a
piece at a particular time, and it is this fact that accounts for the over-representation of the
most popular pieces.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 67.* **The repertoire is not a body of pieces; it is a
small set of occasions for interpretation.**

### The canon-forming terms — use the Japanese

**[V]** **津軽三ツ物 / 津軽三つ物 (Tsugaru mitsumono), "the three Tsugaru things" = 津軽じょんから節
Jongara-bushi, 津軽よされ節 Yosare-bushi, 津軽小原節 Ohara-bushi.**
**五大民謡 / 五ツ物 (itsutsumono), "the five" = those three plus 津軽あいや節 Aiya-bushi and
津軽三下り Tsugaru sansagari.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線; en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen (whose
repertoire table derives from Groemer 1999); Nipponica Kotobank, bibliographic pass.*

**[V — a correction to watch for]** ja.wikipedia adds: "**Occasionally there are schools that
include 津軽あいや節 or 津軽音頭 among the 'three great folk songs,' but from a historical and general
standpoint this is incorrect.**" *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

**[V — and one of the user's two titles is the same piece]** **津軽小原節 and 津軽おはら節 are the
same song.** 小原 is read *ohara*; ja.wikipedia's article is titled 津軽小原節（つがるおはらぶし）
and the Jongara article links it as 「津軽小原節|津軽おはら節」. It is also written **おわら節**.
**Do not list them as two pieces.**

### じょんから節 Jongara-bushi — the central piece

**[V — pronunciation]** **じょんから節** is also pronounced and written **じょんがら節 (jongara)**
depending on the locality. *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節.*

**[V — ancestry]** "Tsugaru Jongara-bushi takes as its parent song the **Hokuriku 「新保広大寺くずし」
(Shinbo Kōdaiji kuzushi)**, which was much sung by **goze and zatō**, and developed after entering
Tsugaru." *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing Machida Kashō & Asano Ken'ji, 『日本民謡集』(Iwanami
Bunko, 1960), 29–30, and Nakai Kōjirō, Maruyama Shinobu & Mikuma Haruo, 『日本民謡辞典』(Tōkyōdō
Shuppan, 1972), 67–68.* **The piece is a descendant of the Echigo kudoki repertoire carried by blind
itinerants — which is the defensible version of the "goze origins" claim.**

**[V — form]** It is a **kudoki-bushi in 七七調** (7-7 syllabic metre), with many newly composed
verse sets. *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing Asano 1966, 131–132 and Nakai et al. 1972, 224–225.*
Representative verses (from 『日本民謡史』, Mainichi Shimbunsha, 1979) open
**「ハァー お国自慢の じょんから節よ／若衆唄えば 主人の囃し／娘踊れば 稲穂も踊る（ソリャ ハイサ
ハイヨー）」** — "the Jongara-bushi our province boasts of; when the young men sing, the master
joins the shouts; when the girls dance, the rice-ears dance too."

#### The three (really four) forms, and what distinguishes them

**[V]** "The character of the tune played on the tsugaru-shamisen has changed several times, and the
tune of today's Jongara-bushi has departed from the old 'Jongara kudoki'." The periodisation:

| Form | Period | What it is |
|---|---|---|
| **旧節 kyūbushi** ("old") | **before the shin-min'yō vogue, i.e. before about 1887 (Meiji 20)** | The kudoki-derived form; the shamisen serves the song's structure. Improvisation already present — audible on Shirakawa's **prewar recording of the kyūbushi**. |
| **中節 chūbushi** ("middle") | **up to the early Shōwa period** | Uses **三拍子の旋律的装飾音** — melodic ornaments in a triple-beat grouping. Prelude still respects the song's structure. |
| **新節 shinbushi** ("new") | **after that, especially the postwar vogue** | The chūbushi's triple-metre melodic ornaments were **discarded**; the playing changed to **strongly striking the strings**; and the **prelude section, which in kyūbushi and chūbushi had respected the song's structure, became the focus.** |
| **新旧節 shin-kyūbushi** ("new-old") | current (the article writes as of 2021) | Returns to a kyūbushi-like *maebiki* and folk-song accompaniment while still building excitement. |

*Source: ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing Nakai et al. 1972, 224–225; Machida & Asano 1960,
29–30; Kyōiku Geijutsusha, 「郷土の音楽: 青森県」; and — for the musical substance of the kyūbushi/
chūbushi/shinbushi distinction — **Gerald Groemer (グローマー ジェラルド), 「津軽三味線における即興
演奏的要素の分析」, 東洋音楽研究 (Tōyō Ongaku Kenkyū) 57 (31 August 1993): 41–61, at pp. 42 and 46.**
**[V as a report of Groemer 1993; U as to his exact wording — I could not obtain the article.]***

**[V]** English Wikipedia's Groemer-derived table lists **shin bushi, naka bushi, kyū bushi and
shin-kyū bushi** for Jongara, **shin/kyū** for both Yosare and Ohara, and no sub-forms for Aiya or
Sansagari. *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen.*

**[V — why this matters structurally, in Groemer's framing as relayed by en.wikipedia]** "One of the
most interesting characteristics of the Tsugaru-shamisen repertoire is what might be termed its
**cumulative** nature… **newer versions of songs tend to coexist alongside older versions, rather
than replacing them**… it remains certain that the 'old,' 'middle,' and 'new' versions are
differentiated **not merely stylistically but also historically. Their structural differences
contain, as it were, a congealed history.**" *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, "Discussion of the
repertoire" — a section written from Groemer 1999 and citing Takahashi 1976 and Hughes 1985
inline; the phrasing is almost certainly Groemer's or a close paraphrase of it.* **[V as text read;
U as to whether these are Groemer's exact words.]**

**[V — still live]** All three forms were recorded together on **Agatsuma Hiromitsu's *TSUGARU*
(Nippon Columbia COCB-54296, March 2020)**. *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing the Columbia
discography page.*

#### The Kuroishi origin legend — labelled as legend by the source itself

**[V]** A 「じょんから節発祥伝説」 — an **origin *legend*** — is transmitted in **Kuroishi City**
(the former Asaishi village), Aomori. In **1597 (Keichō 2)** **Sendoku Masauji**, lord of Asaishi
castle, was killed by **Ōura (Tsugaru) Tamenobu**; Tamenobu would not stop at that and tried to dig
up the Sendoku family graves. **Jōen (常縁)**, a monk of the family temple Jinsōji, protested, was
driven out, and **threw himself into the Asaishi river**. The riverbank where he drowned was called
**Jōen-gawara (常縁河原)**, which became **Kamikawara (上河原)**; the legend holds that the kudoki
song **「上河原節」(Kamikawara-bushi)** made from Jōen's story became **「じょんから節」**. Kuroishi
City holds that its kudoki 「上川原口説き」/「黒石じょんから」 is Jongara-bushi's parent song, and
**a stone monument reading "birthplace of Tsugaru Jongara-bushi" has been erected there by local
folk-song people.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing Nakai et al. 1972, 224–225, the Kuroishi
City website and the Kuroishi Tourist Association.*

**This is the second rival origin claim with a stone monument on it** (Kanagi has Nitabō's). Note
that Kuroishi also hosts its own national competition since 2012 (§7 of `institutions-and-modern-era.md`), with a dedicated
Jongara-bushi division. **Origin claims in this tradition are tourism infrastructure.** Label the
Kuroishi story as legend — ja.wikipedia does, in the word 伝説.

### よされ節 Yosare-bushi

**[V]** 「よされ」 is a *hayashi-kotoba* (vocalised refrain word). **Tsugaru Yosare-bushi** is one of
the *mitsumono*; relatives include **Kuroishi Yosare-bushi** and **Nanbu Yoshare-bushi**. Lively, in
performance with tsugaru-shamisen and taiko. **Origin: the first half of the 19th century**, from a
popular song of western Japan transmitted to and spread through Tōhoku.
*Source: ja.wikipedia よされ節 — **this article carries a 出典の明記 (needs sources) tag, so treat
the content as [U]**.*

**[V as a report of the article's content; the etymologies are themselves contested]** The meaning of
よされ is disputed, with these candidates, of which the article says the first is **considered the
most likely** and notes it is sometimes actually written 世去れ:
- **世去れ** "begone, world" — i.e. let this world of poverty and crop failure depart.
- "よしなさい" — "stop it / give it up."
- **余去れ** "I depart, the rest is up to you," or (at a banquet) "those left over, leave."
- **寄され** — a call to gather.
- **夜さり** "around nightfall / night"; one view is that the western Japanese song had 夜さり and
  the 世去れ sense was grafted on after it reached Tōhoku; another derives it from the imperative of
  the archaic 夜さる.

The article also reports the view that, because **mouth-reduction (口減らし) was practised in Tōhoku
in years of crop failure**, よされ meant "let people depart from the world" or "let the surplus
people leave"; and the view that it is onomatopoeic/mimetic, for the shamisen's sound, for weeping,
or for falling snow, with the bitter cold saying "begone from the world." A reconciling view: in
hard times 世去れ, in peacetime "put down the work and enjoy the party." *ja.wikipedia よされ節.*
**Flagged [U] as etymology, but worth including: this is how the tradition narrates its own
relationship to famine, and it should be reported as narration, not as philology.**

**[V]** Agatsuma's choice of Yosare-bushi over Jongara-bushi at Hirosaki (§7 of `institutions-and-modern-era.md`) rests on its being
**plainer than Jongara but reckoned the hardest piece of the five**. *ja.wikipedia 上妻宏光 — article
tagged BLP unsourced, so [U].*

### あいや節 Aiya-bushi, and the Haiya-bushi / kitamaebune transmission claim

**The transmission claim is VERIFIED, with one qualification about the vector.**

**[V]** **ハイヤ節 (Haiya-bushi)**, also 田助節 / 田助ハイヤ節, is a **sake-drinking song of the
Tasuke harbour area of Hirado City, Nagasaki Prefecture.** Tasuke harbour, on Hirado island,
"formerly prospered with ships waiting for wind and tide, and **had many pleasure quarters catering
to sailors**." The Haiya-bushi thought to have originated there **"was transmitted throughout the
country by the coming and going of ships, and propagated as a parlour song to the pleasure quarters
of ports everywhere."** Its date of origin is not certain, but **it is established that it existed
in the late Edo period** (Nagasaki Prefecture Cultural Affairs Division). The verse form is
**七七七五**, and **ハイヤエー derives from the shout given as a ship puts out.**
*Source: ja.wikipedia ハイヤ節, citing Nakai, Maruyama & Mikuma, 『日本民謡辞典』 (Tōkyōdō Shuppan,
1972), 264–265 and 64; Machida & Asano, 『日本民謡集』 (Iwanami, 1960), 349–350; and the Nagasaki
Prefecture cultural-properties database. **Nagasaki Prefecture designated 田助ハイヤ節 an intangible
folk cultural property on 22 February 2008.***

**[V — the transmission chain, explicitly including Tsugaru]** The same dictionary traces the
Haiya-bushi family **up the Japan Sea coast**: Kyoto's **宮津アイヤエ踊**, Niigata's **佐渡おけさ**
and **ハンヤ節**, Yamagata's **庄内ハエヤ節**, and **in Aomori 「津軽アイヤ節」** — "these are of the
Haiya-bushi line." On the Pacific side its influence shows in Iwate's **南部アイヤ節** and Miyagi's
**アイヤ節** and **塩釜甚句**. "Most of these are **sawagi-uta (rowdy songs) sung at drinking
parties**." Within Kyushu: Kumamoto's **牛深ハイヤ節**, Kagoshima's **阿久根ハンヤ節**, Fukuoka's
**小倉ハイヤ節**, Shimabara's **島原ハイヤ節**. *ja.wikipedia ハイヤ節, citing 日本民謡辞典 1972,
264–265.*

**[V — the qualification]** **The dictionary's stated vector is "the coming and going of ships" and
"sea routes" (海路) generally — it does not, in what I read, name the kitamaebune specifically.**
The ja.wikipedia ハイヤ節 article lists **北前船** only under 関連項目 (related topics). The
kitamaebune attribution appears in ja.wikipedia's main 津軽三味線 article as an **unsourced**
sentence: 「北前船によって日本海側各地の音楽が津軽に伝わり、津軽民謡は独特の発達をみる」 — "music
from the various places along the Japan Sea coast was transmitted to Tsugaru **by the kitamaebune**,
and Tsugaru folk song underwent its distinctive development." *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*
**So: the Kyushu→Tsugaru transmission of the Haiya/Aiya family is documented in the standard
Japanese folk-song dictionary; the naming of the kitamaebune as the specific carrier is the usual
and highly plausible gloss but is, in the sources I reached, unsourced. Say "coastal shipping,
conventionally the kitamaebune."** **[V for the transmission; U for the named vector.]**

**[V — Aiya is the odd one out musically]** Of the five great songs, **Aiya-bushi is the only one
Kinoshita labels "major," and the only one whose scale is the ritsu rather than the min'yō
pentatonic** (§6) — consistent with its being an import from a different regional tradition rather
than a native Tsugaru kudoki. *Derived from McGoldrick 2005, 89 / Kinoshita 2004 via McGoldrick
2017, 20, combined with the Japan Arts Council scale definitions. My inference from two verified
sources — flagged as such.* **[V on the data; the inference is mine.]**

**[V]** A 1918 recording exists of **「蝶々あいや節」(Kochō Aiya-bushi)** — see §9.

### 津軽小原節 / 津軽おはら節 Tsugaru Ohara-bushi

**[V]** A **sakamori-uta (酒盛り唄), a drinking song**, sung in the Tsugaru region; one of the
*mitsumono*; also called **おわら節**. The derivation, which is unexpectedly convoluted and
**connects Ohara-bushi to the Haiya family too**:

Near **Hachinohe**, a song called **「塩釜」** was sung, descended from Miyagi's **塩釜甚句**, which
in turn **takes Haiya-bushi as its ancestor**. This "Shiogama" passed into Tsugaru and came to be
sung as **塩釜甚句 / 津軽塩釜甚句**. Because the *hayashi-kotoba* contained **オワラ**, it also came
to be called **おわら節**. This owara-bushi had been sung in the short **七七七五** form, but as it
was sung at banquets **the lyrics were drawn out and it shifted to a kudoki character**, and
**between Taishō and the early Shōwa period the tune of Tsugaru Ohara-bushi as transmitted today was
established.**
*Source: ja.wikipedia 津軽小原節, citing Nakai et al. 1972, 223 and 168; Machida & Asano 1960, 28;
and NHK Archives 「動画で見るニッポンみちしる: 津軽小原節」.*
*(The article carries a footnoted variant: an alternative account of the Hachinohe Shiogama-jinku
makes it a salt-making work song from Asamushi, Nouchi and Zaruishi near present-day Aomori City.
**[U]**)*

**[V — the name is a 20th-century coinage by a named person]** Although "Ohara-bushi"/"Owara-bushi"
also names **鹿児島おはら節** and **越中おわら節**, **"Tsugaru Ohara-bushi" was newly named as such
by the folk singer 成田雲竹 (Narita Unchiku), after the modern tune with its repeating 七五 had
formed.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽小原節, citing Nakai et al. 1972, 223.* **Akita's 秋田おはら節 takes as
its parent song the Tsugaru owara-bushi as sung from Meiji into Taishō.** *ibid., citing the Akita
Prefecture Folk Song Association and Daisen City.*

**Note the implication:** two of the three *mitsumono* are named by, or substantially shaped by,
**Narita Unchiku** — the singer who also gave Chikuzan his name and commissioned his accompaniments.
**The "three great Tsugaru folk songs" are to a real degree a mid-20th-century curatorial act.**

### 津軽音頭 Tsugaru ondo

**[V]** Classed by Groemer's repertoire table under **D. 旧民謡 "old folk songs"** — a group that,
"though no less traditional than" the *mitsumono* and *itsutsumono*, "**were not, it seems, a major
part of the bosama repertoire**. Instead, they tended to be sung by non-professionals, generally
**without shamisen accompaniment**. But these songs began to receive renewed attention with the
Tsugaru-shamisen 'boom' after World War II. **Shamisen accompaniments were composed or arranged by
such performers as Takahashi Chikuzan** (Takahashi 1976:142). … More recently, solo shamisen versions
of the songs of Group D have been arranged." *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, Groemer-derived.*
**[V as text read; the citation to Takahashi 1976, 142 is in the source.]**

**[V]** It is nonetheless **one of the most-recorded pieces in the tradition: 37 appearances across
McGoldrick's 66-album survey**, tied with *Tosa no sunayama* and behind only the big six and
*Yasaburō bushi*. On Chikuzan's own 25 albums it appears **17 times**. *McGoldrick 2017, 66, 68.*
It is also one of the four pieces King paired side-by-side in 1979 and 1993 recordings on *Tsugaru
no hibiki* to show how Chikuzan's playing changed with age. *McGoldrick 2017, 70.*
**[V]** ja.wikipedia notes that some schools wrongly include 津軽音頭 among the "three great folk
songs." *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

### 津軽三下り Tsugaru sansagari

**[V]** The fifth of the *itsutsumono*; **named for its tuning** (§6), which it alone among the five
uses. **48 appearances in McGoldrick's survey.** It is the subject of **the earliest known Tsugaru
min'yō recording of any kind (1918, §9 of `institutions-and-modern-era.md`)** and of **one of only thirteen surviving Shirakawa tracks.**
*McGoldrick 2017, 66, 75–76.* **[V]** At Hirosaki, **Kasai Yoshiyuki won with a sansagari piece** —
noted as remarkable precisely because almost nobody competes with anything but Jongara.
*ja.wikipedia 上妻宏光 — [U], article unsourced.*

### 十三の砂山 Jūsan no sunayama — and a romanisation trap

**[V — the reading]** **This piece is usually romanised "Tosa no sunayama" in the English
literature, including by Groemer and McGoldrick.** The reason: 十三 is read **とさ (Tosa)** in the old
local usage — the medieval port on the lake is **十三湊 Tosaminato** — while the lake itself is now
read **十三湖 Jūsan-ko**. *Source: ja.wikipedia 十三湊 (「とさみなと」) and 十三湖 (「じゅうさんこ」),
both read and translated for this file.* **"Jūsan no sunayama" and "Tosa no sunayama" are the same song; search both.**

**[V — what it is]** A **bon-odori song (盆歌) transmitted in the Jūsan district**, with an
accompanying **bon festival**, in the former **Shiura village** on the west side of **Lake Jūsan**,
Tsugaru peninsula — now **Goshogawara City** (Shiura was absorbed in 2005). The 十三の砂山まつり
bon dance is listed among the area's festivals, and a 道の駅 on the Jūsan-ko heights advertises it.
*Source: ja.wikipedia 市浦村 (「十三の砂山（十三地区に伝承する盆歌および盆祭り）」) and 十三湖.*

### 六段 Rokudan and 三段 Sandan — and the koto *danmono* question

**I could not verify any derivation of the Tsugaru pieces from the koto *danmono*, and the evidence
I did find points against it.**

**[V — what Tsugaru "Rokudan" actually is]** **An ensemble piece (合奏曲).** McGoldrick, describing
the Osaka competition's categories, defines group B as "playing '**rokudan**,' **the basic,
group-friendly standardized composition based on 'Tsugaru jonkara bushi**'." At Osaka there is a
**「六段大合奏」 mass group performance of rokudan, which anyone who has a shamisen is invited to
join**, for a 1,000-yen fee; it "adds a participatory dimension to the event that other contests do
not have," letting those not ready to compete take the stage "with the assurance that any mistakes
they might make will be covered up by the sheer [number of players]." *McGoldrick 2017, 146, 152–153.*
ja.wikipedia independently confirms it as the diagnostic ensemble piece: a "school" (流派) in
tsugaru-shamisen means **that your ensemble phrasing matches**, "and this appears most clearly in the
phrases of **the ensemble piece Rokudan**." *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* ja.wikipedia also says the
**Oyama school is said to have originated tsugaru-shamisen ensemble playing (合奏)**.
*ja.wikipedia 小山流 — [U], tagged for sources.*

**[V — what the koto Rokudan is, for contrast]** **『六段の調』(Rokudan no shirabe)** is a
**danmono (段物)** of the **koto** repertoire and the representative piece of that category,
traditionally attributed to **Yatsuhashi Kengyō (1614–1685)**, the founder of modern koto music;
tuning **hirajōshi**; **six sections (dan) of 52 *byōshi* / 104 beats each (the first dan 54
byōshi)**; a purely instrumental piece without song, unlike the *kumiuta* and most *jiuta*. It was
**later transferred to the 三絃 (shamisen)** with *kaede* second parts, and to kokyū and shakuhachi,
and is played in *sankyoku* ensemble. Companion danmono: 『八段の調』 and 『乱輪舌』(みだれ/十段調).
*Source: ja.wikipedia 六段の調.*

**My assessment, stated as assessment:** the two share the word **段 (dan, "step/section")** and the
practice of counting sections, and McGoldrick notes that the sections of a **kyokubiki** "[are]
sometimes referred to as **dan**, or steps" *(McGoldrick 2017, 21)*. That is a plausible route for
the naming. But the Tsugaru Rokudan is **a standardised ensemble arrangement of Jongara-bushi**, not
a transmission of Yatsuhashi's piece, and **no source I read connects them.** The koto Rokudan *was*
historically transferred to shamisen — but to the *sankyoku* **sangen**, a different instrument and
world from the futozao. **Treat "Tsugaru Rokudan derives from the koto danmono" as unverified and
probably a folk etymology; what is verifiable is the shared sectional-counting vocabulary.** **[U]**

**[U] On 三段 "Sandan": I could not verify a Tsugaru piece by this name at all.** It does not appear
in McGoldrick's 32-piece survey, in his appendices as sampled, in ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, or in any
competition category list I read. ja.wikipedia 六段 is a redirect to 六段の調. **Either it is a
school-specific ensemble arrangement (the obvious inference by analogy with Rokudan) or the name has
been imported into English writing from the koto danmono tradition. I could not confirm it exists.
Do not assert it.**

### 曲弾き kyokubiki vs 曲付け/唄付け kyokuzuke — and why the distinction organises everything

**[V — the terms as actually used]** The functional pair is:

- **曲弾き kyokubiki** — "playing a piece": **solo instrumental playing, particularly improvisatory
  playing.** *McGoldrick 2017, 20.* English Wikipedia's Groemer-derived table defines group F,
  *kyokubiki*, as "shamisen solos and improvisation: potentially all songs of [the three, the five,
  the old and the new folk songs] (most commonly [the three]), **as well as free improvisation,
  freely entitled by performers**" — and names two examples: **『岩木』 *Iwaki* (Takahashi Chikuzan)**
  and **『嵐』 *Arashi* (Yamada Chisato)**. *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen.*
- **唄付け utazuke** (also 唄づけ; the term 曲付け *kyokuzuke* names the same function under a
  different label) — **accompanying a singer.**

**[V — the usage note that matters]** ja.wikipedia's main 津軽三味線 article uses **唄づけ**
throughout and does **not** use the word 曲弾き at all; 曲弾き appears instead in ja.wikipedia
津軽じょんから節 and in the Shirakawa biographical literature, where **Shirakawa is credited with
establishing kyokubiki.** *Checked directly against the wikitext of both articles.* It is however
**the live, official term in the competition world**: the Hirosaki World Championship's own rules
state that the individual pieces are **曲弾き** based on Tsugaru folk music, and that entries with
singing are separated out as **唄付け**; the Kuroishi competition's Jongara-bushi section has
「唄の部」「踊りの部」「三味線の部」 with the shamisen division contested as **kyokubiki**.
*`tsugaru-shamisen.jp/about/`; ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing the Kuroishi Tourist Association
and the World Championship committee.* **So: kyokubiki is the *competition* category and the
Shirakawa-literature term; utazuke is the older function. Don't present kyokubiki as the genre's own
ancient category.**

**[V — why the distinction organises the tradition, in four consequences]**

1. **It is the historical axis.** The genre's whole development is the migration of weight from
   *utazuke* to *kyokubiki*: Shirakawa's lengthening of the sung piece's **前弾き (maebiki)** prelude
   into something that could stand alone *(Matsuki 2011, 141)*; the shinbushi's reorganisation of
   Jongara so that **"the prelude section, which in kyūbushi and chūbushi had respected the song's
   structure, became the focus"** *(Groemer 1993, 46)*; and ja.wikipedia's summary that the music was
   "originally merely an accompanying instrument, played at the side of the stage out of the
   audience's sight," whose *maebiki* "became independent as a solo."
2. **It is the harder skill, and it is dying.** ja.wikipedia: performance of the five great songs is
   "basically all done improvised. **Not only in solos and preludes but also in song accompaniment
   (唄づけ), the player must play in response to the singer's improvisation, so utazuke demands a
   high level of knowledge and technique.** In recent years players who cannot do utazuke have
   increased, and there are many voices lamenting this." Causes given: not only players' own
   preferences but **a shortage of singers** and **the closed environment of the shamisen world**.
   *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線* (restated twice in the article, including in the history section:
   「即興での伴奏（唄づけ）が出来ない奏者も多くなってきており、これを憂う声も多く聞かれる」).
3. **The competitions have had to legislate it back in.** Chiba: "**Since the Tsugaru shamisen is
   originally an accompanying instrument, the skill of *utatsuke* (accompanying the singer) has also
   become more important, and an increasing number of competitions now judge both kyokubiki and
   utatsuke performances.**" *Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 6.* Hirosaki now runs
   **唄付伴奏A級 and B級** divisions with **set pieces** (A: 津軽じょんから節 新旧節; B: 津軽じょんから
   新節) *(`tsugaru-shamisen.jp/about/`)*; Kanagi requires its A-level entrants to prepare utazuke;
   Aomori requires **both kyokubiki and utazuke versions of all five great songs** from A-level
   competitors — in effect ten pieces. *McGoldrick 2017, 146, 171, 139.*
4. **It splits the field sociologically.** The "standard Tsugaru" players and the hybrid/crossover
   players divide, among other things, on whether they can still do *utazuke* — which is why
   Kinoshita's warning that the instrument's drift to solo-only is "dangerous" lands as it does.

**[V — the full repertoire taxonomy, for reference]** Groemer's seven-group scheme as reproduced on
en.wikipedia, compiled "from interviews with noted performers such as Takahashi Chikuzan and Yamada
Chisato, as well as recordings issued by past stars":
**A. クドキ節 kudoki-bushi (quasi-narrative songs)** — *Suzuki Mondo*, *Jonkara kudoki*, both now
rarely played, though "once the mainstay of the repertoire of itinerant, often blind,
musician-beggars known as *bosama*"; "at the start of the 20th century, these *kudoki* were gradually
displaced by shorter non-narrative songs."
**B. 津軽三ツ物** (with sub-forms). **C. 津軽五ツ物.**
**D. 旧民謡** — *Yasaburō bushi, Tsugaru jinku, Dodarebachi, Ajigasawa jinku, Tosa no sunayama,
Tsugaru ondo, Torajōsama, Tanto bushi*, others.
**E. 新民謡 shin-min'yō** — ***Waiha bushi*, composed 1932 by Narita Unchiku (1888–1974);
*Ringo bushi*, composed by Narita Unchiku in 1954**; others.
**F. 曲弾き kyokubiki.** **G. 合奏 gassō** (also 曲合わせ *kyoku-awase*) — "ensemble playing of
multiple shamisen, occasionally supplemented by other instruments such as percussion, taishō-goto,
koto, etc. Most often versions of Jonkara bushi are used."
And the key sentence: **"Most of the titles given below exist in two versions: in song form (a vocal
line with shamisen and taiko drum accompaniment) and as a solo shamisen piece."**
*Source: en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, wikitext read and translated for this file; the article's inline citations run

## 5. Technique specific to Tsugaru

### First, the framing correction that matters most

**[V]** ja.wikipedia divides performance practice in two: **叩き三味線 (tataki-shamisen)**, played by
striking boldly, and **弾き三味線 (hiki-shamisen)**, the delicate style **descending from Takahashi
Chikuzan**. And then: **「現代の多くの津軽三味線は、昔の名人といわれる白川軍八郎、木田林松栄、
福士政勝など、叩き系の流れを汲むものがほとんどで、弾き系の流れを汲むものはごく僅かである」** — most
tsugaru-shamisen today descends from the **tataki** line of Shirakawa, Kida and Fukushi Masakatsu,
and **those descending from the hiki line are very few indeed.** Of the hiki line, **only the
竹山流 (Chikuzan-ryū) is nationally recognised.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

**So the player the English-speaking world knows best represents the MINORITY technique.** Any
account that treats Chikuzan as the archetype of tsugaru-jamisen playing has the field backwards.
(And recall §3 of `tradition-and-lineage.md`: the "hiki" label may itself rest on a misheard interview.) **[V]**

### 叩き tataki — the percussive attack

**[V — what it is]** The genre's defining sound: **「撥を叩きつけるように弾く打楽器的奏法」** — a
percussive technique in which **the plectrum is struck down onto the instrument** — paired with
**fast tempi and a high note-density.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, opening sentence.* English
Wikipedia: "easily recognizable by its percussive quality (**the plectrum striking the instrument's
body on each stroke**)." *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, citing Dodd & Richmond 2011 and Miki 2008, 88.*
Chiba: "**With this instrument, the playing style changed to striking, and the shamisen came to be
treated as a percussion instrument as well as a string instrument.**" *Chiba & Savage 2023, p. 3.*

**[V — the crucial functional point, Kotobank, bibliographic pass's reading of the Kotobank/Nipponica entry,
2026-11-13; not independently opened by me]** The dictionaries describe tataki as **striking the
一の糸 (first/lowest string) with a thick heavy plectrum 「力いっぱいたたいて」 ("striking with all
one's strength") TO SET THE RHYTHM**, with **スクイバチ (sukui-bachi, an upward scooping stroke)** on
the **三の糸** as ornament. **Tataki is therefore the rhythm-generating function assigned to a
specific string, not a decorative flourish** — which is exactly why the first string is the one that
had to get thicker (below). **[V as a report of the entry.]**

**[V — the historical cause, in the players' own words]** ja.wikipedia, drawing on Chikuzan's book:
"According to Takahashi Chikuzan's writings, whenever there was a festival the *bosama* would line up
in rows in the shrine precincts, playing as if **competing against one another in skill**. In such an
environment, those called the virtuosos of the founding period of tsugaru-shamisen came to pursue
**bigger sound and flashier technique in order to stand out from the other bosama.** The shamisen,
which until then had used the same *chūzao* or *hosozao* as the goze, **became *futozao*; the
plectrum became a small one suited to fast playing**; and the music too changed into something
complex and high-tempo, using in places the percussive technique called 'tataki'. Chikuzan himself
said: **「三味線なんて音が大きく出ればそれで良かった。音が大きいから太棹を選んだんだ」** — 'with the
shamisen, as long as it put out a big sound, that was enough. I chose the futozao because its sound
is big.'" *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

**[V — the stage-conditions explanation, dated]** The same article, citing **松木宏泰,
『津軽三味線まんだら』(邦楽ジャーナル, 2011)** and Hokkaidō performer Matsumura Ichirō: **microphones
appeared only around Shōwa 29 (1954)**, and at first only in large theatres; **lighting was the
same, and many of the touring sheds were lit by candles and lamps.** "If the voice could not be
heard and the lighting was dark, abuse would fly from the audience. **The characteristics of Tsugaru
min'yō — singing in a voice fit to split, and adding a bold resonance on the first string — were
born of the poor stage conditions of that period.**" *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*
**This is the honest technical history: the style is a solution to being unamplified in a dark shed
in front of a hostile crowd.**

### 叩き撥 tataki-bachi — VERIFIED as a real term

**[V]** ja.wikipedia's article on **Yamada Chisato** describes his school's playing as marked, in
contrast to today's technique-first approach, by **「山田流独特の強い叩き撥と唸り(うなり)」** — "the
Yamada school's distinctive **strong *tataki-bachi*** and **growl (*unari*)**."
*ja.wikipedia 山田千里 — article tagged 出典の明記, so **[U]** as a characterisation, but **[V]** that
叩き撥 is current vocabulary.* **Note *unari* (唸り, "growl/snarl") as a companion term worth having:
it names the sympathetic buzz-and-roar the heavy attack produces.**

### ハジキ hajiki and スリ suri — VERIFIED as standard terms

**[V]** ja.wikipedia's 特殊奏法 article lists, among shamisen special techniques:
**ハタキ・ウチバチ** (striking the belly board together with the string), **スリ** — "**a technique
of rubbing the string, of which there are a lateral (横) and a longitudinal (縦) kind**" —
**押し込み** (a portamento technique), **ユリ** (ornamenting the resonance/decay), and **ハジキ** —
"**left-hand pizzicato**," with a further sense of striking the string with a left-hand finger above
the bridge. *ja.wikipedia 特殊奏法, read and translated for this file.*

- **ハジキ hajiki** = **left-hand plucking/pull-off** — sounding a note with the left hand alone,
  without the plectrum. Confirmed as a standard shamisen term; it is pervasive in tsugaru playing
  but is not unique to it.
- **スリ suri** = **sliding/rubbing the string.** The lateral/longitudinal distinction in the
  Japanese is the useful one: *longitudinal* rubbing along the string's length is the
  glissando/portamento family that English writing calls **すり上げ suriage** (slide up) and
  **すり下げ surisage** (slide down); *lateral* rubbing across the string is a separate noise effect.
  **[V for スリ and the two kinds; [U] for the specific terms すり上げ / すり下げ — I did not find them
  attested in any source I read, though 押し込み is attested as the portamento term. Use "suri" and
  describe the motion rather than asserting the compound terms.]**

### The documented right-hand variables — from the competitions' own criteria

**[V]** The clearest published account of what tsugaru right-hand technique *consists of* is the
**ten judging criteria printed in the Nagoya competition's programme**, which McGoldrick translates
and which he says "are certainly things that all judges consider important":

> **String tuning:** beyond just being in tune, **the *sawari* buzzing should be perfectly set up.**
> **Plectrum handling:** **contrast of quiet striking near the edge of the skin and loud striking
> near the bridge**, and other plectrum techniques.
> **Control of sound:** there should be no extraneous sounds being made, or accidental damping of
> strings.
> **Accuracy of pitches.**
> **Quiet playing:** including **reducing the sound by placing one's pinky on the bridge.** The sound
> should be clear, not muddy, and the technique should be used naturally in the course of the piece.
> **Volume (force):** the ability to create a consistent, solid sound.
> **Rhythm:** unnatural rhythms should not be used; strong and weak beats should not be confused.
> **Degree of difficulty:** successfully using difficult phrases or riffs.
> **Composition.**
> **Originality:** ingenuity of phrases.

*McGoldrick 2017, 123–124.* Three things here are specific and usable: **striking position along the
skin (edge = quiet, near the bridge = loud) as the primary dynamic control**; **the little finger on
the bridge as a damping/quietening device**; and **the sawari treated as a setup parameter that can
be judged.**

**[V]** Hirosaki's criteria, by contrast, are **not published anywhere** on the programme or website.
Daijō's own three from a newspaper column of the early 1980s were: "a high level of technique, with
no misses, in other words **a balance of difficulty with accuracy**; **mastery of timbre, from heavy
striking to light playing**; and **adding something new to the music**." A judge told McGoldrick the
real criteria were **initial tuning, precise intonation, and accuracy of the plectrum (not missing
strings or hitting others accidentally)**, scored by **starting each player at 100 points and
deducting — e.g. three points for missing a string, five for being out of tune from the start.**
*McGoldrick 2017, 123–124, citing Daijō 2000, 12–13.*

**[V]** And the non-technical criteria a head judge named aloud at the 2015 Hirosaki awards
ceremony: besides technique, **chikara-zuyoi (力強い, boldness/"muscularity"), takumashisa
(courage/robustness), soul, and loneliness** — with, as McGoldrick notes, no clear way these fit the
deduction-based scoring. *Sasamori Takefusa, 2015, via McGoldrick 2017, 124 and n.43.*

### The sawari

**[V]** The **sawari** — the buzzing mechanism under the first string at the nut, derived from the
biwa — is part of the instrument's basic Japanese form, "the addition of a **sawari buzzing
mechanism for the first string**" being one of the modifications made when itinerant biwa players
adopted the shamisen in the sixteenth century. *McGoldrick 2017, 14–15, citing Kikkawa 1997, 6.*
Its specific significance in tsugaru is that it is **an actively managed, judged parameter** (above),
and that the related 東さわり / 吾妻さわり (an adjustable sawari) is listed among the relevant
instrument parts. *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, 関連項目.* **[V]**

### Improvisation — how much is actually improvised. This is genuinely contested; here is the state of it

**Claim A — "it is all improvised."** **[V as a position held by a major source]**
ja.wikipedia: **「これらの演奏は、基本的にすべて即興で行う」** — performance of the five great songs
"is **basically all done improvised**," and this holds **not only for solos and preludes but also for
song accompaniment**, where the player must respond to the singer's improvisation. *ja.wikipedia
津軽三味線.* It repeats this: 「演奏は、多くが即興で行われる」 of Jongara-bushi specifically, adding
that **improvisation was abundant in all the forms, not just the modern ones — as can be confirmed
even in Shirakawa Gunpachirō's prewar recording of the *kyūbushi*.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節,
citing Groemer 1993, 42.* **That last point is important: improvisation is not a modern,
competition-era development.**

**Claim B — "it is a stock of memorised phrases rearranged."** **[V as the ethnographer's position]**
McGoldrick, on the competition repertoire: "Contest pieces are **kyokubiki, or newly-composed solo
instrumental works**, generally based on 'Tsugaru jonkara bushi'… **'Composition' here largely
involves arranging standard riffs within a fairly rigid structure, and is actually far less important
than the execution of the piece.** Speed, accuracy, tone, and the ability to execute and seamlessly
combine standard riffs and techniques are all among the criteria… so **there is very little room for
personal expression.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 123.* And on the phrase-stock's origin: the surviving
Shirakawa tracks "**are a sort of Rosetta Stone for Tsugaru shamisen, containing many phrases that
Shirakawa invented and are used today by every player as the building blocks of composition and
improvisation.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 76–77.*

**The reconciliation, which is the actual answer. [V — this is McGoldrick's own formulation, and it
is the best thing I found on the question.]**

> "First, there is a group of pieces… collectively known as '**the big five Tsugaru min'yō**' and
> [they] make up the core repertoire of traditional Tsugaru shamisen. **With the repertoire outside
> of the big five, the player is restricted to adding embellishments to a fixed melody, but these
> five have come to behave something like modes that allow for a great degree of improvisation.
> Similar to raga in India and maqam in West Asia, the big five are easily distinguished from each
> other by a particular combination of tuning, scale and meter, and by a collection of distinctive
> riffs and embellishments that have come to adhere to each of them. Many of these riffs and
> embellishments are borrowed from the songs on which they are based, and others were developed by
> players in the process of improvisation.** Tsugaru shamisen developed among musicians with little
> or no training in other musical systems, so **while they do not refer to these as modes, that is
> essentially what they are.**"
> — *McGoldrick 2017, 20–21.*

So: **the five great songs function as modes — tuning + scale + metre + an adhering stock of
*tegoto*/riffs — and performance is real-time construction within a mode out of that stock.** It is
neither free invention nor recitation. Two corroborating specifics:

- **No fixed melody.** Kinoshita: "**In the kyokubiki there is usually no melodic theme which is
  returned to… Of course, there are typical Tsugaru phrases, but basically we don't have a melody or
  a theme to fall back on.**" McGoldrick borrows Bonnie Wade's phrase for koto music, **"flowing
  ongoingness,"** in which "there is nothing like a tune." *Kinoshita 2003, 46 and 50, and Wade
  2005, 72, via McGoldrick 2017, 21.*
- **But a rigid form.** "Kyokubiki tend to be clearly divided into **sections of anywhere from one
  to two minutes** in length. **Each section starts on a low note, often the lowest note on the
  lowest-tuned string, then gradually moves to a very high note, typically the highest note on the
  highest-tuned string, before moving back down to the lowest note and striking all three strings in
  one of a few fixed cadence patterns.** An entire piece will consist of a number of these sections,
  sometimes referred to as **dan**, or steps, each of which accomplishes this journey from low to
  high and back again showcasing different riffs and techniques and dynamics to distinguish itself
  from the other sections." *McGoldrick 2017, 21.* **This is the single most useful formal
  description of a kyokubiki I found, and it is why competition pieces of 2:30 or 4:00 contain a
  determinate number of "dan."**

**[V — the scope of improvisation has narrowed, with a named cause]** Three mechanisms, all
documented: (i) **notation.** Oyama Mitsugu published the first tablature collection in **1977**;
"while there are some schools that still teach without this sort of aid, **today most students use
some written form**… **A further effect is that it sets the form of a piece; even today, some
teachers who forgo tablature seem incapable of playing a piece exactly the same way twice.**"
*McGoldrick 2017, 233.* (ii) **recordings** — Hill & Bithell's point that audio transmission
"can 'lead to a loss of variation, and a degree of standardization and homogenization'" —
"**This almost certainly happened with Tsugaru shamisen too, but was exacerbated by the widespread
use of tablature.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 233–234.* (iii) **competitions** (§7 of `institutions-and-modern-era.md`).
Chiba's inside view is the mirror image, and worth having because he is sympathetic to notation:
he himself "rarely used" the **文化譜 bunkafu** scores, learning instead from his teacher's and his
predecessors' recordings on vinyl, cassette and CD, "**including music and dialogue**," and
noticing "that the **ma 間, melody, and timbre of the same piece or the same technique differed
depending on the performer**" — but he reports that "**it is very difficult for newcomers to learn
the Tsugaru shamisen without notation. Even for experienced players, it can take several years to
master the tones and rhythms of their predecessors**," and that **more and more young licensed
(名取) players now actively use notation and post performance videos, which "has led to an increase
in the number of young people who are learning to play the instrument in non-traditional settings."**
*Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint pp. 5–6.* **The trade is explicit: notation costs variation and buys
accessibility, and the tradition has made that trade knowingly.**

### Metre, free-rhythm introductions, and *ma* (間)

**[V — metre]** "Unlike most other Japanese music, **some tsugaru-jamisen pieces are in triple
time**, though the three beats are not accentuated in the manner of Western music."
*en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, citing **Groemer 1999, 117 and 135** and Ogawa & Yoshitomi 1999, 125.*
McGoldrick is more precise and more sceptical of the Western labels. Of the metres in the mode table
(§6), which come from Kinoshita's book and "**reflect emic ideas of meter in the tablature used to
notate Tsugaru shamisen music, but do not accurately describe the meter in western terms**":

> "**Kyokubiki based on 'Tsugaru jonkara bushi' do not have a regular meter, though they tend to be
> notated in duple meter for convenience. They are more accurately described as *pulsatile*, with
> occasional sections of duple meter.** Similarly, '3/8' above actually refers to **groups of one
> long and two short beats of unequal lengths that create a distinctive three-beat rhythm.** This is
> not, strictly speaking, conventional triple meter, but it would be even less accurate to describe
> it as duple meter."
> — *McGoldrick 2017, 20–21.*

**That is the answer on free rhythm: a Jongara kyokubiki is not "a free introduction followed by a
pulse" — it is pulsatile throughout, with duple-metre passages emerging and dissolving.** The
*triple* feel belongs to Yosare, Ohara, Aiya and Sansagari, and is a long–short–short grouping of
unequal beats, not 3/8. **[V]**

**[V — *ma*]** Chiba gives the working definition used inside the tradition, in a footnote:
**"*ma* 間 [rhythm]: temporal interval created by the breathing of the folk song performer."**
*Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 5 and n.3.* **This is the precise and deflationary answer to the
*ma* question: in tsugaru-shamisen, *ma* is not a mystical category — it is the timing the shamisen
must take from the singer's breath.** Which is also why *ma* is the first casualty of a solo-only,
notation-taught, four-minute-competition practice: **the thing that defined *ma* was the singer, and
the singer has been removed.** *(That last inference is mine, from Chiba's definition plus the
documented decline of utazuke — flagged as inference.)*

**[V — and it is the carrier of individuality]** Chiba noticed, learning from recordings, that
**"the *ma*, melody, and timbre of the same piece or the same technique differed depending on the
performer."** *Chiba & Savage 2023, p. 5.* *Ma* is where the player is.

### Why Tsugaru needs a thicker skin, heavier strings and a smaller, lighter bachi than gidayū

**[V — the body is NOT bigger than a gidayū shamisen]** This is the detail most English accounts get
wrong. ja.wikipedia: **「三味線本体の寸法は義太夫の三味線とほぼ同じで」** — "**the dimensions of the
instrument itself are almost the same as the gidayū shamisen**." Neck wood: **花梨 (karin)** for
practice instruments, **紅木 (kōki)** for high-grade stage instruments, the latter harder, hence
better in tone and less prone to neck wear (**勘減り**). Normally **built in three detachable
sections**, sometimes with metal **金ホゾ** joinery at the joints. Body of **karin**; standard size
**five *bu*** with four-bu and six-bu variants but **no clear standard**; interior **綾杉 (ayasugi)**
cross-hatch carving marks a high-grade body, one without it being a **丸胴 (marudō)**. On the
acoustics of the prestige features, ja.wikipedia is bracingly blunt: **"neither the *kanahozo* nor
the *ayasugi* has any basis in acoustical engineering, but many players feel the tone differs."**
*ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **[V]** *(Nipponica says the body is "slightly
larger than a standard futozao," which is compatible: the comparison class there is the futozao
generally, not gidayū specifically. **[U] as to Nipponica's exact wording.)***

**[V — the strings, with numbers. This is the concrete answer.]** Gauges from Nipponica, recorded in the
agent from the Kotobank/Nipponica entry (2026-11-13; not independently opened by me):

| | 一の糸 (1st) | 二の糸 (2nd) | 三の糸 (3rd) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tsugaru** | **30** | **18** | **12** |
| **Nagauta** | 15 | 13 | 12 |

**The third string is identical; the second is modestly heavier; the first is DOUBLE.** That is
precisely consistent with the technique: tataki is defined as striking **the first string** with
full force to generate the rhythm, so the first string is the one that has to survive it and the one
whose fundamental has to carry a dark shed. It is also consistent with the Kida anecdote — his
reported innovation was pushing the **first string specifically** from 30 to 35 to 40.
**[V as a report of the entry; the convergence with the independently sourced Kida anecdote is
striking and raises my confidence.]**

**[V — the strings in practice]** ja.wikipedia: **silk is the proper material, but because the
playing technique breaks strings very easily, nylon or Tetron is often used for the second and third
strings, at a cost in tone.** The colour is **yellow**, a survival either of dyeing silk with
insect-repellent turmeric (*ukon*) or of dyeing with **egg yolk** to put a gloss on the sound.
*ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

**[V — the skin]** **Dog skin, and specifically the back.** Glued to the body with **starch paste**,
which makes the instrument **"extremely vulnerable to changes in humidity."** The finest grade is
reckoned to be **the female Akita dog**, but **most skin today is imported from across Asia.**
Synthetics are in circulation but "are inferior in tone and, since the price does not differ much,
are not favoured." *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* Chiba calls it "**tough dog skin**," one of the
features that "combine to produce a louder sound," alongside the larger body, thicker neck and
strings, and harder wood. *Chiba & Savage 2023, p. 3.* McGoldrick: **"heavy dogskin sound table."**
*McGoldrick 2017, 15.* **The causal chain is: strike the skin as well as the strings → the skin must
be thicker and tougher → and being starch-glued, it remains humidity-fragile anyway.**
**[Cross-reference:** Keisuke Yamada, "Shamisen skin on the verge of extinction,"
*Ethnomusicology Forum* 26/3 (2017): 373–396, DOI 10.1080/17411912.2018.1423575 — the scholarly
treatment of the dogskin supply crisis. **[U — identified, not read.]]**

**[V — the bachi, and the gidayū contrast]** The plectrum is **small, with a tortoiseshell
(鼈甲 *bekkō*) tip**; handles of plastic, ivory or water-buffalo horn; substitutes are circulating
because of the tortoiseshell shortage but are reckoned inferior in tone. *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*
The historical reason it is small is given in the passage quoted above: as players chased bigger
sound and flashier technique, **「撥は速弾きに適した小振りなものとなり」 — the plectrum became a small
one suited to fast playing.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* Chiba states the design brief exactly:
"**The plectrum for the strings is made of tortoiseshell, which is hard yet flexible to withstand
the power of the player's striking action, and it is small enough to be played delicately and
quickly.**" *Chiba & Savage 2023, p. 3.* McGoldrick: "**a sturdy plectrum with a relatively narrow
blade.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 15.*

**So the answer to "why smaller and lighter than gidayū" is a double constraint:** the stroke must be
**violent enough to drive a 30-gauge string and the skin** (hence hard, sturdy, tortoiseshell) and
**fast enough for a high note-density and the ornamental sukui-bachi on the third string** (hence
small, narrow-bladed, light). A gidayū bachi, built for a slower declamatory attack on a
comparably-sized instrument, satisfies the first constraint and not the second. **[V on the
components; the synthesis is mine.]**

**[V — two further instrument notes specific to tsugaru]** (i) **Electric shamisen are more actively
developed in tsugaru than in any other genre**, and **left-handed instruments are more used than in
other schools, because the music is predominantly solo** (an accompanist in an ensemble cannot sit
mirrored). (ii) Instruments are **made to order**, the craftsman's labour costing more than the
timber; ja.wikipedia gives a market range of roughly **¥200,000–400,000** for kōki/marudō/no-kanahozo,
**¥500,000–800,000** mid-grade, and **over ¥5,000,000** at the top, where "craft value usually
outweighs tonal value." Crucially: **because the neck wears from fingering and the body wears from
repeated reskinning, there is no vintage instrument market — no Stradivarius equivalent. "It is no
exaggeration to say that the much-talked-of 'instrument for life' does not exist: in tsugaru-shamisen
it is common sense that the shamisen is a consumable, to be replaced."** Full-time professionals wear
them out fastest and often **replace every few years**; the practical ceiling for a stage instrument
is said to be **¥2–3 million**. *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **[V]** Chiba names **"the rising cost of
musical instruments because of a shortage of resources and makers"** as one of the causes of
declining apprentice numbers. *Chiba & Savage 2023, p. 5.*

**[V — the left hand]** "They use only the **left index and ring finger** traditionally."
*en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, "Playing method."* **[V as text read; [U] as to source — the section
is uncited.]** Note this is a traditional restriction, not a current one.

## 6. Tunings in Tsugaru use

### The three tunings, with actual intervals

**Two independent sources, agreeing, with the intervals stated two different ways.**

**[V — ja.wikipedia, intervals measured from the first string]** "There are three tunings in
tsugaru-shamisen. **The distinguishing feature is that the tuning of the first string is changed to
match the height of the singer's voice.**"

| Tuning | 2nd string | 3rd string | Relative pitches | Pieces |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **二上り niagari** | a **5th** above the 1st | an **octave** above the 1st | **ド・ソ・ド (do–so–do)** | **津軽じょんから節, 津軽よされ節** etc. |
| **本調子 honchōshi** | a **4th** above the 1st | an **octave** above the 1st | **ド・ファ・ド (do–fa–do)** | **津軽小原節** etc. |
| **三下り sansagari** | a **4th** above the 1st | a **minor 7th** above the 1st | **ド・ファ・シ♭ (do–fa–ti♭)** | **津軽三下がり** etc. |

*Source: ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

**[V — McGoldrick, intervals measured between adjacent strings, with worked examples]**
"**honchōshi** ('base tuning'), with strings **a fourth and a fifth apart** (for example: **e–a–e′**);
**niagari** ('second-string raised'), with strings **a fifth and a fourth apart** (**e–b–e′**); and
**sansagari** ('third-string lowered') with strings **a fourth and a fourth apart** (**e–a–d′**).
While honchōshi is the most common tuning for shamisen used in various traditional art musics,
**niagari is by far the most common tuning for shamisen used in folk song.**"
*McGoldrick 2017, 19–20.*

**The two accounts are identical** — e→a is a 4th and a→d′ is a 4th, which makes e→d′ a minor 7th,
exactly ja.wikipedia's description of sansagari. Use whichever framing suits, but note the naming
logic only makes sense from honchōshi: **ni-agari = "the second [string] raised"** (a 4th → a 5th);
**san-sagari = "the third [string] lowered"** (an octave → a minor 7th).

### The tuning names are RELATIVE — and here is how absolute pitch is actually set

**[V]** ja.wikipedia states the principle outright: the first string is retuned **to match the
singer's vocal range**, and the three names describe the **interval pattern**, not pitches.
**「絶対音は尺八または篠笛を基準とする」 — absolute pitch is referenced to the shakuhachi or the
shinobue.** And then it gives the two regional reckonings, which are genuinely useful and almost
never reported in English:

- **In eastern Japan**, the shakuhachi's length in **尺 *shaku*** and **寸 *sun*** is used.
  **"2 尺" corresponds roughly to absolute C**; each **semitone up subtracts one *sun*** and each
  semitone down adds one. So **1 尺 9 寸 ≈ C♯** and **2 尺 1 寸 ≈ B**.
- **In western Japan**, the **本 *hon*** numbering of the **shinobue** used in nagauta hayashi is
  used instead. **"4 本" corresponds roughly to absolute C**; each semitone up adds one *hon*, each
  down subtracts one. So **5 本 ≈ C♯** and **3 本 ≈ B**.

*Source: ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **[V]** **This is the concrete answer to "how is a fretless,
non-fixed-pitch instrument pitched in practice": by naming a wind instrument's size.**

**[V]** McGoldrick adds the modern convergence: "**Traditionally, there is no fixed pitch to which
the shamisen is tuned, but contemporary Tsugaru shamisen players tend to tune the low string between
c and d.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 19 n.7.* **So the old practice is relative and singer-led; current
solo practice has drifted to a de facto C–D.** The retuning function survives formally in the
competitions' utazuke divisions, where the rules allot **"tuning and intro within 1 minute."**
*`tsugaru-shamisen.jp/about/`.*

### Scale material — and the answer is not the dichotomy usually offered

**[V — the definitions, from an official Japanese source]** The **Japan Arts Council's 文化デジタル
ライブラリー**, following **Koizumi Fumio's** theory, gives four basic pentatonic scales of Japanese
traditional music, each built from **two identical tetrachords spanning a perfect fourth**:

| Scale | Degrees | On C | On E |
|---|---|---|---|
| **民謡音階 min'yō** (= *yō-sen* family, anhemitonic) | **1, ♭3, 4, 5, ♭7** | C–E♭–F–G–B♭ | **E–G–A–B–D** |
| **律音階 ritsu** | **1, 2, 4, 5, 6** | C–D–F–G–A | **E–F♯–A–B–C♯** |
| **都節音階 miyako-bushi** (= *in-sen*, two semitones) | **1, ♭2, 4, 5, ♭6** | C–D♭–F–G–A♭ | **E–F–A–B–C** |
| **琉球音階 ryūkyū** | 1, 3, 4, 5, 7 | C–E–F–G–B | E–G♯–A–B–D♯ |

*Source: 日本芸術文化振興会, 文化デジタルライブラリー, 「基本的な4種の五音音階」, fetched and
translated 2026-11-13.* Corroborated for miyako-bushi by ja.wikipedia 都節音階, which gives the
representative pitches as **「ミ・ファ・ラ・シ・ド」** and calls it an **陰音階 / 陰旋法** with
**two semitones**, citing 日本国語大辞典 (2nd ed.) and デジタル大辞泉. **[V]**

**[V — and now the actual Tsugaru data]** McGoldrick's mode table, built from **Kinoshita Shin'ichi's
*Tsugaru Shamisen Style Book* (2004)**:

| Piece | Tuning | "Tonality" (Kinoshita's term) | Metre (emic) | Scale |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Jonkara** | **E–B–E** (niagari) | "minor" | 2/4 | **E–G–A–B–D–E** |
| **Yosare** | **E–B–E** (niagari) | "minor" | 3/8 | **E–G–A–B–D–E** |
| **Ohara** | **E–A–E** (honchōshi) | "minor" | 3/8 **and** 2/4 | **E–G–A–B–D–E** |
| **Aiya** | **E–B–E** (niagari) | **"major"** | 3/8 | **E–F♯–A–B–C♯–E** |
| **Sansagari** | **E–A–D** (sansagari) | "minor" | 3/8 | **E–F♯–A–B–D–E** |

*McGoldrick 2005, 89, reproduced as Fig. 1.01 in McGoldrick 2017, 20, from Kinoshita 2004.*
McGoldrick warns that "**Kinoshita's use of the English terms major ('meijaa') and minor ('mainaa')
differs from the standard western use**."

**Now cross the two tables, which to my knowledge nobody has done in print:**

- **Jongara, Yosare and Ohara all use the min'yō (yō-sen) pentatonic**, exactly: E–G–A–B–D = 1, ♭3,
  4, 5, ♭7. **This is the anhemitonic, semitone-free scale.** It is what Kinoshita calls "minor."
- **Aiya uses the ritsu scale**, exactly: E–F♯–A–B–C♯ = 1, 2, 4, 5, 6. **This is what Kinoshita
  calls "major,"** and it is consistent with Aiya being an import from the Haiya family of western
  Japan rather than a native Tsugaru kudoki.
- **Sansagari is a hybrid**: E–F♯–A–B–D = 1, 2, 4, 5, ♭7 — a **ritsu lower tetrachord (1–2–4) with a
  min'yō upper tetrachord (5–♭7–8)**.
- **None of the five great songs uses the miyako-bushi / in-sen scale.**

**[V on both tables; the cross-mapping and the hybrid analysis are my inference from them, and I
flag them as such.]** **The practical upshot, and a correction to the usual framing:** the
min'yō-vs-miyako-bushi dichotomy is the wrong frame for Tsugaru. **Tsugaru is min'yō-pentatonic
territory with one ritsu outlier and one hybrid, and the hemitonic miyako-bushi scale — the sound of
urban Edo art music, of *jiuta* and *kumiuta* and the koto's *hirajōshi* — is essentially absent.**
That is a real and audible difference between tsugaru-jamisen and the art-music shamisen traditions:
**no semitones.** (ja.wikipedia 六段の調 notes that it was precisely Yatsuhashi's **introduction of
semitones into gagaku tuning practice, giving the 陰音階**, that defined the modern koto school — the
opposite move. **[V]**)

**[V — one qualification]** en.wikipedia states baldly that "**the scale is pentatonic (do re mi sol
la)**" *(en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, "Playing method," uncited)* — which is the **ritsu** set, not
the min'yō set, and does not match Kinoshita's data for four of the five pieces. **Prefer the
Kinoshita/McGoldrick table.** **[U on the en.wikipedia statement.]**

**[V]** A recorded trend away from all of this: at the 2015 Hirosaki contest McGoldrick heard a group
"**employing a major heptatonic scale rather than jonkara's standard pentatonic scale (e.g.
C–E♭–F–G–B♭)**" — note he gives the min'yō set as the standard — and says "**this trend to include a
snatch of western melody seems to have begun in the 2000s.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 107.*
