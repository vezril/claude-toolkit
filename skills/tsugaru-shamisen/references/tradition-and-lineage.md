One methodological warning that recurs below. **Almost the entire popular history of this tradition
descends from one man's reconstruction**: Daijō Kazuo (大條和雄, b. 1928), a Hirosaki writer, not an
academic. Japanese Wikipedia's own 津軽三味線 article states it bluntly: Daijō was essentially the
first to collect and write down the oral traditions, and current understanding of the history "owes
much to his research results" — *but* "it too is no more than a reconstruction of oral tradition,
and because it contains a great deal of fictional element, its value as a historical source is low"

## 1. Place and preconditions

### Where Tsugaru is

**[V]** The Tsugaru region (津軽地方) is the **western part of present-day Aomori Prefecture**,
northern Honshū, corresponding closely to the Edo-period domain of the Tsugaru clan (Hirosaki han
and its sub-domain Kuroishi han) and to the old Tsugaru district of Mutsu province. Area
**4,828.08 km²**, 50.06% of the prefecture (Geospatial Information Authority, 2010 survey);
population **774,617**, 56.4% of the prefecture (2010 national census). *Source: ja.wikipedia
津軽地方, with the GSI and Aomori Prefecture statistical citations it carries.*

**[V]** Three sub-districts: **Chū-Kō-Nan-Koku / 中弘南黒** centred on **Hirosaki**;
**Seihokugo / 西北五** centred on **Goshogawara**; **Tōsei / 東青** centred on Aomori City. In the
narrow sense "Tsugaru" means only the first, or the first two; the Seihokugo-to-Tsugaru-peninsula
area is called **Oku-Tsugaru / 奥津軽**. The region is often conceived as the **Tsugaru plain
(津軽平野)** formed by the **Iwaki river (岩木川)**, dominated on the horizon by **Mt. Iwaki**.
*Source: ja.wikipedia 津軽地方.*

**[V]** **Kanagi (金木)** — Nitabō's birthplace and the site of the Tsugaru-shamisen Kaikan — is a
small former town absorbed into **Goshogawara City in 2005**; its population was **10,557 in 2005**,
just before amalgamation. McGoldrick, who travelled there repeatedly, puts it about **40 km north of
Hirosaki**, roughly two hours by train with a change at Goshogawara onto the single-car Tsugaru
Railway. *Source: McGoldrick 2017, 130 and n.46.*

**[U — worth flagging]** The usual claim that tsugaru-jamisen is "from the Tsugaru plain" sits badly
with **Takahashi Chikuzan's own birthplace: Kominato, Nakahiranai village, Higashi-Tsugaru-gun (now
Hiranai-machi)** — on the **Mutsu Bay** coast east of Aomori City, inside "Tsugaru" in the broad
administrative sense but well outside the Tsugaru plain. *Source: ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山, citing
Ōshiro Hidena 大城英名, 「『高橋竹山』考」, 秋田大学教育文化学部研究紀要 教育科学 67 (2012): 23–32.*
The most internationally famous exponent of "Tsugaru" music was not a man of the Tsugaru plain.

### Climate, poverty, and the national image of the place

**[V]** "The area's severe winters and relatively short summers left it vulnerable to famine even
into the nineteenth century, and infanticide, particularly of girls, was not unheard of… The less
than ideal growing conditions meant that historically Tsugaru was one of the poorest regions in
Japan. This prompted the practice of *dekasegi*, which sees men spend part of the year working in
the larger urban centres to the south, particularly Tokyo." *McGoldrick 2017, 13, drawing on Guo et
al., Tsugaru: Regional Identity on Japan's Northern Periphery (University of Otago Press, 2005), 18.*

**[V]** At the point of Tokugawa recognition of **Tsugaru Tamenobu (1550–1607)** in 1590 and the
founding of the castle town of Hirosaki, Tsugaru was regarded less as part of Japan proper than as a
**"domestic colony at the edge of the nation,"** with Ainu still present in the northernmost
districts. *McGoldrick 2017, 13, quoting Kawanishi in Guo et al. 2005, 52.*

**[V]** Into the Meiji era (1868–1912) "the area's national reputation as a primitive hinterland,
populated by people who spoke an unintelligible Japanese dialect, continued," and **the Meiji
emperor declined an invitation to tour the prefecture in 1874**. Kawanishi argues this was
internalised as a regional inferiority complex lasting until 1945; McGoldrick says it persists, and
reports Aomori people bitterly recalling being "made fools of" (*baka ni sareta*) for their accent.
*McGoldrick 2017, 13–14 and n.4, citing Kawanishi 2005, 53, 56.*

**[V]** A concrete, dated piece of evidence for how low the music's prestige was: a **1934 Tō-ō
Nippō** (the Aomori daily) article praised local min'yō but urged that the lyrics be sung in
standard Japanese — "'Jonkara Bushi' had been considered a bad song because 'it was improperly
pronounced and reeked of the countryside.' But now… 'the songs of the homeland have overcome such
defects and have demonstrated their deep spiritual affinity with the people.'"
*Groemer 2012, 81, quoted in McGoldrick 2017, 209–210.*

### Why an itinerant begging-performer economy existed, and what the *bosama* actually were

**[V]** **Bosama (坊様)** in Tsugaru = **blind male itinerant performers** who performed
**kadozuke (門付け)**, playing at the gates/eaves of houses for rice or money. Chiba, himself a
tsugaru-shamisen player, states the economics without sentiment: "At that time, Japan was a feudal
society, and those who were in a socially vulnerable position could not receive support from the
government, were despised by others, and were not allowed to work. In order to survive, bosama had
no choice but to travel around in the harsh winters of the Tōhoku region, playing the shamisen at
the eaves of houses and receiving rice and money through a practice known as *kadozuke-gei* 門付芸…
These circumstances required bosama to attract people through shamisen playing, and hence the
Tsugaru shamisen was devised." *Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 3, citing Daijō 1995, Matsuki 2011,
Takahashi 1991.*

**[V]** "One of the few professions open to a blind man in pre-modern Japan was musician, and while
this changed in the modern era, **these traditions held on in the far north much longer**. Typically
young blind boys apprenticed under *bosama* for a time before striking out on their own to travel
seasonal routes through fishing and farming villages playing at the gates of homes… They also played
at festivals and extremely lucrative **horse-trading fairs**, and it is thought that the competitive
atmosphere at these events spurred the development of the genre's loud, fast style."
*McGoldrick 2017, 15–16, citing Suda, Daijō & Rausch 1998, 42 and 79.*

**[V — the legal position]** The relevant institution is the **tōdōza (当道座)**, the guild through
which blind men in the Tokugawa status order could acquire a licensed livelihood in biwa, shamisen,
*anma* (massage) and acupuncture. It was a **status-based organ of the feudal system**, and
admission could be refused on grounds of parentage. It was **abolished in 1871 (Meiji 4)** by the
Dajōkan proclamation 「盲人之官職自今以後被廃候事」. *Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊 and 当道座.*
Tsugaru's *bosama* were, structurally, **blind musicians outside or below the guild**.

**[V — the contempt, dated]** Groemer records that **as late as 1962** a television broadcast
featuring Chikuzan prompted conservative Tsugaru residents to exclaim **"how dare they drag a
beggar into our homes!"** *Groemer 2012, 8, quoted in McGoldrick 2017, 211.* Two corroborating
details: **Chikuzan listed his occupation as "masseur," not "musician," until the early 1970s**
*(Hughes 2008, 85, via McGoldrick 2017, 211)*; and Daijō "recounts the anger and embarrassment he
encountered from the surviving relatives of *bosama* he tried to question in the early years of his
research" *(Suda et al. 1998, 123, via McGoldrick 2017, 211)*. McGoldrick's summary: "If being a
shamisen player earned one little respect, **being a bosama was literally a source of shame.**"

**[V]** Even within living players' families the profession was contemptible on economic grounds:
Yamada Chisato's parents "were firmly against his taking this route" because "the profession of
shamisen player was still looked down on, mainly because it was so hard to make a living at it," and
he had to **run away from home at fifteen** with the shamisen. *McGoldrick 2017, 115–116, citing
Matsuki 1994 and the Sasamori & Westerhoven 2005 interview.*

**[V — do not sentimentalise the "folk" framing either]** Groemer notes that the music was not
originally conceived as "folk music" at all: the goze sang *uta* (songs), not *min'yō* (folk songs),
and **the travelling troupes Chikuzan, Shirakawa and Kida belonged to were called *utakai*
(「唄会」, "songfests") even into the 1940s**, not *min'yō taikai*. *Groemer 2012, 53, via
McGoldrick 2017, 209.* And of the commercialised post-1920s product Groemer writes that it
**"should probably be labeled 'folk' only with great reservations."** *Groemer 2012, 73, quoted in
McGoldrick 2017, 190.*

## 2. Nitabō (仁太坊) — the founding figure, and the founding legend

### What the standard account says

**[V]** Real name **Akimoto Nitarō (秋元仁太郎)**; nickname formed the standard *bosama* way, by
suffixing **-bō (坊)** to part of the given name — "Nitarō → Nitabō," exactly as "Ōta Chōsaku →
Chōsakubō." *Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊; Kotobank / Shinsen Geinō Jinbutsu Jiten 2010;
McGoldrick 2017, 17 n.5.*

**[V]** **Dates: born Ansei 4 (1857), 7th day of the 7th month; died 2 January 1928 (Shōwa 3), aged
70.** Japanese Wikipedia sources the birth to **Daijō Kazuo, 『津軽三味線の誕生：民俗芸能の生成と
隆盛』(Shin'yōsha, 1995), p. 10**, and the death to Kotobank. It also records **a variant tradition
that he died on 2 November**, sourced to 『津軽三味線物語 始祖仁太坊』, みんよう文化 25th-anniversary
special issue (Sankei Shimbun, 2004), p. 45. *Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊.*

**[V — strong institutional confirmation of 1857]** The Kanagi competition's own 2027 masthead reads
**「仁太坊生誕１７０年記念」** — "commemorating the 170th anniversary of Nitabō's birth" — for the
39th competition on **3–4 July 2027**. 1857 + 170 = 2027. *Source: `kanagi-gc.net/genkon/`,
fetched 2026-11-13.*

**[V — birthplace, and a correction]** The documented birthplace is **Kanbara village, Kanagi
Shinden (金木新田 神原村), now Kanbara, Kanagi-machi, Goshogawara City, Aomori** — *not* "Kanizawa."
*Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊.* The Nitabō Festival page likewise calls him **「神原の仁太坊」**,
"Nitabō of Kanbara." *Source: `kanagi-gc.net/genkon/genkon_3.html`.* (Kotobank gives only the
coarser "Kanagi, Mutsu province.")

**[V — father]** Father **Santarō (三太郎)**, who lived in a **boat hut on the bank of the Iwaki
river and worked as a ferry keeper (渡し守)**. Note the precision: a river-ferry man, not a boatman
in the seagoing sense. He **drowned in 1866** when the Iwaki flooded in bad weather, leaving Nitarō
an orphan who had to live by the shamisen. *Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊.*

**[V — the blinding]** **Smallpox (天然痘) at age 8**, with loss of sight. *Sources: ja.wikipedia
仁太坊; Kotobank.* (Contrast Chikuzan, blinded at 3 by complications of **measles** — the two are
routinely confused.)

**[V — the exclusion, which is the sociologically important bit]** Because he was **the son of a
ferry keeper**, a lineage classed as **「筋目悪しき者」**("a person of bad lineage"), **Nitarō was
refused admission to the tōdōza** and so was shut out of the licensed route by which a blind man
might make a living. *Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊.*

**[V — taking up the instrument]** The standard account: at **10** he heard a **goze** passing
through Kanbara and was violently moved; his father asked her to teach him. After the 1871 abolition
of the tōdōza *and* of the *komusō* order (he had earlier been given rudimentary **shakuhachi**
instruction by a *komusō* who happened by the ferry landing), these arts became freely performable
and he widened his field of work as a *bosama*, appearing at festivals with **shamisen on his back
and shakuhachi and bamboo flute at his waist**, known for the comic riddle act **nazokake (なぞかけ)**
and above all for **hachinin-gei (八人芸)** — a one-man street act doing the work of eight
performers (shamisen, shakuhachi, drum, vocal mimicry), called **hachinin-zatō** before the
Restoration. *Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊; Kotobank corroborates the hachinin-gei.*

**[V — the futozao and the tataki, with a date]** He **heard gidayū-bushi at the Momori-za (茂森座)
theatre in Hirosaki**, fell for the sound, and **first took up the thick-necked *futozao* shamisen
in Meiji 11 (1878)**; with it he worked out his own method of **striking the strings violently to
bring out a dynamic low register — the 叩き (tataki) technique.**
*Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊; Kotobank gives the same innovation without the date.*

**[V — the documented students, with dates]**
- **Meiji 13 (1880):** took in **Furukawa Kinosuke (古川喜之助)**, aged 15, as live-in disciple —
  first apprentice. Nitabō had married about two years before; wife **Man (マン)**; first child
  **Sakusuke** born November 1880. **Meiji 14 (1881), late autumn:** Kinosuke went independent as
  **Kinobō (喜之坊)** and returned to Arata in Minami-Tsugaru.
- **Meiji 21 (1888):** **Ōta Chōsaku (太田長作)** of Nagadoro came to apprentice; blinded in
  childhood, he had decided at 15 to become a shamisen player, and walked from Nagadoro to Kanbara
  with a cane. Independent after three years as **Chōsakubō**; moved from Nagadoro to Kitsunemori in
  **Meiji 28 (1895)**; eventually **some 300 disciples**. **Umeda Hōgetsu (梅田豊月)** was one of
  Chōsakubō's pupils, not Nitabō's.
- Other disciples include **Kase no Momo (嘉瀬の桃)**.
- **Taishō 6 (1917):** **Shirakawa Gunpachirō** applied, and became the **last disciple**.

*Source: ja.wikipedia 仁太坊 throughout, sourced to Daijō 1995 and the 2004 Sankei/みんよう文化 issue.*

**[V]** **Nitabō left no recordings.** No recording of him appears in any discography I examined,
including McGoldrick's exhaustive 2017 survey of 66 albums and his appendices of early players
compiled from actual and facsimile 78-rpm labels. McGoldrick's generational scheme places Nitabō,
Kinbō and Ōta Chōsaku in the stratum of "blind itinerant originators" explicitly distinguished from
the four who recorded. *McGoldrick 2017, 63–64 and 61 n.15.* The earliest Tsugaru min'yō recording
of any kind is **1918** (§9 of `institutions-and-modern-era.md`), when Nitabō was 61 — so a recording was technologically possible and
simply does not exist.

### What is legend — and exactly where Groemer corrects it

**This is the part of the tradition most heavily mythologised, and the correction is specific.**

**[V] The whole "sole originator" thesis is Daijō Kazuo's, and it is rejected by scholars.**
Japanese Wikipedia introduces Nitabō with the hedge **「大條によると、津軽三味線の始祖は…」**
("*according to Daijō*, the founder of tsugaru-shamisen is…"). *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*
English Wikipedia's own further-reading note on Daijō states: "His writings, often in
**semi-novelistic form**, emphasize that everything originated with one 'Nitabō.' **Most scholars
reject this unilinear derivation of the genre.**" *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen, wikitext read in
session.*

**[V] Groemer's position, as reported with page citations by McGoldrick:**

> "Nitabō was certainly an important early figure, and many subsequent players can trace their
> musical lineage back to him, but **it is exceedingly unlikely that he was the first bosama.
> Gerald Groemer makes a convincing argument against Daijō's claims for Nitabō as the genre's
> originator (Groemer 2012, 66–68)**."
> — *McGoldrick 2017, 16–17.*

> "It seems that just as **Daijō collapsed all of the early players into the person of Nitabō**, he
> also made the goze's purported role in the creation of the genre more literal. **Groemer points
> out how unlikely Daijō's story is, and notes that an earlier source contradicts its key points
> (Groemer 2012, 60–68)**."
> — *McGoldrick 2017, 17.*

**[V] Groemer also establishes the historiography of the legend — Daijō did not invent the name, but
he made it a cult.** Groemer notes that **Shirakawa Kanegorō, in *Tales of Old Kanagi* (1981), had
already called Nitabō "the virtuoso who created the sources of Tsugaru-jamisen"** — but that Daijō
"has been his most vocal promoter." *Groemer 2012, 60, via McGoldrick 2017, 206.*

**[V] The decisive negative evidence is documentary silence.** Nitabō is **"completely absent"** from
the account of "the shamisen of the Tsugaru pieces" in the **1956 NHK anthology of Aomori folk song**,
which instead **identifies Kase no Momotarō as the creator of this new way of playing** (Kawanishi
1956, 253). And in **Yamada Chisato's *The Tradition of Tsugaru Min'yō* (1978)**, Yamada names
**three** men as the first notable performers — **Kase no Momotarō, Tanesato no Matsutarō, and
Dezaki no bō** — says they were **self-taught** and played only to accompany themselves "with no
melody or introduction," i.e. without even short solo sections; **Nitabō appears once later in the
book, only as a bosama who taught Shirakawa Gunpachirō.** *McGoldrick 2017, 205–206, citing
Kawanishi 1956, 253 and Yamada 1978, 13–16, 58.* In other words: as recently as 1978, the single
most influential living figure in the Tsugaru-shamisen world did not regard Nitabō as the founder.

**[V] One point where Groemer gives Daijō some credit.** On the claim that the *bosama* held
contests among themselves, Groemer "writes more cautiously that the bosama played on temple grounds
at summer festivals, and that some of these occasions 'might have turned into competitions'"
(*Groemer 1999, 36*), **but he also cites an interview by the scholar Kimura Genzō (1902–1978) with
Maeda Takesaburō, one of the last living bosama, who said there were contests in Kanagi, with prizes
for the loudest sound, or for new riffs — "which gives some credence to Daijō's assertions"**
(*Groemer 2012, 62*). *Via McGoldrick 2017, 115.* Daijō dates such contests as early as **1913**;
McGoldrick calls the idea that they resembled today's competitions "part of Daijō's 'invented
history.'" *McGoldrick 2017, 114–115.*

**[V] A further sign that the received history is a construction, not a record:** the accompanist
**Igarashi Kiyoei (五十嵐 清栄?, 1920–1952)**, who worked alongside Shirakawa and Kida throughout the
1940s, first recorded in 1940 and **drowned in 1952 trying to cross a swollen river on the way to a
performance**, is "almost entirely absent from writings on Tsugaru shamisen prior to Matsuki" (2011).
He does appear in Groemer's monograph — **"but listed as 'Igarashi Shōei,' Groemer's best guess on
the reading of the characters… after consulting with several experts in Hirosaki who were apparently
unable to recall the correct pronunciation" (Groemer 2012, 65)**. A 2008 television documentary
ranks him among the **four** most important players of the era alongside Shirakawa, Kida and Fukushi.
*McGoldrick 2017, 206–207, citing Matsuki 2011, 75–77.* A tradition in which experts cannot agree on
how to pronounce a major 1940s player's name is not a tradition with reliable records of the 1870s.

**[V] How the legend was built, institution by institution.** McGoldrick lists the machinery, all of
it Daijō's or Daijō-assisted: numerous books and articles; **founding the Kanagi national
competition in 1989**; **helping establish the Tsugaru-shamisen museum in Kanagi in 2000**; **stone
memorials in Kanagi**; and **the 2004 animated feature *NITABOH*.** He adds a field observation
worth having: "When I first read about Nitabō in 1998 in the English adaptation of Daijō's *The
Birth of Tsugaru Shamisen*, acquaintances in Tsugaru shamisen circles were quick to tell me that he
was not generally recognized as the creator of the genre, but **during my field work in 2010 I was
surprised at how widely accepted the Nitabō legend had become.**" He judges that Henry **Johnson,
writing that "the contemporary place of Nitabō is one that is sometimes questioned by some
performers, scholars and enthusiasts alike" (Johnson 2009, 84), "drastically overestimates the
degree to which the theory is accepted"** — i.e. in the scholarly literature the Daijō thesis is not
merely "sometimes questioned," it is rejected. *McGoldrick 2017, 206–207.*

**[V] And the institutions state the legend as fact.** The Nitabō Festival page describes the event
as held annually "in accordance with the **historical fact (史実)** that Nitabō of Kanbara, **the
founder (始祖) of tsugaru-shamisen** and a native of Kanagi town, went around playing at various
places in Kanagi during his lifetime." *`kanagi-gc.net/genkon/genkon_3.html`.* The Dazai Museum
likewise labels him 津軽三味線の始祖. *ja.wikipedia 仁太坊, citing dazai.or.jp.* The Tsugaru-shamisen
Kaikan's exhibit list includes a dedicated **"Nitabō biographical panel"** and an exhibit titled
**「津軽三味線誕生の経緯」** ("how tsugaru-shamisen came to be born"). *Goshogawara City official page.*

**[U — a direct contradiction inside the legend, unresolved]** Kotobank's *Shinsen Geinō Jinbutsu
Jiten* entry says **Nitabō's mother was herself a blind shamisen-playing woman**, that he lost both
parents by 11, and that he followed a wandering goze to learn her art. Japanese Wikipedia says the
mother **fell ill after childbirth and died**, with no musical role. McGoldrick identifies the goze
mother as a feature of **Daijō's myth** specifically: "In Daijō Kazuo's Nitabō myth, Nitabō's
mother, who dies shortly after he is born, is a goze, and he learns shamisen directly from another
goze (Suda et al. 1998, 28–29)." *McGoldrick 2017, 17.* **The goze-mother is legend.** The
encyclopedia entry has absorbed it.

**[U]** Kotobank also says he was a disciple **of Nitabō at age 9 / performing by 1871 at 14**, and
that he **lost his sight at 8** — all consistent; but Kotobank's date for Shirakawa's apprenticeship
conflicts with ja.wikipedia (see §3).

### The exhortation to "play your own music" — what the actual text is

This is the most quoted sentence in the tradition and it has **no contemporary documentation**.
Three attested forms, in descending order of institutional weight:

1. **[V] The Kanagi competition's official motto, in Tsugaru dialect:**
   **「人真似でね、汝の三味線を弾け！」**
   *hito-mane de ne, na no shamisen o hike* — "Don't imitate others — play **your** shamisen!"
   (汝 *na* = Tsugaru dialect "your"). Printed as the masthead of the 39th competition page.
   *Source: `kanagi-gc.net/genkon/genkon_1.html`, fetched 2026-11-13.* Note: Daijō founded this
   competition, so this is Daijō's rendering under an organiser's imprimatur.
2. **[V] The tagline (キャッチコピー) of the 2004 animated film:**
   **「人まねでない自分の三味線を弾け!」**
   *hito-mane de nai jibun no shamisen o hike* — "Play your own shamisen, not an imitation of
   someone else's!" *Source: ja.wikipedia 「NITABOH 仁太坊-津軽三味線始祖外聞」, wikitext read in
   session.*
3. **[V] Daijō's fuller version, in English paraphrase:** "Daijō Kazuo often quotes Nitabō as saying
   that **imitation is something that even a monkey can do, and that each player must play his own
   Tsugaru shamisen**." *McGoldrick 2017, 21–22, citing Daijō 1998, 57.*

**[V — the provenance, which matters]** The clearest evidence of how thin the sourcing is: **Chiba
Gakuto, a national A-class champion writing in a Brill volume, gives the saying as "Don't just
follow the path of others, play your own Shamisen!" and cites it to *Nishizawa 2004* — i.e. to
Nishizawa Akio, the director of the 2004 anime.** *Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 6; the
bibliography entry reads 西澤昭男「NITABOH 仁太坊-津軽三味線始祖外聞」ワオ・コーポレーション 2004.*
When an insider scholar's citation for the founder's words is a cartoon, the words are tradition,
not record.

**[U]** I could not find any pre-Daijō attestation of the saying in any form, and I could not reach
Groemer to see whether he addresses it. **Treat the sentiment as the tradition's self-description
and the wording as twentieth-century.**

**[V] A real irony to note:** the attributed teaching is *anti-imitation*, and the institution that
most loudly repeats it is a **competition** — whose structure, as §7 of `institutions-and-modern-era.md` shows, rewards uniformity.

## 3. The transmission lineage

### The documented shape of it

**[V]** McGoldrick's stratification, built from lineage charts in **Matsuki 2011, Groemer 2012 and
Daijō 1984** plus **ranking charts from 1942, 1952 and 1967** and from actual and facsimile 78-rpm
labels (his Appendices A and D):

- **Stratum 1 — blind itinerant originators, no recordings:** **Nitabō, Kinbō, Ōta Chōsaku.**
- **Stratum 2 — early *sighted* accompanists:** **Umeda Hōgetsu** and **Kase no Momo**, who "toured
  with min'yō troupes but **are not known to have played solo**."
- **Stratum 3 — the four giants, all born 1909–1913:** **Shirakawa Gunpachirō, Kida Rinshōei,
  Fukushi Masakatsu, Takahashi Chikuzan.** "They… all **learned Tsugaru shamisen as an accompanying
  (rather than solo) instrument, and were the first generation of players to perform solo pieces.**"
- **Stratum 4 — "second tier," mostly born in the 1930s:** **Mihashi Michiya, Oyama Mitsugu,
  Takahashi Yūjirō, Yamada Chisato, Fujita Jun'ichi, Sawada Katsuaki** — "by the time they were
  learning Tsugaru shamisen, its possibilities as a solo instrument were already beginning to be
  explored."

*Source: McGoldrick 2017, 63–64, 2176.*

**[V]** A standing prewar/early-postwar grouping was the **「三羽烏」 ("three crows") / "three big
players": Shirakawa, Kida and Fukushi Masakatsu** — within the Mutsunoya troupe, and in the
recording trade. **Chikuzan was excluded**: "Because of his history, Takahashi Chikuzan has always
been considered something of an outsider in the min'yō world, and so did not make the list."
*McGoldrick 2017, 61 n.16 and 61; the troupe grouping is corroborated by the Shishidō company
article on Kida.*

### Shirakawa Gunpachirō (白川軍八郎)

**[V] Dates: 23 January 1909 – 18 May 1962.** Born **Kanagi-machi, Kita-Tsugaru-gun, Aomori** (now
part of Goshogawara City). **Lost his sight at 4.** Became **Nitabō's disciple**, and is universally
described as **Nitabō's last pupil**. Nicknamed **「津軽三味線の神様」, "the god of
tsugaru-shamisen."** *Source: Kotobank / Nichigai, Shinsen Geinō Jinbutsu Jiten (2010), entry
白川軍八郎(初代); corroborated for dates and nickname by ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, which cites the same
Kotobank page.*

**[V] The technical contribution, concretely.** Kotobank: he overtook his teacher quickly, **taught
himself Ōta Chōsaku's "Chōsaku-te" and Umeda Hōgetsu's "Umeda-te"** (i.e. their characteristic
hand/phrase vocabularies), and **at 15 created his own, the 「軍八郎手」 "Gunpachirō-te."** He is
credited with **establishing the tsugaru-shamisen as a solo instrument, raising its status to equal
that of the singing.** *Kotobank, as above; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 also attributes 軍八郎手 to him.*

**[V] The mechanism of the shift from accompaniment to solo — the key documented claim in the whole
history.** "From the 1930s Tsugaru min'yō began to be widely recorded, and in the same period a
large number of troupes of singers, dancers, and shamisen accompanists toured northern Japan
performing this repertoire. **It was during this period that certain shamisen players, most notably
Nitabō's last student Shirakawa Gumpachirō (1909–1962), began to develop longer introductions to
their accompaniment of songs, which eventually led to the solo instrumental style that we recognize
as Tsugaru shamisen today.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 17–18, citing Matsuki 2011, 141.* Japanese Wikipedia
says the same in its own words: the *maebiki* (前弾き) prelude played by the shamisen alone
"gradually became independent as a solo." *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **This, not Nitabō, is the
documented origin of kyokubiki.**

**[V] He also created the common phrase-stock.** Of the ten Shirakawa tracks on a 2003 King
two-CD set, McGoldrick writes that they "are a sort of **Rosetta Stone** for Tsugaru shamisen,
containing **many phrases that Shirakawa invented and are used today by every player as the building
blocks of composition and improvisation**." *McGoldrick 2017, 76–77.*

**[V] A striking asymmetry.** "Shirakawa Gumpachirō… is often referred to as 'Tsugaru-jamisen no
kamisama,' the god of Tsugaru shamisen, **so it is almost unbelievable that, in contrast to Takahashi
Chikuzan's voluminous output, a total of only thirteen unique Shirakawa Gumpachirō tracks have been
available on official releases in the last twenty years.**" His **last studio session was with King
Records in Tokyo in 1955** — before the phrase "Tsugaru shamisen" had any currency. **Ill health
prevented him from joining the move to Tokyo.** His full available instrumental output: *Tsugaru
yosare bushi* (4 recordings), *Tsugaru jonkara bushi* (3), *Tsugaru aiya bushi* (3), *Tsugaru
sansagari* (1), *Kogarashi* (1), *Tsugaru no kyōshū* (1). He recorded with **Victor, King, Teichiku,
Nitto and Regal** (a Columbia imprint). *McGoldrick 2017, 75–77, 194, citing Matsuki 2011, 142 and
Kawanishi 1956, 253.*

**[U — date discrepancies, flagged]** (a) Kotobank says Shirakawa became Nitabō's disciple **at 9
(1914)**; ja.wikipedia 仁太坊 says he **applied in Taishō 6 (1917)**, when he would have been 8.
(b) McGoldrick gives **1909–1962** consistently in his tables but writes on p. 194 that Shirakawa
"did not die until **1963**." The Kotobank/Nichigai date, **18 May 1962**, is the best-sourced and
should be preferred.

### Kida Rinshōei (木田林松栄)

**[V] Dates: 1911 (Meiji 44) – 1 January 1979 (Shōwa 54), aged 67.** Real name **Tanaka Rinjirō
(田中林次郎)**. Born in **Minami-Tsugaru-gun** — Kotobank says Hiraga town, the Shishidō article
specifies **Kashiwagi village** within it — third son of Tanaka Shigetarō and Sashi. **Began
shamisen at 10; stage debut at 19.** Known for a rugged, intense style; toured abroad often,
including **Expo 67 in Montreal**. *Sources: Kotobank / Nichigai Shinsen Geinō Jinbutsu Jiten 2010
and 20-seiki Nihonjin Jimei Jiten 2004 (dates, birthplace, debut, Montreal); 株式会社獅子道 (Shishido
Inc.) article 「幕末〜昭和を生きた津軽三味線の祖　木田林松栄」, 14 June 2021 (real name, parents,
village).*

**[V] Nickname: 「叩きの林松栄」, "Rinshōei of the striking."** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, citing the
Shishidō article.*

**[U — the lineage, and a disputed point, from a commercial source]** The Shishidō page (a
**promotional site** for a Tokyo shamisen-lesson and booking business — treat accordingly) states:
as a child he followed a street performer, **"Kamebō," a grand-pupil of Nitabō**; left agricultural
school at 17 to study shamisen; became the regular pupil of **"Fukita Sanshōe" (福田三松栄?) at 17**;
**at 19 (1930) joined the Mutsunoya troupe**, where he, **Fukushi Masakatsu and Shirakawa** became
the "three crows." Crucially: **he was never formally Shirakawa's pupil**, but **secretly listened
to Shirakawa's playing from the dressing room** and was deeply impressed. His **innovation was gauge:
he pushed the first string from the usual limit of **30** to **35** and eventually to a very thick
**40**, in pursuit of a more powerful sound.** He is a founder of the **Kida-ryū (木田流)**, focused
on accompanying singers, with many students. Career points given: **1932** recorded accompaniment
for the singer Kudō Mie; **1943** draft notice; **1961–62** worked at the Tokyo folk-song venue
「七五三」then the Ikebukuro banquet hall 「白雲閣」, becoming a headline act with the singer Asari
Miki; **1966** went independent with Asari Miki; **1974** the TV programme 「三味線の詩」 pitted him
against Chikuzan, a rivalry the page says was partly a media construction.
**I could verify only the dates, birthplace, Montreal and the nickname against non-commercial
sources. The rest rests on this promotional page alone.**

**[V — corroborated separately]** "From 1961, Kida was based in Tokyo, performing in folk song bars,
teaching, and touring." *Matsuki 2011, 143 and Groemer 2012, 92, via McGoldrick 2017, 194.* He
toured **the US, Canada, Iran, Turkey and Afghanistan in the late 1960s.** *Matsuki 2011, 144–145,
via McGoldrick 2017, 234.* He **"'taught' as he himself had learned, simply by allowing would-be
students to watch him play."** *McGoldrick 2017, 233.* And **in 1965 an instrument explicitly
labelled "Tsugaru shamisen" began to be sold in Tokyo under Kida's guidance** — the moment the
instrument was reified as a separate category. *Matsuki 2011, 144, via McGoldrick 2017, 234.*

**[V]** Kida's **iemoto/natori system** was adopted in **1970 (Shōwa 45)**, modelled on the one the
first Oyama Mitsugu had introduced in 1967. *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, citing Daijō, 『汝なだば』.*

**[V]** **Prewar evidence of instrumental duets:** a **Regal 69014** disc of **Kida Rinshōei and
Fukushi Masakatsu** playing instrumental duets of *Yosare bushi* and *Jongara bushi*. McGoldrick
dates it prewar because the label is printed right-to-left, and notes that **the label describes it
as a "shamisen contest," since the rubric "Tsugaru shamisen" did not yet exist.**
*McGoldrick 2017, 60–61 and n.15, citing Matsuki 2006, 205.*

### Takahashi Chikuzan (高橋竹山) — first generation

**[V] Real name: Takahashi Sadazō (高橋定蔵).** Yes — the user's guess is right.
**Born 18 June 1910; died 5 February 1998** of **laryngeal cancer** at Hiranai Town Central Hospital,
aged 87. Born in **Kominato, Nakahiranai village, Higashi-Tsugaru-gun** (now Hiranai-machi).
**Blinded at age 3** by complications of **measles (麻疹)** — not smallpox.
*Source: ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山, every one of these facts footnoted to **Ōshiro Hidena 大城英名,
「『高橋竹山』考」, 秋田大学教育文化学部研究紀要 教育科学 67 (2012): 23–32**, hdl.handle.net/10295/1874.
I could not open the Akita repository record itself (HTTP 406), so this is one remove from the
scholarly article.*

**[V] The bosama years.** **At 14 he apprenticed to Toda Shigejirō (戸田重次郎), a bosama in the
neighbouring village; at 17 he went independent**, doing **kadozuke through Aomori, Iwate, Akita and
Hokkaidō**. In **1933** he survived the **Shōwa Sanriku earthquake and tsunami**, escaping to the
hill behind his inn at Tamagawa on the Sanriku coast moments before the wave destroyed it. In
**1938** he married **Nayo**, an **itako** (blind female shamanic medium).
*Source: ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山, the apprenticeship and independence footnoted to Ōshiro 2012.*

**[V] The war and the licence.** As the Pacific War intensified the shamisen could no longer support
him; in **1944 he entered the Aomori Prefectural Hachinohe School for the Blind to qualify as an
acupuncturist and masseur** and withdrew from performing for a time. *ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山 /
Ōshiro 2012.* Groemer: **he trained from 1944 to 1949 and "seems to have practiced for only a very
short time before going full time with Narita."** *Groemer 2012, 302–304, via McGoldrick 2017, 211
n.57.*

**[V] The name, and the discovery.** **From 1950** he toured as **accompanist to Narita Unchiku
(成田雲竹, 1888–1974)**, "the godfather of Tsugaru min'yō" and sometimes "the god of Tsugaru min'yō,"
and **it was Unchiku who gave him the stage name "Chikuzan" (竹山)**. From **1954** the pair appeared
on Radio Aomori's folk-song programme. Unchiku asked him not merely to accompany but **to compose
shamisen accompaniments for Tsugaru min'yō that had previously been sung unaccompanied** — the pair
jointly unearthed and produced a large repertoire, including Unchiku's own **『りんご節』 Ringo-bushi**.
*ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山 / Ōshiro 2012.* McGoldrick adds that **he began creating *solo* compositions
only from about 1960.** *Matsubayashi 2000, 328, via McGoldrick 2017, 194.*

**[V] 1963 — the record that changed the category.** **King Records released the first-ever solo
tsugaru-shamisen LP**, and it was a major hit, making Chikuzan's name as a tsugaru-shamisen player
rather than an accompanist. **The partnership with Unchiku ended in 1964** on Unchiku's retirement.
*ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山 / Ōshiro 2012.* McGoldrick supplies the commercial mechanics: the album was
released **on the initiative of Saitō Kōji, a young director at King**, over the opposition of senior
staff, with a conservative **2,000 discs pressed — and it sold 70,000 copies within two years**, at a
time when a min'yō record selling 3,000–4,000 was considered a success. *Matsubayashi 2000, 140, via
McGoldrick 2017, 194.*
**[U — title discrepancy]** ja.wikipedia calls the LP 『津軽三味線　高橋竹山』; McGoldrick calls it
***Takahashi Chikuzan no sekai* (The World of Takahashi Chikuzan)**, King, 1963, re-released 1972 and
1977, reissued on CD in 1997 with the original cover and new liner notes, and again in 2011 in
King's "Stars of Min'yō" series. **I could not reconcile the two titles.**

**[V] The 1960s–70s move onto the concert stage — the actual mechanism.** Two things, in order:
1. **1964: he began playing concerts for Rō-on (労音), the Kinrōsha Ongaku Kyōgikai or "Workers'
   Music Council," a music-appreciation society with 220 chapters and 650,000 members nationwide.**
   Chikuzan had said that as an accompanist in noisy bars he could hardly hear himself play and found
   it hard to care; **playing for attentive Rō-on audiences, "he could not help but take his playing
   more seriously."** *Groemer 2012, 308 and Sato 2010, 112, via McGoldrick 2017, 212.* This is the
   under-reported hinge: the concert career began in the organised-labour music-appreciation circuit.
2. **1971: the Radio Aomori documentary 『寒撥』 (*Kanbachi*, "Cold Plectrum") was broadcast**, which
   made his name widely known; it won an **Excellence Prize at the Agency for Cultural Affairs Arts
   Festival** for that year. **1973: his first appearance at the small avant-garde theatre
   渋谷ジァン・ジァン (Shibuya Jean-Jean)**, after which regular concerts there "captured the hearts
   of many young people and became the forerunner of the tsugaru-shamisen boom."
   *ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山, the Arts Festival prize footnoted to the Agency for Cultural Affairs
   award list PDF.* **[U — one-year discrepancy:** McGoldrick dates *Kanbachi* to **1972**
   (*Matsubayashi 2000, 329*, via McGoldrick 2017, 194–195). ja.wikipedia says **1971** and cites the
   Agency's own prize list for Shōwa 46. **Prefer 1971.]**

**[V] The autobiography, and Groemer's translation.** 『自伝 津軽三味線ひとり旅』, **Shinshokan**;
later Chūkō Bunko 1991. **[U — year:** ja.wikipedia's body text says he published it in **1975**; its
own bibliography and the 関連文献 list in ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 both say **1976**; en.wikipedia's
bibliography says **1976**. **Prefer 1976**, with 1975 noted.] Groemer's translations:
- *The Autobiography of Takahashi Chikuzan: Adventures of a Tsugaru-jamisen Musician*, translated
  and annotated by Gerald Groemer, Detroit Monographs in Musicology / Studies in Music, **Harmonie
  Park Press, c.1991**.
- *The Spirit of Tsugaru: Blind Musicians, Tsugaru-jamisen, and the Folk Music of Northern Japan*,

  autobiography translation.
- **2nd revised edition (新訂増補): Hirosaki: Tsugaru Shobō, 2012, 369 pp.** English Wikipedia's

  information and brings the volume up to date. **Currently only available from amazon.co.jp, it
  remains the most detailed study of Tsugaru-shamisen to date in any language.**"
*Sources: ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山 and 津軽三味線; en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen (wikitext).*

**[V] Film and international reception.** **1977: 『竹山ひとり旅』**, written and directed by
**Shindō Kaneto**, with **Hayashi Ryūzō** as Chikuzan, entered as Japan's official selection at the
**Moscow International Film Festival**. **1986: a US tour — 10 concerts in 7 cities** (New York,

**[U]** The same article quotes a *New York Times* notice in Japanese translation ("as though he had

original NYT review and cannot verify the wording or that it exists.**

**[V] Honours.** 1961 Japan Folk Song Association skill award; 1971 Aomori Prefecture Culture Prize;
**1974 the Japan Folk Song Association's *meijin-i* (名人位, "master rank")**; 1975 9th Yoshikawa
Eiji Culture Prize and 12th Tenji Mainichi Culture Prize; 1982 3rd Matsuo Entertainment Prize;
**1983 Order of the Sacred Treasure, 4th class**; 1988 Pola Traditional Culture Special Prize; 1996
honorary citizen of Hiranai. *ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山, the 1974 meijin-i footnoted to Ōshiro 2012.*

**[V] The Chikuzan II succession — verified.** **In 1997** Chikuzan **passed the name to his
live-in disciple 竹与 (Takeyo), who became 二代目高橋竹山 (Takahashi Chikuzan II)**; the original
Chikuzan died in February 1998. ja.wikipedia explains the choice: he had other pupils, but
**recognised that Takeyo alone did no teaching at all and lived purely by performing**, and was
worried about where she would end up. *ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山.* McGoldrick independently confirms the
**1997** handover and gives her earlier name as **Takahashi Chikuyō**, born **1955**.
*McGoldrick 2017, 196–197, 62.* **Chikuzan II is a woman.**

**[V — and an important deflation] "Hiki-shamisen" may be a misunderstanding.** The received
contrast is between Kida's **叩き三味線 (tataki, "striking")** and Chikuzan's **弾き三味線 (hiki,
"plucking")**. Japanese Wikipedia reports that the label arose from a **misheard interview**:
asked "is the shamisen something you *strike* or something you *play/pluck*?", Chikuzan — taking it
as a question of the same order as "a drum is something you hit, a shakuhachi something you blow"
and not as a question about technique at all — answered "the shamisen is something you *play*." This
was transmitted as a statement of style. **This section of the article is tagged 独自研究
(original research) by Wikipedia editors — treat as [U].** *ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山.*
McGoldrick independently records that the hiki style is also called **nejimi (ねじみ, "clear
sound")**, correcting the "nezumi" transliteration in Suda et al. 1998, 70 on the authority of older
players. *McGoldrick 2017, 18 and n.6.*

### Fukushi Masakatsu (福士政勝)

**[V]** One of the **four giants** born 1909–1913, and one of the **"three crows"** with Shirakawa
and Kida. **In 1956 he moved to Tokyo to play in the *min'yō sakaba* (folk-song pubs) catering
largely to migrant workers from the north**, especially Aomori men on Tokyo construction projects
(*dekasegi*); the postwar construction boom swelled their numbers enough to support a host of such
pubs, and **by 1958 a min'yō boom was underway, centred on the pubs of Akasaka**.
*McGoldrick 2017, 63–64, citing Guo et al. 2005, 18.* He is listed among the old **tataki-line**
masters alongside Shirakawa and Kida. *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* A 2008 Aomori Television documentary
counts him among the **four** most important players of the 1940s with Shirakawa, Kida and Igarashi.
*McGoldrick 2017, 207.*

**[V — his recorded legacy is largely lost]** McGoldrick: recordings "too poor in quality to merit
re-release. **This is certainly the case with Fukushi Masakatsu**." *McGoldrick 2017, 67.* He
survives principally on the prewar Regal duet with Kida and on compilations.

**[V — his significance for the lineage]** He was **Yamada Chisato's de facto teacher** (below), and
therefore stands behind the whole Hirosaki competition line.

### Yamada Chisato (山田千里)

**[V] Dates: 10 October 1931 – 12 April 2004**, died at home of **congestive heart failure** at
about midnight, aged 72, after repeated hospitalisations for complications of diabetes. Born in
**Ajigasawa, Nishi-Tsugaru-gun** — a port town on the Japan Sea coast. **Second-youngest of nine
siblings.** His wife was the min'yō singer **Fukushi Ritsu (福士りつ)**.
*Sources: ja.wikipedia 山田千里, death date footnoted to 『現代物故者事典2003〜2005』 (Nichigai
Associates, 2006), p. 634; sibling position and the phonograph detail from Matsuki 2011, 88 via
McGoldrick 2017, 115.*
**[U — discrepancy]** McGoldrick's generational table at p. 61 gives "**Yamada Chisato (1935–2004)**"
while his own narrative at p. 115 gives **"born October tenth, 1931."** The 1931 date, supported by
the 現代物故者事典, is correct.

**[V] Self-taught, then apprenticed by proximity.** He listened as a child to the great players on
the family phonograph and taught himself on an unused shamisen lying around the house; local rumour
ran that **"in the hills of Ajigasawa there is a boy who will not be beaten by anyone at the
shamisen or at counting money"** — a reputation for both playing and business sense.
**In 1947, at fifteen, he ran away from home to travel with Fukushi Masakatsu's troupe**, carrying
his bags from town to town for the privilege of listening. His own account of the pedagogy:
**"All he told me was, 'You learn to play the shamisen by listening.' That was it. He didn't teach me
anything — not a single lick or trick."** *ja.wikipedia 山田千里; the quotation from the
**Sasamori Takefusa & James Westerhoven interview, "Life with the Tsugaru Shamisen: An Interview
with Yamada Chisato (山田千里, 1931–2004)," 2005, vol. 3, pp. 1–27**, via McGoldrick 2017, 116.
I located the interview's bibliographic record via OpenAlex but could not obtain its full text. [U
for anything beyond the quoted lines.]*

**[V] 1949:** formed his own small travelling troupe, **津軽民謡団**. **1964:** opened
**ライブハウス山唄 (Live House Yamauta)** in Hirosaki and married Fukushi Ritsu. The venue was named
after the folk song **「津軽山唄」**. It began as a high-end *ozashiki* establishment with hostesses
pouring drinks, was renovated into a popular min'yō *izakaya*, and became a landmark: roughly **100
seats** and a stage, cheap for a place where real tsugaru-shamisen could be heard, with a
well-regarded local-food menu, and queues around the block during the cherry-blossom and Neputa
festivals. **His live-in disciples worked in the restaurant and played there** — a modified version
of the apprenticeship he had served. **Over one hundred people studied with him across the forty
years he ran it**, by the same "learn by listening" method. **It closed in April 2016 for want of a
successor.** *ja.wikipedia 山田千里, the closure footnoted to Web Tō-ō (Tō-ō Nippō), 18 April 2016
and Mutsu Shinpō, 30 April 2016; the hundred students and the staffing arrangement from
McGoldrick 2017, 116.*

**[V] Internationally and in mass events:** **1976 Hungary tour**; **1982 New York, performing with
the jazz drummer Elvin Jones**; through the 1990s mass group performances including **a
1,000-player event at the Tokyo Dome**; **1990** appeared on NHK's Kōhaku Utagassen with Fukushi
Ritsu; **1988** a joint monument to the couple erected at Hamanasu Park, Ajigasawa; **1999**
prefectural commendation; **2002** MEXT commendation as a Person of Merit in Regional Culture.
*ja.wikipedia 山田千里; Tokyo Dome from Matsuki 1994 via McGoldrick 2017, 116.*

**[V — IMPORTANT CORRECTION to the usual account]** **Yamada founded the HIROSAKI competition, in 1982 — not
the Kanagi one.** The Kanagi competition (1989) was founded by **Daijō Kazuo**, partly *against*
Yamada's. See §7. *ja.wikipedia 山田千里 and 津軽三味線; `kanagi-gc.net`; McGoldrick 2017, 116, 132.*
**And even for Hirosaki, "founder" overstates it:** McGoldrick, "Yamada Chisato, **while not strictly
the founder** of Hirosaki's Tsugaru shamisen International Contest, was its driving force until his
death in 2004," and the contest "was originally conceived of by **ATV (Aomori Television Broadcasting
Company) as part of their twenty-fifth anniversary celebrations**," with Yamada stepping in to help
when ATV struggled to get it off the ground. *McGoldrick 2017, 115–116, citing Matsuki 2011, 95.*

**[V] His style and his students.** Yamada-ryū is characterised, in contrast to today's
technique-first playing, by **「強い叩き撥と唸り(うなり)」 — a strong *tataki-bachi* and a growl** —
and by "the earthy, unadorned timbre of the real Tsugaru." Students who won the Hirosaki contest:
**Satō Michihiro (1982, 1983), Nagamine Ken'ichi (1984), Okada Osamu (1985), Shibutani Kazuo (1990,
1992, 1993, 1994), Odori Shōtarō (1997, 1998, 1999), Narita Saori (1998, 1999 women's A-class),
Nakamura Haruko (2001, 2002 women's A), Sasagawa Kimito (2003), Muratomi Mitsuyo (2003 women's A)**.
*ja.wikipedia 山田千里 — note this article carries a 出典の明記 "needs sources" tag, so the student
list is [U] except where corroborated: Satō's 1982/83 wins are confirmed by McGoldrick 2017, 81
(citing Matsuki 2011, 97), and Shibutani's three-in-a-row by McGoldrick 2017, 183.*

### Sawada Katsuaki (澤田勝秋)

**[V]** **Born 1944.** Placed by McGoldrick in the **"second tier"** / the generation after the
1930s cohort, which Tanaka & Oda characterise as **more influenced by rock**; McGoldrick notes
"**Sawada, in particular, has pointed out connections between Tsugaru shamisen and jazz and rock**,"
while adding that no recordings are cited by Tanaka & Oda to support the claim and that the 1990s
compilation tracks show no obvious rock influence. He and Hasegawa Yūji (b. 1941) "are now **among
the last generation of players from Tsugaru**" — i.e. from the region itself.
*McGoldrick 2017, 61–62, citing Tanaka & Oda 2009, 165.* He is **iemoto of the 澤田流 (Sawada-ryū)**,
named by ja.wikipedia as one of the famous and organisationally large tataki-line schools.
*ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* He has at least one track on the 1990s compilation series.
*McGoldrick 2017, 66.*
**[U]** There is no Japanese Wikipedia article on him (I checked: the title returns an error page),

### Kinoshita Shin'ichi (木乃下真市, formerly 木下伸市)

**[V] Born 1965, from Wakayama Prefecture — not from Tsugaru.** Taught by his father, a
professional entertainer, from age **3**; began tsugaru-shamisen seriously at **10**. **1982:**
passed the NHK hōgaku audition at 17. **1986 and 1987: consecutive wins at the Tsugaru-shamisen
National Competition in Hirosaki.** Around then became a key member of Itō Takio's band. **1993:**
formed the 木下伸市グループ and launched his own **"tsugaru-shamisen rock."** **1999:** performed with
the Hungarian violinist **Roby Lakatos** on an NHK programme, beginning a recurring collaboration.
**May 2000:** won the inaugural **津軽三味線全国大会歴代A級チャンピオン山田千里杯争奪戦** (Yamada
Chisato Cup, contested among past A-class champions); October 2000, Wakayama City arts encouragement
prize. **1 October 2004:** changed his stage name from 木下伸市 to 木乃下真市.
*Source: ja.wikipedia 木乃下真市.* McGoldrick adds: **between 2001 and 2004 he released two albums of
traditional music, three hybrid albums, and a tsugaru-shamisen instructional book**, the 2004 book
using "pedagogical techniques that anyone familiar with popular guitar instruction manuals would
recognize" — a real break with the older tablature collections, which "invariably presented nothing
but full songs." *McGoldrick 2017, 87, 234, citing Kinoshita 2004.*

**[V — he is also a primary source on the music's theory]** His ***Tsugaru Shamisen Style Book***
(2004) sets out the system of tunings, tonalities, metres and scales that underlies the mode table
in §6. And his statement on improvisation is the single most-quoted insider formulation:
**"In the kyokubiki there is usually no melodic theme which is returned to… Of course, there are
typical Tsugaru phrases, but basically we don't have a melody or a theme to fall back on…"**
*Kinoshita 2003, 46 and 50, quoted in McGoldrick 2017, 21.*

**[U — his critique, wiki-only]** ja.wikipedia reports him saying in interviews that the current
state of affairs — tsugaru-shamisen drifting away from min'yō into a solo-only instrument, and
*performance* being valued over *playing* — **is dangerous (危険である)**; and that his models are
**Shirakawa Gunpachirō and Kida Rinshōei**, whom he says he could never catch up to in a lifetime.
*ja.wikipedia 木乃下真市, unsourced.*

### Shibutani Kazuo (渋谷和生)

**[V]** A **Yamada Chisato student** and an Aomori-based player listed among the famous contemporary
performers. *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, 山田千里.* **He won the Hirosaki competition in 1990, 1992, 1993
and 1994**, and was **the first player to win Hirosaki three times in a row, at which point he was
forced to retire from the contest** — the rule that has since shaped the whole prize economy (five
other players have since achieved three consecutive Hirosaki wins). *McGoldrick 2017, 183;
competition years from ja.wikipedia 山田千里.* In 1998 he was one of four players on the *Tsugaru
Modern* "Battle Royale" record, where he and Shindō Shōtarō "**represented young players of Tsugaru,
playing standard Tsugaru shamisen**," against Kinoshita and Agatsuma as representatives of "the
style that was developing outside the Tsugaru region." *McGoldrick 2017, 82.* He is therefore best
placed as **the last generation's in-region traditionalist champion**, not an innovator.
**[U]** No ja.wikipedia article exists for him; no birth year found.

### Two others who matter for the lineage, usually omitted in English

**[V] Oyama Mitsugu (小山貢) and the Oyama-ryū / 小山会 — the institutionalisation of pedagogy.**
Founded **1963** as 津軽三味線小山流 by the first Oyama Mitsugu (now styled **小山貢翁, Oyama Kōō**);
joined the Nihon Kyōdo Min'yō Kyōkai in **1965**; first recital 27 February **1966** at Meguro Public
Hall (opening piece *Yamanaka-bushi*); **1977: published 『津軽三味線小山貢民謡集』 through Hōgakusha
— in effect the first collection of notation in the tsugaru-shamisen world**; renamed **小山会 in
1992**. Iemoto succession: 1st Oyama Mitsugu → 2nd Oyama Mitsugu → 3rd **Oyama Yutaka**.
Characteristics: **first to publish notation and to teach from it**; a wide *bachi* opening;
**not returning the plectrum after the strike**; in-tempo playing. The article also states that
**this school originated tsugaru-shamisen ensemble (合奏) playing.**
*Source: ja.wikipedia 小山流 — article carries a 出典の明記 tag, so [U] on details, but the 1977
notation date is independently confirmed by McGoldrick 2017, 233.* **[U — discrepancy:**
ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 says the **名取 (natori) system was introduced in 1967 (Shōwa 42)** by the
first Oyama Mitsugu, citing Daijō's 『汝なだば』; the 小山流 article puts the first natori at the 1963
founding.]
**[V]** A naming oddity worth knowing: "**Oyama Mitsugu II is actually referred to simply as Oyama
Mitsugu; his father, the original Oyama Mitsugu, took the name Oyama Mitsuo when he passed the
iemoto on to his son. While this practice is common in kabuki theatre, it is rare in the min'yō
world.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 62 n.17.* **Citations naming "Oyama Mitsugu" are therefore ambiguous.**

**[V] Mihashi Michiya (三橋美智也, 1930–1996) — the man who actually carried it to the nation.**
A hugely successful pop singer — **in 1983 the first Japanese singer to have sold over 100 million
records** — who had trained as a min'yō singer and **had studied tsugaru-shamisen with Shirakawa
Gunpachirō** before turning to popular song, then returned to min'yō in the late 1950s and **ignited
the first postwar min'yō boom**. Matsuki credits a specific, dateable event: **a two-night stand on
21 and 22 September 1959 at the Yūrakuchō Nihon Gekijō in Tokyo**, billed as commemorating "twenty
years in min'yō" for the then 29-year-old Mihashi, **featuring both Kida Rinshōei and Shirakawa
Gunpachirō on tsugaru-shamisen** — the introduction of the music to Tokyo and so to the rest of
Japan. Shirakawa was by then unwell, but **Kida had many subsequent opportunities on stage,
television and record with Mihashi, and it was through this that his tataki style became widely
known.** Midorikawa: "the performance style of the thick-necked instrument known as the Tsugaru
shamisen was introduced across Japan to min'yō fans, or at any rate the common people, by Mihashi
Michiya." *McGoldrick 2017, 193–194, citing Matsuki 2011, 143, Midorikawa 1995, 5, Groemer 2012, 92.*
**It was also Mihashi and his circle who popularised the very name:** ja.wikipedia says that during
the Shōwa-40s min'yō boom this music, previously called merely **「津軽もの」 ("Tsugaru stuff")**, was
dubbed **「津軽三味線」 by Mihashi Michiya and others, and the name stuck.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*
Corroborating the lateness of the term: **a 1956 scholarly anthology of Aomori folk songs speculates
about the tsugaru-shamisen's roots, distinguishes it from ordinary min'yō accompaniment, and lists
the major players — all without once using the phrase "Tsugaru shamisen."** *Kawanishi 1956, 253,
via McGoldrick 2017, 61 n.15.*

### Lineage claims that are disputed — summary

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| Nitabō as sole originator of the genre | **Rejected** by Groemer and most scholars; it is Daijō's reconstruction. *Groemer 2012, 60–68, via McGoldrick 2017, 16–17, 206* **[V]** |
| Nitabō as *a* significant early figure whose lineage many players trace | **Accepted.** *McGoldrick 2017, 16* **[V]** |
| Shirakawa as Nitabō's last pupil | Consistently attested (Kotobank, ja.wikipedia), but ultimately within the same oral tradition; Groemer notes Shirakawa "had studied under him for three years, after all" as a reason Nitabō "was not unknown." *McGoldrick 2017, 205* **[V]** |
| Kida as Shirakawa's pupil | **Denied** by the Shishidō account: never formally a pupil; learned by listening from the dressing room. **[U — single commercial source]** |
| Goze (Niigata/Echigo) as source of the genre | A "long-held belief," still argued in 2002 (Takeuchi Tsutomu, *Jongara to Echigo goze*); the *specific* Daijō version (Nitabō's mother a goze; direct instruction from a goze) is what Groemer attacks. *McGoldrick 2017, 16–17* **[V]** |
| Yamada Chisato founded the Hirosaki competition | **Overstated** — conceived by Aomori Television for its 25th anniversary; Yamada was the driving force. *McGoldrick 2017, 115–116* **[V]** |
| Yamada founded the Kanagi competition | **False.** Daijō Kazuo founded Kanagi in 1989. **[V]** |
| Kase no Momotarō as creator of the new way of playing | The position of the **1956 NHK anthology** and (in effect) of **Yamada 1978**. *McGoldrick 2017, 205–206* **[V]** |
| Chikuzan as Nitabō's pupil (common in English writing) | **False.** His teacher was **Toda Shigejirō/Jūjirō (戸田重次郎)**, a bosama of a neighbouring village. He is at least two steps removed from Nitabō. *ja.wikipedia 高橋竹山 / Ōshiro 2012; Kotobank* **[V]** |
| Nitabō as originator of **kyokubiki** | **Misattributed.** The Kotobank/Nichigai entry credits Nitabō with the **striking technique** and credits **Shirakawa Gunpachirō with establishing 曲弾き kyokubiki** and raising the instrument to parity with the voice. Matsuki likewise locates the origin of the solo style in Shirakawa's lengthening of song introductions in the 1930s. **Keep the two innovations separate.** **[V]** |

### A rival framing you should know about: the Japanese reference works do not start with Nitabō

**[V — kotobank.jp, fetched 2026-11-13 in this project's bibliographic pass; recorded in the
`shamisen` skill's `references/sources.md` §9. Not independently opened for this file.]** The Kotobank entry for
**津軽三味線** collates **デジタル大辞泉**, **精選版 日本国語大辞典** and **日本大百科全書
(Nipponica)** — and **none of the three mentions 仁太坊 or 坊様 at all.** Nipponica's lineage for the
style as it actually exists runs **Umeda Hōgetsu → Shirakawa Gunpachirō → Kida Rinshōei → Takahashi
Chikuzan** — a **twentieth-century** lineage beginning a generation *after* the supposed founder.

**This is the most useful single corrective in the whole file.** The standard Japanese reference
treatment is organised around **twentieth-century professionalisation**, not around a founding
individual. **To open an account of tsugaru-jamisen with Nitabō is to adopt Daijō Kazuo's framing
rather than the reference literature's.** That same pass notes that, stylistically, these entries
use **no hedging** for the twentieth-century material (plain passives, not という/とされる/説),
whereas the Edo-origins material *is* hedged — including the founder claim itself, where the
lineage is said 「行き着くとされる」 ("is *said to* trace back") to Nitabō. **The Japanese literature
is confident about the twentieth century and cautious about the origins — the inverse of how
English-language popular writing distributes its confidence.**

#### How to write the origin paragraph, honestly

A three-way reconciliation, not a founding story:

1. **A plural, undocumented *bosama* milieu** in late-Meiji Tsugaru, competing at festivals and
   horse fairs, out of which a loud, fast, percussive accompaniment style emerged. No individual can
   be shown to have originated it; English Wikipedia's own formulation is that "the specific
   developers of tsugaru-jamisen are not known with certainty," with "some consensus" that it was
   developed by the *bosama*. **[V]**
2. **The tradition traces itself to Nitabō**, and the Japanese dictionaries mark that as traditional
   attribution rather than fact. Groemer argues against the sole-originator thesis and shows an
   earlier source contradicting its key points. **[V]**
3. **Separable, better-documented innovations:** the **futozao + striking attack** to Nitabō
   (traditional attribution, dated by Daijō to 1877/1878); **kyokubiki, the solo art**, to
   **Shirakawa Gunpachirō** in the 1930s–40s. **[V]**
4. **The genre as a named, marketed category is mid-1950s.** See the dating problem immediately
   below.

#### When did "津軽三味線" become the name? Three datings, none fully reconciled

- **c. 1956**, beginning with a stage show featuring **Mihashi Michiya and Kida Rinshōei** —
  *Nipponica, Kotobank, bibliographic pass.* **[V as a report of Nipponica; U as to the
  show]**
- **September 1959**, the two-night Yūrakuchō Nihon Gekijō bill with **Kida *and Shirakawa*** —
  *Matsuki 2011, 143, via McGoldrick 2017, 193.* **[V]**
- **The Shōwa-40s (1965–74) min'yō boom**, when "Tsugaru stuff" (津軽もの) was renamed 津軽三味線
  "by Mihashi Michiya and others, and the name stuck" — *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **[V]**

What all three agree on, and what matters: **the name postdates the music by decades, and a 1956
scholarly anthology of Aomori folk song does not use it once** *(Kawanishi 1956, 253, via McGoldrick
2017, 61 n.15)*. **The category was assembled in the mid-to-late 1950s around players who were
already working — it is not a nineteenth-century genre that was later commercialised.**

#### And a market cause for the virtuosity that nobody mentions in English

**[V — Nipponica, Kotobank, bibliographic pass, 2026-11-13; not independently opened for this file]** Nipponica gives
**three** reasons the style spread: geography, performance conditions, and **the influence of
浪花節 (naniwa-bushi / rōkyoku)** — stating that **the 津軽三つ物 became popular as stand-ins for
rōkyoku from late Meiji to early Shōwa, and that this is what added the "show" element.** If that is
right, the virtuoso solo idiom is in part a **market response to a competing futozao narrative
genre**, and a standard reference work says so.

**[V — independent corroboration]** ja.wikipedia's 浪曲 article states that
**「津軽三味線の小原節には浪曲「壺坂霊験記」を取り入れた演題がある」** — there exists a *Tsugaru
Ohara-bushi* number that incorporates the rōkyoku piece *Tsubosaka Reigenki* — and lists
tsugaru-shamisen among the genres that spread nationally in rōkyoku's wake. *ja.wikipedia 浪曲,
read and translated for this file.* **The rōkyoku connection is real and is independently attested.**
**[V]** Chikuzan also **made a living accompanying naniwa-bushi for a time**, and a 1958 Chikuzan
naniwa-bushi performance survives as a bonus track on King's 2010 *Tamashi no hibiki*.
*McGoldrick 2017, 70–71 and n.20.*
