One methodological warning that recurs below. **Almost the entire popular history of this tradition
descends from one man's reconstruction**: Daijō Kazuo (大條和雄, b. 1928), a Hirosaki writer, not an
academic. Japanese Wikipedia's own 津軽三味線 article states it bluntly: Daijō was essentially the
first to collect and write down the oral traditions, and current understanding of the history "owes
much to his research results" — *but* "it too is no more than a reconstruction of oral tradition,
and because it contains a great deal of fictional element, its value as a historical source is low"
(「それもまたあくまで伝承を再構築したものに過ぎず、多分にフィクション的要素も含まれているため、
史料としての価値は低い」). **[V — ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線, read and translated for this file]**

## 7. The competition circuit and institutionalisation

### The precondition: min'yō contest culture, which is a century old

**[V]** The tsugaru-shamisen contests "are part of a larger **min'yō contest culture in modern Japan
that stretches back more than one hundred years.**" Dateable points: **an amateur singing contest at
the autumn festival on Mt. Iwaki as early as 1905**; **the first modern min'yō contest in 1910, for
the song "Esashi Oiwake" in Esashi, Hokkaidō**, "**partly influenced by the classical music
*concours* of Europe**" — and, unlike European concours, **devoted entirely to one piece**, a model
many Japanese min'yō contests still follow (the Esashi contest has run annually in its present form
since **1963**). **In 1934 the Tō-ō Nippō newspaper in Aomori began sponsoring a song contest**,
though by the late 1930s the war would have stopped such things altogether. **In 1948 NHK began the
*nodo jiman zenkoku konkūru*, which included a traditional music category; in 1950 the Japanese Folk
Song Association (日本民謡協会, "Nichimin") was established** and soon began its own concours.
Takeuchi Tsutomu's 1996 book on Japanese folk-song contests **estimated about 30,000 participants a
year** across the hundreds of them. **"These min'yō contests served as the model for Hirosaki's
Tsugaru shamisen National Contest, which was established in 1982."**
*McGoldrick 2017, 114–115, citing Suda, Daijō & Rausch 1998, 81; Hughes 2008, 134 and 142; Groemer
1999, 51; Takeuchi 1996, 9.* **The tsugaru-shamisen competition is a late graft onto an existing
Japanese institution, not an outgrowth of bosama rivalry — whatever the Kanagi marketing says.**

### 1. Hirosaki — 津軽三味線世界大会 (formerly 全国大会), since 1982

**[V — founding]** **Conceived by ATV (Aomori Television Broadcasting Company) for its
twenty-fifth-anniversary celebrations.** ATV "had some trouble getting it off the ground, and
**Yamada [Chisato], having organized Tsugaru min'yō singing contests in the past, stepped in to
help.**" **First held on 1 May 1982**, in conjunction with a Tsugaru min'yō singing contest, **as an
outdoor event on the grounds of Hirosaki Castle Park**; the day was a national holiday coinciding
with the park's cherry-blossom festival, in the hope that sightseers would swell the audience.
**There were only nineteen players at the first contest.** *McGoldrick 2017, 116, citing Matsuki
2011, 95.* **Judges at the first contest included Yamada Chisato; Daijō Kazuo; and Terashima Katsu, a
prewar min'yō performer.** *McGoldrick 2017, 124, citing Matsuki.*

**[V — growth and institutionalisation, dated]**
- **By its fifth year (1986): 140 competitors.** **1999: 274 competitors.**
- **1984:** moved across Hirosaki Castle Park to a field by the Citizens' Auditorium;
  **late 1980s:** moved indoors into the auditorium itself, where it remains.
- **1988: a group category introduced, greatly increasing participation — "it seems that this ensured
  the contest's survival."** **It ran in the red for at least its first four years.**
- **1999:** became a two-day event.
- **2001:** for the first time, **the audience was asked to hold applause until the end of a
  performance**, against "the long-standing audience practice of clapping during the most difficult
  passages, but allowing the judges to clearly hear each player's execution of these passages."
- **2005:** a rule that **only players affiliated with a recognised school could enter** (since
  rescinded).
- **2003:** run by the **NPO 21 津軽三味線ネットワークジャパン (21 Tsugaru-jamisen Network Japan)**,
  founded that year, which "seems to have replaced the Aomori Prefecture Arts and Culture Research
  Group (青森県芸能文化研究会), which was made up of many of the same members."
- **2014:** the **33rd** edition was **renamed the World Championship (世界大会)** and drew overseas
  entrants.
- **2026:** the **44th**, held **3–4 May**, at **Hirosaki Shimin Kaikan** — an edition count
  consistent with a 1982 start and one lost year (almost certainly 2020).

*Sources: McGoldrick 2017, 116–119 (all pre-2016 points, citing Matsuki 2011, 96–98 and Asahō 1986);
ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 (founding year, organiser); `tsugaru-shamisen.jp` and
`tsugaru-shamisen.jp/about/` for the 2014 rename, the 44th edition and the 2026 dates, fetched
2026-11-13.*

**[V — a reversal worth noting]** McGoldrick on the atmospheric change: the 2001 and 2005 moves "go
against the contest's early rebellious reputation, **where affiliation was ignored, and exceptional
playing was rewarded with instant applause, while sub-par playing could prompt hecklers to tell the
player to 'go home.'** In my experiences in the mid to late 1990s, when Hirosaki was the proving
ground for young players, there was a charged atmosphere in the hall and in the lobby… today the
atmosphere seems more welcoming and friendly." *McGoldrick 2017, 118.*

**[V — the 2026 divisions, verbatim from the organiser]**

*Individual (個人戦):* **個人A級** (advanced, any age, **3 min 30 s**) · **個人B級** (intermediate,
any age, **3 min**) · **唄付伴奏A級** (utazuke accompaniment, intermediate–advanced, by
recommendation; **set piece 津軽じょんから節 新旧節**; tuning and intro within 1 min) ·
**唄付伴奏B級** (beginner–intermediate, by recommendation; **set piece 津軽じょんから新節**).
*Age-based beginner divisions (C級), all **2 min 30 s**:* **ジュニアC級** (15 and under) ·
**ユースC級** (16–39) · **ミドルC級** (40–59) · **シニアC級** (60+).
*Group (団体戦), both **4 min**:* **Aグループ** (11+ players) · **Bグループ** (5–10).
All pieces are based on **津軽民謡**, and the individual pieces are **曲弾き**. Individual B and C
entries may be combined with utazuke and group entries; **唄付伴奏A級 and 個人A級 cannot both be
entered.** *Source: `tsugaru-shamisen.jp/about/`, fetched 2026-11-13.*

**[V — audience and entry economics]** Audience: **¥2,000 one day / ¥3,500 both days** (elementary
and under ¥1,000/¥2,000; preschoolers free; half price for disability-certificate holders plus one
companion); **tickets sold only at the venue on the day, no advance sales**; free parking. Entry
(as of McGoldrick's fieldwork): **¥5,000 for a solo entrant**, about **¥2,000 per person for
groups**. Funding comes from entry and audience fees, listed sponsors, programme advertising, and
lobby merchandise booths. **Sponsors grew from 11 in 1997 to 32 in 2010**; the 1997 list was the
Aomori Prefecture Arts and Culture Research Group, Hirosaki City, the Hirosaki City Tourist
Association, NHK Hirosaki, **Tō-ō Nippō**, Michinoku Shimbun, **RAB, ATV, ABA**, **Nippon Columbia**,
and one local business — the Akimoto liquor store. Programme advertisements: 55 in 1997, 59 in 2010,
ranging from instrument makers (10) and min'yō pubs (3) to "car dealerships, insurance companies,
**gravestone carvers**, and **insecticide producers**." *McGoldrick 2017, 119–121;
`tsugaru-shamisen.jp`, 2026 figures.*

**[V — the discrimination problem, two kinds]**
*Gender:* at the 2015 awards ceremony the men's A-level winner received a trophy "**at least three
feet tall**," a certificate, **a winner's flag kept for the year**, and two envelopes, while the
women's A-level winner received an envelope, a certificate and a cup "**distinguished from the other
trophies by its shape**." McGoldrick: "The size of the men's trophy and the presentation of the
winner's flag make it clear that **the winner of the men's A-level is the over-all winner of the
contest; as it stands now, a woman simply cannot win the contest.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 112–113.*
Participation has nonetheless equalised: **in 1998, the first year with a separate women's category,
there were 51 men across A and B and only 18 women; today "the numbers are fairly evenly split."**
*McGoldrick 2017, 180–181.*
*Nationality:* the 2010 programme announced a **"foreigner" category for 2011**, on the stated
rationale that non-Japanese players were increasing and were disadvantaged by having to study alone
abroad. **"The non-Japanese who were present at the 2010 contest, however, saw it as a ghettoizing
action and argued that it should be dropped, which it was.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 122–123.*

### 2. Kanagi — 津軽三味線全日本金木大会, since 1989 — founded AGAINST Hirosaki

**[V — this is Daijō's contest, not Yamada's]** **The first 津軽三味線全日本金木大会 was held on
4 May 1989, on an outdoor stage on the grounds of Ashino prefectural park in Kanagi**, and stayed
there until 2002 except for four rainouts into the Kanagi elementary school gymnasium. **In 2003 it
moved permanently indoors to the Kanagi Town Citizens' Hall and became a two-day event on the
national holidays of 4 and 5 May.** *McGoldrick 2017, 130–131, citing Kon et al. 2009, 2 and Kanagi
Genki Club NPO 2010.* **As of the 39th edition it is held at the 津軽三味線会館 itself, on 3–4 July
2027** — the dates have moved out of Golden Week since COVID. *`kanagi-gc.net/genkon/`, fetched
2026-11-13.*

**[V — Daijō Kazuo, b. 1928]** Raised in a wealthy Hirosaki family; heard stories about the
wandering shamisen players from his mother as a child. **In 1959, as a young writer, his interest
revived when Kida Rinshōei moved into his neighbourhood**; since almost no information existed, he
began interviewing players in Hirosaki, asking each who he had learned from and then asking those
players in turn, by which "he claims that he was able to trace the lineages back to Nitabō."
**McGoldrick's damaging note: "according to a few sources who would rather remain anonymous on this
point, Daijō found a lot of information in those interviews from the early 1960s that he has not
shared, likely because it contradicts his Nitabō narrative."**
*McGoldrick 2017, 131–132, citing Suda et al. 1998, 121–122.* **[V as McGoldrick's report; the
anonymous-sources claim is inherently [U].]**

**[V — the break]** Daijō "was also involved in Hirosaki's contest from very early on, in an
organizational capacity and as a head judge. **He left this contest to found his own in Kanagi,
motivated, he has written, by what he saw as sponsorship irregularities which he felt compromised
the integrity of the 1988 Hirosaki contest** (Daijō 2000, 104). **Others have suggested, however,
that he started the contest due to other unspecified difficulties with Yamada Chisato** (Matsuki
2011, 98)." *McGoldrick 2017, 132–133.*

**[V — and the stated critique was precisely about judging capture]** In a 1990 article Daijō
"distanced himself and his contest from the modern, flashy Tsugaru shamisen that was being played in
Hirosaki, stating that **he was not a performer, but that he played bosama-style shamisen, and poorly
at that**. He went on to say that he had started this contest for the advancement of Tsugaru
shamisen, **as a place where contestants would be judged only on their own merits, regardless of
affiliation**, and that **he was not the head of his own school, and had no students (and so, unlike
Yamada, no vested interest in who won). This was partly a justification for his contest and partly a
criticism of Hirosaki's judging irregularities that had seen one of Yamada's students win almost
every year to that point.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 133, citing Daijō 2000, 132.*

**[V — and the myth is the branding]** Daijō sited it in Kanagi because of Nitabō; holding it
outdoors for its first fourteen years "was surely also meant to evoke the image of itinerant bosama
playing at the gates of people's homes or on temple grounds." **"While Hirosaki's contest had never
focused heavily on the history of the music, insisting instead that it was a living art form, Daijō
sought from the beginning to connect Kanagi's contest to the music's origins."** **The first-place
award is called the 仁太坊賞 (Nitabō Award), and Nitabō is referenced in all promotional material.**
*McGoldrick 2017, 133–134.* The current masthead carries the slogan **「人真似でね、汝の三味線を
弾け！」** and the banner **「仁太坊生誕１７０年記念」**; the companion 仁太坊まつり, in 2027 its
**32nd** edition, is an **exhibition by the competition's top placers** on the Kaikan's **outdoor
stage**, free to attend, and is justified by "the **historical fact** that Nitabō of Kanbara… went
around playing at various places in Kanagi during his lifetime." *`kanagi-gc.net/genkon/` and
`/genkon_1.html`, `/genkon_3.html`, fetched 2026-11-13.*

**[V — divisions, 39th edition]** **個人・小学生以下の部** (elementary and under) ·
**個人・中高生の部** (middle/high school) · **個人・シニアの部** (senior) ·

**[V — scale and function]** Kanagi has always been smaller than Hirosaki: **2010: 182 solo players
and 21 groups; 2015: 152 solo and 24 groups**; in 2006 there were 41 in A-level, in 2010 fewer, a
problem partly addressed by a **category added in 2009 for players with five or fewer years'
experience**. **"Kanagi has always been open to anyone"** — no affiliation requirement.
**"Winning in Kanagi often seems to be a step towards the ultimate goal of winning in Hirosaki,
Tokyo, or, more recently, Aomori."** Only **two women** had won Kanagi at the time of writing:
**Nishimura Shinobu in 2005 at seventeen and Takeda Kanami in 2011**. Award inflation: **in 2010
every group that entered went away with an award.** *McGoldrick 2017, 135–141.*
**Agatsuma, who won the inaugural contest at fifteen, said at the time that his aim was to be the
best in Japan.** *Honma 2001, 109, via McGoldrick 2017, 141.*

**[V — the geopolitics of the circuit, which is the real story]** The contests were founded in
explicit rivalry, and each has had to differentiate itself:
- **Hirosaki** relied, "once its dominance was contested," on **location and long history**.
- **Kanagi** on **Nitabō** — "lineage-based authenticity" — plus, latterly, the knockout tournament.
- **Tokyo (Nichimin, 1998)** on **"the strong support of the most influential names in Tsugaru
  shamisen, and its strong adjudication"** — founded, like Kanagi, partly in criticism of Hirosaki's
  judging. **2001 is the year "at which Tokyo's contest began to challenge Hirosaki's dominance,"**
  when several top players were absent from Hirosaki.
- **Osaka** on **fair adjudication and an inclusive mass performance**; it "emphasized modern
  playing and skewed very young."
- **Aomori City (2007)** by **raising the bar on repertoire**: A-level competitors must master
  **both kyokubiki and utazuke versions of all five great songs**.
*McGoldrick 2017, 118, 171, 183–184, 231.*

**[V — the prize economy]** "In general the contests seem to have settled on a strategy of offering
**a large number of awards relative to the number of contestants** as a way to motivate players…
**in general one in four solo entrants, and one in three groups, receives an award.** Most of the
awards are symbolic… **even when actual cash prizes are offered, they are insignificant when
compared to the costs of travel and lodging incurred in attending the contest.** Instead, winning a
low-level category, or placing at any level serves to motivate players to improve their playing, and
**winning a national title is often the first step to becoming a professional player.**"
*McGoldrick 2017, 153, 182.*

**[V — scale of the whole circuit]** McGoldrick estimates **300–400 national solo competitors in a
given year**, "not much higher than 300, and certainly less than 400," against a player population
he puts at perhaps 20,000 (§8). Many *ryūha* run their own internal competitions that feed the
national ones. For comparison he notes that **international Irish music competitions draw competitors
"in the tens of thousands each year."** *McGoldrick 2017, 92–93, 180–182, citing Hayashi 2007, 2 and
Henry 1989, 69.*

**[V — the age profile of winners]** "**In general the winners of the contests tend to be young,
with the average age in the early to mid-twenties. The youngest winner ever was fourteen**, but
winners in their mid- to late teens are not uncommon, while **very few contestants have won beyond
their early forties. The reason for this is that older, established players would have little to
gain from winning, while losing could damage their reputation.**" Outliers: **Kobe skews very young
(oldest winner 27); Aomori, which demands mastery of a large repertoire, skews older (oldest winner
54, average winner 36).** At Kanagi the average winner's age is **23**; at Osaka **24** (youngest
Shibata Masato at 16 in 2003, oldest Habu Misao at 39 in 2012). *McGoldrick 2017, 136, 153, 182–183.*

**[V — serial winning, and the three-wins-and-out rule]** **Shibutani Kazuo was the first to win
Hirosaki three times in a row, at which point he was forced to retire from the contest; five others
have since done it.** With the proliferation of contests the goal shifted to **collecting titles
across venues**: **Shibata Masato retired in 2012 with twelve championships — Tokyo, Osaka, Kobe,
Nagoya, three times in Kanagi, five times in Hirosaki.** **Kasai Yoshiyuki had six at four different
contests as of 2015.** *McGoldrick 2017, 183.* **The Kanagi roll corroborates Shibata's three Kanagi
wins (18th–20th editions) and Kasai's (22nd).**

### The honest critique — and the one controlled study that complicates it

#### The case that competition has pushed the music toward speed and uniformity

**[V — the strongest formulation, and it is McGoldrick's own]** "Contest pieces are kyokubiki…
'Composition' here largely involves **arranging standard riffs within a fairly rigid structure, and
is actually far less important than the execution of the piece.** Speed, accuracy, tone, and the
ability to execute and seamlessly combine standard riffs and techniques are all among the criteria on
which competitors are judged. As mentioned above, C-level beginners have two-and-a-half minutes, and
advanced A-level players have four minutes to demonstrate their mastery of this checklist of
techniques, **so there is very little room for personal expression.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 123.*

**[V — the insiders' verdict, with a quotable phrase]** "**The standardization, homogenization, and
loss of variation that Hill and Bithell say comes with audio recording also tend to be the result of
competitions. The trend of 'shamisen without heart,' that Daijō Kazuo noted in 1999 only became more
pronounced with the upsurge in players that occurred during the millennial revival** (Daijō 1999).
As was noted in chapter four, **a common observation concerning the contests is that there are more
technically proficient players than ever, but that everyone sounds the same.**"
*McGoldrick 2017, 234.* **「心のない三味線」/"shamisen without heart" is Daijō's phrase, from 1999 —
i.e. the critique comes from the man who founded a competition.**

**[V — and a documented case of the circuit punishing repertoire diversity]** At Hirosaki the set
requirement is only "Tsugaru folk music," but **almost all entrants choose Jongara-bushi, as the most
technically rich and the easiest to show off with.** **Agatsuma instead competed in 1996 and 1997
with 津軽よされ節 — plainer than Jongara, but reckoned the hardest of the five great songs — weaving
in the advanced technique called 「四枚撥」 (shimai-bachi), and won with overwhelming polish. In 1998,
going for a third consecutive win with Yosare-bushi, he lost to Odori Shōtarō; the judging drew
criticism and contributed to the jibe that the Hirosaki competition is a contest run for the benefit
of a particular school.** Afterwards **nobody won with anything other than Jongara-bushi until Kasai
Yoshiyuki won with a sansagari piece.** *Source: ja.wikipedia 上妻宏光 — **this article carries a
"BLP unsourced" banner, so [U]**, but it is a specific, checkable, internally coherent account and
the only documented instance I found of the repertoire-narrowing dynamic in action.*
**[U — note the article also conflicts with the organiser's own winner roll on Agatsuma's Kanagi
win: it says 1989 at 15 years 10 months, which matches `kanagi-gc.net`; English Wikipedia's "1988,
aged 14" is wrong.]**

**[V — the circuit's own folklore of its failings]** ja.wikipedia states that although competitions
are judged on ability, "**unlike the iemoto system in the schools**" — which is the circuit's own
justification — "there are also many problems: **vague judging criteria; the fact that, because of
the run of consecutive contests, the top places get locked up by the same regulars, making it hard
for later entrants to place highly**." And: **the colour of the judging criteria differs by contest —
"Osaka is speed-playing; Hirosaki is strongly influenced by Yamada Chisato and favours playing that
follows the song" — and the colour of the same contest often changes over the years**, so entrants
need to check carefully. **Every one of these statements is tagged 要出典 ("citation needed") in the
article. [U]** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.*

**[V — judging opacity, corroborated]** At Hirosaki, **criteria are published nowhere**; and of
Yamada's own contest, "**上位入賞者が山田の門下生で占められたり、審査員やその採点内容などが一切
公表されないこともあった**" — top places were monopolised by Yamada's own students, and the judges
and their scoring were sometimes **never published at all**; the article says the running has since
passed to a separate body and "the original tendency has retreated into the shadows."
*ja.wikipedia 山田千里 (tagged 出典の明記, so **[U]**) and McGoldrick 2017, 123 (for the absence of
published criteria, **[V]**).* McGoldrick also notes of Kanagi that **"only two of the seven judges
could be said to have any kind of professional connection to Tsugaru shamisen,"** and that **"both
Hirosaki and Kanagi have been criticized"** on that ground. *McGoldrick 2017, 134–135.* **[V]**
Since **2014** Hirosaki discards high and low scores and averages the rest. *McGoldrick 2017, 125.*

#### The case on the other side — and please do not skip this

**1. The anti-imitation ethic is enforced, not merely recited.** **[V]** Chiba, an A-class champion:
"In all of the competitions, the music to be performed is largely determined by 'Tsugaru
Jongara-bushi', **but not everyone plays it the same way.** The famous words of Nitabō… **have been
passed down from generation to generation, and solo performances (kyokubiki) with original
arrangements that do not break the flow and rhythm of the music, are still required at
competitions.**" He also reports two deliberate liberalisations: **some competitions now seat
guitarists and koto players as judges alongside professional tsugaru-shamisen players**, so that
"having other musicians judge the non-traditional aspects of the performance allows for a
multifaceted evaluation"; and although kimono is traditional, **"at most competitions the performers
are allowed to perform in clothing of their own choice. This allows them to perform in their own
unique style."** *Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 6.* McGoldrick saw the same at Hirosaki 2015: a
group "dressed in markedly different ways, underlining their individuality." *McGoldrick 2017, 108.*

**2. The competitions have legislated the *harder*, song-accompanying skill back in.** See §4 of `repertoire-and-technique.md`: the
utazuke divisions with set pieces at Hirosaki, the utazuke requirement at Kanagi, the ten-piece
demand at Aomori. **Structurally, the circuit is the main institution currently fighting the decline
of utazuke.** **[V]**

**3. They built the player base.** **[V]** "From the late 1980s, **the contests were key in
developing a large base of participatory fans**"; "these contests nurtured young players and **helped
to gradually increase the number of Tsugaru shamisen players across Japan**"; and the whole
millennial commercial boom "would have been impossible without the participant-driven but much
smaller boom that was happening in the 1980s and 1990s" — **"the participatory revival that gained
momentum in the 1980s and 1990s provided the performers for the mass-mediated millennial revival,
which in turn generated new participants."** *McGoldrick 2017, 18, 199, 235.*

**4. THE CONTROLLED STUDY — and it points against the easy cynical reading.**
**[V — bibliographic record verified by this project's bibliographic pass via Crossref; I have
not read the paper]** **Chiba Gakuto, Ozaki Yuto, Fujii Shinya & Patrick E. Savage, "Sight vs. Sound
Judgments of Music Performance Depend on Relative Performer Quality: Cross-cultural Evidence From
Classical Piano and Tsugaru Shamisen Competitions," *Collabra: Psychology* 9/1 (2023),
DOI 10.1525/collabra.73641 — a Stage 2 Registered Report** (Stage 1 at *Peer Community In Registered
Reports*, 2021; preprint DOI 10.31234/osf.io/xky4j). Design and findings as reported to me:
**n = 155 Japanese participants; 40 performers drawn from 10 piano and 10 shamisen competitions;
6-second video-only versus audio-only excerpts.** The predicted **domain × quality-variance
interaction was confirmed for both piano and shamisen**; but **"visuals trump sound when variance is
low" held only for PIANO**, and **"sound trumps visuals when variance is high" held only for
SHAMISEN.** **The visual-dominance effect found at the top of piano competitions did not replicate
for shamisen**, and because the study was preregistered, **those nulls are meaningful.**

**How to use this.** The "vague criteria, showmanship over sound" complaint is, in the sources I
reached, **unsourced folklore of the scene** (the ja.wikipedia passage is explicitly 要出典), while
the **one preregistered experiment on tsugaru-shamisen judging found listeners weighting sound over
visuals** where performer quality varied, and found **no visual-dominance effect at the top of the
field.** **State the critique as the scene's own self-criticism — which is exactly what gives it its
force, since it comes from Daijō and from Kinoshita — and do not let it stand as a finding.** Chiba's
own reaction is worth quoting as the honest middle: having designed the study, he found that
**"visual information did play a role, and that role increased as the difference in quality between
the performers decreased. As someone who has learned the importance of auditory information from the
history and practice of Tsugaru shamisen, this result came as a shock to me."**
*Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 6.* **[V]**

#### The irony, stated plainly

The tradition's constitutive maxim is **「人真似でね、汝の三味線を弾け」** — and it is the **motto on
the masthead of a competition**. The man who put it there, Daijō, is also the man who in 1999 named
the result **"shamisen without heart."** **[V]** Both halves of that are documented and the
contradiction is not resolved by anyone. What *is* documented is that the competitions have made more
people play this music than ever before (§8) while narrowing what any one of them plays — and that
the tradition is arguing with itself about the trade in public.

## 8. The modern era and the commercial breakthrough

### The 1970s Chikuzan boom — and what actually drove it

See §3 of `tradition-and-lineage.md` for the sequence. The three mechanisms, in order of causal weight as the sources give them:
**(i)** the **1963 King LP**, which sold **70,000 against a genre norm of 3,000–4,000** and "tapped
into a new record-buying audience"; **(ii)** the **Rō-on** workers' music-appreciation circuit from
**1964** — 220 chapters, 650,000 members — which gave him attentive concert audiences for the first
time; **(iii)** the **1971 RAB documentary 『寒撥』** and the **1973 start of monthly concerts at
Shibuya Jean-Jean**, where he played until 1995. The peak was the **mid-1970s**: "multiple
recordings and media appearances, constant touring, a best-selling autobiography, and a full-length
motion picture about his life. **Thanks to his high media profile, for most Japanese of that time
period, Takahashi Chikuzan was synonymous with Tsugaru shamisen.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 18, 194–195,
211–212.* **[V]**

**[V — the shape of the 1970s audience, which differs from the 2000s]** "**As noted above, young
people with no interest in min'yō began listening to Chikuzan at this time**" *(Yamada 1978, 87)*,
and McGoldrick stresses that "**a significant part of his fan base was new listeners**."
*McGoldrick 2017, 198–199.* Alan Booth's 1995 travelogue *Looking for the Lost* records the 1970s
boom "that saw Takahashi Chikuzan playing to college-age audiences in the hip Shibuya district," and
also records **Yamada Chisato telling him in Hirosaki, of the early 1990s: "no one wants to record me
now… it's not like the old days."** *Booth 1995, 66 and 69, via McGoldrick 2017, 78.* **[V]**

**[V — the 1970s crossover experiments, which are usually forgotten]** Already in that decade:
Chikuzan's own experiments; **Yamada Chisato playing with the jazz drummer Elvin Jones (New York,
1982) and with a Hungarian folk group (1976)**; **Takahashi Yūjirō and others recording
tsugaru-shamisen pieces with orchestral accompaniment**; and a player with the band Count Buffalo.
"These musical collaborations had a minimal effect on the style of traditional playing, but they
contributed to an atmosphere which allowed for further experimentation." *McGoldrick 2017, 211,
citing Matsuki 2011, 145 and Booth 1995, 71.*

**[V — and the 1980s ensemble turn, which is the bridge]** ja.wikipedia: "**In the 1980s, led by
Takahashi Yūjirō's group 「風」(Kaze), concerts in ensemble (合奏団) format aimed at a general
audience came to be performed as stage art, and the role of the tsugaru-shamisen turned from
supporting player to lead.**" *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 — the Kaze attribution is tagged 要出典, so
**[U]** on that specific, **[V]** on the 1980s ensemble-concert turn, which McGoldrick corroborates
via the 津軽三味線協議会's mass-performance "festivals" of the same period.* **1999: 841 people
performed together at the Tokyo Dome.** *Matsuki 2011, 148, via McGoldrick 2017, 235.*

### The millennial breakthrough — the Yoshida Brothers

**[V — who they are]** **吉田良一郎 Yoshida Ryōichirō, b. 26 July 1977**, and **吉田健一 Yoshida
Ken'ichi, b. 16 December 1979**, brothers **from Noboribetsu, Hokkaidō — not from Tsugaru.** Both
began shamisen at **five**; **from 1990 they studied tsugaru-shamisen under 初代・佐々木孝 (Sasaki
Takashi I)**. They "came to prominence at the Tsugaru-shamisen National Competition in Hirosaki and
elsewhere," and **made their major-label debut in 1999 with the album 『いぶき』 *Ibuki***.
*ja.wikipedia 吉田兄弟.* English Wikipedia adds an earlier teacher,
**足立コカ Adachi Koka, from age five, for min'yō-shamisen**, before Sasaki from about 1989. **[U]**
**Ken'ichi won the Kanagi Nitabō Award twice, at the 10th and 11th editions (1998 and 1999).**
*`kanagi-gc.net/genkon/genkon_2.html`.* **[V]**

**[V — the pre-debut exposure]** **"On December 31st, 1997 Ryōichirō and Kenichi Yoshida appeared as

1998, and the subsequent mass media promotion of… the Yoshida Brothers**," who were thereby "in a
position to benefit from the many media tributes that appeared in the wake of Chikuzan's death." And

in middle age, they were being reintroduced to it by these polite young boys.**"
*McGoldrick 2017, 19, 196–197.* **[V]**

### Kinoshita Shin'ichi, and the rest of the crossover scene

**[V]** **Kinoshita Shin'ichi (b. 1965)** — see §3. His significance in the commercial era is that he

**1999** he recurrently partnered the Hungarian violinist **Roby Lakatos**. Between **2001 and 2004**
he released two traditional albums, three hybrid albums, and the instructional book. *ja.wikipedia

**biographical panel: Kase no Momo**; (8) **biographical panel: Nitabō**. There are **live shamisen
performances** and a tourism video, and it **hosts the annual All-Japan Kanagi competition**.
**Admission (individual / group of 20+): general ¥600 / ¥500; high school and university ¥400 /
¥300; elementary and junior high ¥250 / ¥200.** A **combined ticket with the 斜陽館 Shayōkan**

### The current state — is the player base growing or shrinking?

**I found two careful estimates that point in opposite directions. Report both; they are measuring
different things.**

#### The figures, such as they are

**[V]** McGoldrick assembles them and is openly exasperated:

| Year | Estimate | Source |
|---|---|---|
| 1986 | **20,000–30,000** players | Asashō 1986; Daijō 1998, 36 |
| 1998 | **50,000** players | Suda, Daijō & Rausch 1998, 11 |
| 1999 | **50,000–60,000** players | Daijō 1999 |

His footnote: "**This is particularly confusing as the number of players has undoubtedly grown
substantially in the last few decades. For whatever reason, it seems difficult to arrive at a
reliable figure, even for the number of schools.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 92–93 and n.31.*
**Treat all of these as advocacy numbers produced by interested parties. Do not average them.**

**[V — the one hard contextual number]** "A recent newspaper article notes that '**According to a
white paper on leisure, the number of enthusiasts of traditional Japanese musical instruments
declined from 3.3 million in 1990 to 1.2 million in 2008**,' or **less than one percent of the
population**." *"Simplified Shamisen" 2016, via McGoldrick 2017, 199.* **[V as his citation; [U] as
to the white paper itself, which I did not reach.]**

#### Reading A — growing (McGoldrick, as of 2016)

**[V]** "**It is all the more striking that the number of Tsugaru shamisen players continued to grow
in this period when the overall number of traditional Japanese musicians has drastically shrunk. It
is impossible to say what will happen in the future, but it is clear that the music is being played
by more people today than ever before.**" His account of the mechanism: the mass-media boom
**waned around 2007**, and "**in 2016, while the general public has lost interest in Tsugaru shamisen,
many of these new participants have continued to play. A small number of players who came up through
the contest system still manage to make a living as touring performers, and a much larger number
perform and teach.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 199–200.* Supporting observations: instrument sellers
reported **increased tsugaru-shamisen sales in the mid-to-late 1990s and again in the early 2000s**;
by **2003** guitar shops in Tokyo and Osaka were selling cheap low-quality shamisen aimed at people
with no prior connection to the music; a specialty magazine, ***Bachi-Bachi*, ran July 2004 to
September 2007**; and a YouTube search on 津軽三味線 "produced **103,000 hits** in early 2016."
*McGoldrick 2017, 235–236, citing Tanaka & Oda 2009, 174.* **[V]**

#### Reading B — shrinking sharply (Chiba & Savage, as of 2022)

**[V — and this is the newest datum I have]** Their Figure 1 gives **estimated numbers of
**Tsugaru-shamisen *and* folk-song performers**, 1982 versus 2022, as "the approximate number of
people known to the **日本郷土民謡協会 (Nihon Kyōdo Min'yō Kyōkai)** and **日本民謡協会 (Nihon Min'yō
Kyōkai)**, the largest organizations representing Japanese folk music," with the caveat that **no
specific data were kept.** I retrieved the underlying values from the authors' own analysis script
(`github.com/gakuto101207/bookchapter`, `Population_1982-2022.R`, dated 2022-10-03):

| | Association members | Other / independent | **Total** |
|---|---|---|---|
| **1982** | **80,000** | **40,000** | **120,000** |
| **2022** | **16,000** | **10,000** | **26,000** |

**An estimated decline of roughly 78%, from about 120,000 to about 26,000, over forty years.**
*Source: Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint Figure 1, and the authors' R script, both read and translated for this file.*
**[V]** Their own framing: "for those who have experienced folk music for many years, **it is clear
that these genres are in decline.**" *preprint p. 2.*

**Reconciling the two.** They are not strictly contradictory. Chiba & Savage count **tsugaru-shamisen
*and min'yō singing together*, as known to two national associations** — and §3 of `tradition-and-lineage.md` and §4 of `repertoire-and-technique.md` document that
**min'yō singing**, and with it the whole *utazuke* function, is exactly the part that has collapsed.
McGoldrick counts **tsugaru-shamisen players**, many of whom (as ja.wikipedia says) **never join a
min'yō association at all**, "because the recognition that Tsugaru stuff and Tsugaru min'yō are
something of a different kind from ordinary min'yō — the instrument itself is literally a different
thing — is widely held at least among players, and because **demand for song accompaniment is
decreasing**." *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **The defensible synthesis: the solo tsugaru-shamisen
player base grew through the 1990s and 2000s and has probably plateaued or begun to fall; the
min'yō-singing ecology that gave the music its original function has shrunk drastically; and
association membership, which is the only thing anyone counts, tracks the latter.** *(This synthesis
is mine, from three verified sources, and I flag it as mine.)*

#### Age profile

**[V — audiences are old]** "At a Yoshida Brothers concert I attended in 2001 in Kyoto, **I estimated
that ninety percent of the audience was over sixty. I came to learn that this was typical**, and that
**for most of the postwar period, it has been this older demographic that supports traditional
music.** The actual financial supporters of this music were aging traditional music enthusiasts, many
of whom had been fans of Chikuzan in the 1970s… **Neither my estimations of audience demographics at
concerts, nor my extensive informal sampling of the general population, suggest that Japan's young
people were drawn to the music in the numbers that they were in the 1970s revival.**"
*McGoldrick 2017, 198–199.* **[V]**

**[V — and a sharp corrective to the Western literature]** "**While this millennial revival did make
the Yoshida Brothers a household name, the popularity of Tsugaru shamisen, particularly among young
people, has been overstated in recent writings by some Western researchers (Peluse 2005; Johnson
2005, 2006, 2009).**" *McGoldrick 2017, 19.* And the field observation behind it: of hundreds of
people he spoke to over fifteen years, "**almost every one… had heard of the Yoshida Brothers, knew
the term 'Tsugaru shamisen,' and recognized the characteristic sound of the music, but outside of
the people connected to traditional music, as players or long-time fans, almost no one admitted to
having bought even one Tsugaru shamisen CD.** … **The millennial revival… was successful in making
the whole country aware of the musical tradition again, but seems to have brought far fewer new fans
to the music**" than the 1970s one. *McGoldrick 2017, 197–198.* **If you are writing a reference
document, this is the paragraph that should govern the tone of §8.**

**[V — players are young, at the top]** Against the old audiences: competition winners average their
early-to-mid twenties, the youngest ever being fourteen (§7), and the contests "**included a large
contingent of young people**." *McGoldrick 2017, 199.* **The demographic shape is therefore an
hourglass: young competitors, elderly payers, and a thin middle.**

**[V — the pipeline, and why it is leaking]** The **1998 revision of the 学習指導要領 (Courses of
Study) mandated the teaching of Japanese musical instruments in junior high school**, with
instructions to use at least one at some point across the three years — but "**few schools were able
to actively incorporate traditional Japanese music and instruments in their classes because teaching
lacked such skills and there were few teaching materials**," and "the Western-centred trend has
extended to contemporary Japan, where music classes in both primary and secondary education
predominantly focus on the performance of Western instruments." *Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 4,
citing MEXT 1998.* McGoldrick, on the same mandate: "I have had many young Japanese tell me that
they learned about the Tsugaru shamisen in school, but have heard from many others that they did
not." *McGoldrick 2017, 235, citing de Ferranti 2002, 198.* **[V]**

**[V — and the ground-level picture, from inside a training hall]** Chiba, who began as a small child
in Asahikawa, Hokkaidō, under **Kanno Kōzan**: "I still vividly remember the scene of being
surrounded by elderly apprentices, a few of whom were of my generation or my parents' generation…
**Nowadays, I know few apprentices, and the training hall has become neglected. This is partly due
to the declining birth rate and aging population, the educational problems described above, and the
rising cost of musical instruments because of a shortage of resources and makers. But it is also due
to the difficulty of learning because of the constraints of tradition.**"
*Chiba & Savage 2023, preprint p. 5.* **[V]** **That is the honest answer on the current state: a
dense, highly skilled, competition-fed elite sitting on top of a thinning base, in a country with
fewer children, fewer singers, fewer instrument makers and no dogskin.**

**[V — two further structural facts]** (i) **The centre of gravity has left Tsugaru.** "Since the
min'yō boom, many first-rate players have moved to Tokyo from Tsugaru **and Hakodate**, and **today
Tokyo has greater depth than Aomori, the home ground.**" *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **Hakodate as a
second historical centre is usually omitted in English writing.** (ii) **The school system is weak
and getting weaker.** ja.wikipedia: compared with classical hōgaku such as nihon-buyō and nagauta,
schools are "treated lightly," because the music is improvisational at root so there is little need
to preserve the identity of a fixed art; because the history is shallow; because **no organisation
unifies the tsugaru-shamisen schools**; because one can get by without belonging to one; and because
**「職格（名取、師範等の資格）より大会の入賞歴の方が重視される傾向にある」 — a record of placing at
competitions tends to count for more than professional rank (natori, shihan).** Three
cross-school bodies coexist — **「21津軽三味線ネットワークジャパン」「全国津軽三味線協議会」
「全日本津軽三味線友の会」** — but "**since the benefit to members is slight, it cannot be said that
many join**," and **each runs its own competition with almost no cooperation or exchange between
them.** *ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **[V]** *(Cross-reference, Kotobank, bibliographic pass: **Keisuke Yamada,
"Rethinking Iemoto: Theorizing Individual Agency in the Tsugaru Shamisen Oyama-ryū," Asian Music
48/1 (2017): 28–57, DOI 10.1353/amu.2017.0002** — the scholarly treatment of whether the iemoto
model applies in this world at all. **[U — identified, not read.]*)*

## 9. A listening list — the historical arc in fifteen items

A caution first, and it affects everything below. **[V]** "For the most part, **solo albums of
players other than Chikuzan have not been widely available for at least the last twenty years**, so
it is through compilations that these early players have been heard." And the compilations are
badly made: "**Where no information about original release dates is given, the individual recordings
exist outside of historical context.**" King "**continues to release one or more Tsugaru shamisen
anthologies per year**… [which] contain **no information about when or where the recordings were
made**." *McGoldrick 2017, 78, 76, 155.* **If you buy a King anthology you will get undated tracks
from the 1940s and the 1990s shuffled together.**

#### The pre-history and the earliest recordings

1. **Yoneya Gensuke — "Tsugaru sansagari" / "Kochō aiya bushi." Nitto label, 1918.**
   **[V]** "**The earliest known recording** [of Tsugaru min'yō] is Tsugaru native Yoneya Gensuke's
   1918 version of 'Tsugaru sansagari' and 'Kochō aiya bushi' on the now defunct Nitto label."
   *McGoldrick 2017, 59, citing Matsuki 2011, 37 and 139.*
   **Almost certainly unobtainable.** I found no reissue. Note that it predates the word
   "tsugaru-shamisen" by about four decades. *(For context on what a virtuoso shamisen solo sounded
   like before Tsugaru: **Kineya Misaburō's one-minute unaccompanied solo at the end of "Kochae
   bushi," recorded by Gaisberg in Japan in 1903** — "**the earliest known recording of solo shamisen
   improvisation**," described in the CD notes as *kyokubiki* using *bachisabaki*, and sounding
   "fast and fluid, but **lacking the rhythmic and percussive qualities of Tsugaru shamisen**." It is
   on the compilation ***Yomigaeru Oppekepe***. *McGoldrick 2017, 58–59, citing Seno 1997, 43.*)*
   **[V]**

2. **Kida Rinshōei & Fukushi Masakatsu — instrumental duets of "Yosare bushi" and "Jongara bushi."
   Regal 69014, prewar (pre-1945).**
   **[V]** The label is printed right-to-left, which dates it prewar, and **describes the content as
   a "shamisen contest" because the rubric "Tsugaru shamisen" did not yet exist.**
   *McGoldrick 2017, 60–61 and n.15, citing Matsuki 2006, 205.* **Strong evidence that modern
   instrumental tsugaru-shamisen was being issued on record before 1945. Hard to obtain.**

3. **Shirakawa Gunpachirō — the thirteen surviving tracks. Best single source: the King Records
   two-CD set of 2003** (ten Shirakawa tracks); supplemented by **the three Nippon Columbia tracks**
   (a "Tsugaru yosare bushi" and the two semi-improvised Jongara-based pieces **「木枯し」*Kogarashi*,
   "cold wintery wind," and 「津軽の郷愁」*Tsugaru no kyōshū*, "Tsugaru homesickness"**) and by
   **the 2009 centenary CD produced by the 津軽民謡21世紀遺産会 (Tsugaru Min'yō 21st Century Heritage
   Association)**, compiled from his family's own 78-rpm collection, mostly accompaniments with
   **six short solo pieces on the final three tracks**, each under two minutes.
   **[V]** McGoldrick on the King set: "**a sort of Rosetta Stone for Tsugaru shamisen.**" Four of
   King's five stock recordings — **"Tsugaru jonkara bushi (new melody)," "Tsugaru jonkara bushi
   (old melody)," "Tsugaru aiya bushi," "Tsugaru sansagari"** — appear to come from one session on
   the evidence of identical presence and echo. The 2003 set also has **two duets with his famous
   student Mihashi Michiya**, and a "Tsugaru aiya bushi" accompanying the singer Narita Unchiku.
   *McGoldrick 2017, 75–77.*
   **Difficulty: the centenary CD is "of dubious legality… not widely distributed, but only available
   at booths at Tsugaru shamisen contests, and a few shamisen stores."** One of the Aiya tracks on
   78 is **mislabelled "Tsugaru ohara bushi."** **The single most important caveat for a listener:
   these tracks "feel short and hurried by today's standards," and the Columbia LPs of him
   accompanying singers "reinforce the fact that his original role was that of an accompanist rather
   than a solo player."** *McGoldrick 2017, 75–77, citing Takada 2006, 198.* **[V]**

#### Chikuzan — the three recordings that matter most, out of a very large output

4. **Takahashi Chikuzan — the 1963 King LP: the first solo tsugaru-shamisen album ever made.**
   **[V]** "**The first full LP album of instrumental Tsugaru shamisen music.**" Re-released with new
   artwork in **1972** and **1977**; **reissued on CD in 1997 as a commemoration of the historic 1963
   album, with the original cover and new liner notes, and remains in print in that form**; resurfaced
   **2011** in King's "Stars of Min'yō" series with the running order changed.
   *McGoldrick 2017, 69–70.*
   **[U — title:** McGoldrick calls it ***Takahashi Chikuzan no sekai* (The World of Takahashi
   Chikuzan)**; ja.wikipedia calls it **『津軽三味線　高橋竹山』**. **Search both.]**
   **This is the single most historically important recording in the tradition** — see §8 for its
   sales and its effect.

5. **Takahashi Chikuzan — the Sony LPs, 1974–75, especially 『十三潟 — 津軽三味線組曲』
   (*Jūsan gata: Tsugaru-jamisen kumikyoku*).**
   **[V]** Recorded 1973–74 "in the studio, at his home, at festivals in Aomori prefecture, and at
   **Jean-Jean**." Four LPs, three issued in 1974 and the fourth in 1975. **Sony's catalogue "displays
   a much broader range of Chikuzan's abilities than any other label,"** because Sony treated each LP
   as an integral work — "the norm for popular music… **exceedingly rare in min'yō**."
   LPs 1 and 2, **live at Jean-Jean**, cover most of the solo instrumental repertoire **and include
   the anecdotes he told between pieces**; on the first track of the first LP **he sings to his own
   accompaniment, as a demonstration of the earliest kind of tsugaru-shamisen performance** — which
   makes that track the closest thing on record to the *bosama* practice. **LP 3, *Jūsan gata*, was
   recorded in the field in Aomori: Chikuzan on transverse flute and shakuhachi as well as shamisen,
   improvising new compositions, with long spoken passages** and liner notes apologising for frogs,
   crickets and rain. LP 4, in studio, opens with a long talk and then has **Sudō Un'ei singing a
   dozen famous Tsugaru min'yō with Chikuzan on shamisen and his student (and later successor)
   Takahashi Chikuyō on drums** — i.e. **a record of *utazuke*, by the man himself, with Chikuzan II
   playing taiko.** *McGoldrick 2017, 72–73, citing Sato 1989.*
   **Also: the 1978 and 1981 Sony live albums were combined onto one CD in 1996 under the English
   title *High Sound*, with overlapping repertoire, "presumably to show how the same pieces could be
   altered through improvisation (in one case) or the addition of a second shamisen (in others)."**
   Sony issued the whole collection as a **five-LP box in 1983 and a five-CD set in 2009.** **[V]**

6. **Takahashi Chikuzan — *Tsugaru no hibiki* (『津軽の響き』), King Records, 1998: the same four
   pieces in 1979 and in 1993.**
   **[V]** NHK recordings made in **1979** paired with recordings of the same pieces — **"Tsugaru
   yosare bushi," "Tsugaru ondo," "Tsugaru jonkara bushi" and "Iwaki"** — made **towards the end of
   his life in 1993**. "**The liner notes claim that this side-by-side comparison shows that his
   playing only increased in depth and simplicity as he aged.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 70.*
   **The best single purchase for hearing what late Chikuzan was, and note that 「岩木」*Iwaki* is his
   signature free kyokubiki.** **[V]**

   *Also worth knowing, same artist:* **Disques Jean-Jean's two-LP set of the September 1974 Aomori
   City civic auditorium concert** (abridged to one CD by King in 2010 as 『魂の響き』*Tamashi no
   hibiki*, whose second disc adds **eight tracks of Chikuzan accompanying famous singers, eleven
   instructional tracks recorded in 1990, and a bonus excerpt from a 1958 naniwa-bushi
   performance**); **Disques Jean-Jean's 1994 CD marking his seventieth year of performing**, recorded
   at the club shortly before ill health ended his career, which "**showcases the performance style
   Chikuzan honed over decades of small club performances throughout Japan, telling stories and
   commenting on current events between partially improvised renditions**"; the **Victor 1971
   *Ketteiban Takahashi Chikuzan***, repackaged at least eleven times with unchanged content (and
   apparently used, uncredited, in poorly mastered Korean and Chinese bootlegs sold in Japan in the
   2000s); **Nippon Crown's 1973 *Tsugaru-jamisen kettei ban***, which "during much of the 1990s…
   was the only album of Tsugaru shamisen music that was available in general interest CD shops,"
   sold at **¥1,500 — half the price of most Japanese CDs — but "suffered from poor sound quality"**;
   and **Nippon Columbia's five CDs issued in 1999 from radio shows Chikuzan recorded with Narita
   Unchiku between 1955 and 1969**, in which Narita explains and then sings the songs to Chikuzan's
   accompaniment — **"the only officially released examples of the duo performing min'yō from other
   parts of Japan," now out of print.** *McGoldrick 2017, 70–74.* **[V]**
   **Avoid: Canyon Records' *Shamisen no uta* (1975) and *Cold Plectrum: the autobiography of
   Takahashi Chikuzan* (1978), both unauthorised — the first lifted from a television programme —
   and both withdrawn.** *McGoldrick 2017, 74, citing the Takahashi Chikuzan Official Website.* **[V]**

#### Yamada, the competition generation, and the avant-garde

7. **Yamada Chisato — 「嵐」*Arashi*, and whatever solo LPs can be found.**
   **[V]** *Arashi* is named by Groemer's repertoire table as one of the two canonical named
   free-kyokubiki compositions, alongside Chikuzan's *Iwaki*. *en.wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen.*
   **Difficulty: high.** By the early 1990s Yamada himself said "no one wants to record me now…
   it's not like the old days," and Booth owned four of his records. *McGoldrick 2017, 78.*
   **He is the stylistic antithesis of the modern competition sound — 「強い叩き撥と唸り」, a strong
   tataki-bachi and a growl, "the earthy, unadorned timbre of the real Tsugaru."** *ja.wikipedia
   山田千里.* **[V/U as marked.]**

8. **Satō Michihiro & John Zorn — *Ganryujima* (『巌流島』), 1985.**
   **[V]** Tsugaru-shamisen and saxophone duets with Zorn; Satō "plays Tsugaru-based riffs of varying
   lengths in **free-form, experimental conversation**." Follow with ***Rodan* (1988)**, with
   musicians of New York's downtown scene. Then, for the contrast, his wholly traditional
   ***Tamashi no neiro* (『魂の音色』, Waon, 1992)** and ***Pure Sound of Japan: Tsugaru-jamisen no
   sekai* (Kyoto Records, 1995)**, and the hybrid ***Natsu yoi matsuri* (『夏宵祭り』, 1995)** with his
   Tsugaru-shamisen Band. *McGoldrick 2017, 81.* **The point of including him: he is a Yamada student
   and a two-time Hirosaki champion who went to the farthest edge, in 1985, fourteen years before
   *Ibuki*. The crossover story does not start with the Yoshida Brothers.** **[V]**

9. **Various — *We Are the Tsugaru-jamisen*, 1986.**
   **[V]** **Studio recordings of the best players from that year's Hirosaki contest** — the earliest
   contest-linked record McGoldrick is aware of. "**These pieces are longer and more interesting than
   typical contest pieces, which are hampered by the need to show mastery of a given set of
   techniques and riffs in a very limited time frame.**" **It includes the twelve-year-old Agatsuma
   Hiromitsu, who had taken the "special award."** *McGoldrick 2017, 81–82, 87.* **[V]**
   *(More generally: "**through much of the 1990s and the early 2000s various record companies
   released live recordings of the winning solo and group performances from Hirosaki's** … contest;
   **until the millennial revival, these recordings were the best way to hear what young players were
   doing.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 81.*)*

10. **Various — *Tsugaru Modern*, chito-shan label, 1998.**
    **[V]** A live **"Battle Royale"** of the best young players of the moment — **Kinoshita
    Shin'ichi, Agatsuma Hiromitsu, Shibutani Kazuo and Shindō Shōtarō, all past Hirosaki champions**
    — given room to play longer pieces than the contest format allowed. **The lineup is the fault line
    itself: Shibutani and Shindō "represented young players of Tsugaru, playing standard Tsugaru
    shamisen," while Kinoshita and Agatsuma "were representatives of the style that was developing
    outside of the Tsugaru region," performing traditional pieces and Tsugaru-Western hybrids with
    piano and drums.** McGoldrick: "This recording seemed very modern when it was released, but the
    following year the Yoshida Brothers' first album came out and… **changed the way Tsugaru shamisen
    albums looked and sounded.**" *McGoldrick 2017, 82.* **The single best record for hearing the
    moment before the break.** **[V]**

#### The commercial breakthrough and after

11. **Yoshida Brothers — 『いぶき』*Ibuki*, 1999 (Sony; US release via DOMO).**
    **[V]** The record that made the boom: **90,000 copies against a genre average of 5,000**;
    **日本ゴールドディスク大賞 pure-hōgaku album of the year.** Listen to it for the surprise:
    **it is almost entirely traditional** — solo versions of *Aiya*, *Ohara* and *Yosare* from the
    five, plus a *Jongara* in which each brother takes the piece in turn before a closing duet; only
    **"Modern"** (under 2½ minutes) and the first thirty seconds of the title track use non-Tsugaru
    material. *McGoldrick 2017, 82–83, 197.* Follow with ***move* (2000)**, which "follows the
    formula of *Ibuki*" but has **only three wholly traditional pieces** and sold **over 100,000** —
    **the two records together are the hinge.** **[V]**

12. **Agatsuma Hiromitsu — *Classics* (2003) and *十季 Tōki* (2010).**
    **[V]** The two **entirely traditional** solo albums in a catalogue that otherwise runs through
    rock fusion (*BEAMS*, 2002), min'yō-based ensemble writing (*永遠の詩*, 2005), Western standards
    (*AGATSUMA PLAYS STANDARDS*, 2008) and gagaku/noh collaboration (*伝統と革新-起-*, 2015).
    **Pairing *Classics* with *Standards* is the most efficient way to hear what the crossover
    generation did and did not change.** *McGoldrick 2017, 87–88; ja.wikipedia 上妻宏光.* **[V]**

13. **Agatsuma Hiromitsu — *TSUGARU*, Nippon Columbia COCB-54296, March 2020.**
    **[V]** **Contains 旧節, 中節 and 新節 of Tsugaru Jongara-bushi together** — the only release I
    can name that lets you hear §4 of `repertoire-and-technique.md`'s three-form historical sequence side by side, in modern sound.
    *ja.wikipedia 津軽じょんから節, citing the Columbia discography.* **If a reader buys one record off
    this list to understand the tradition's structure, it should be this one.** **[V]**

14. **Kinoshita Shin'ichi — *傳 den: King Of Tsugaru* and *魁 kai: Tsugaru Fusion*, both November
    2001.**
    **[V]** Released simultaneously as a matched pair — one traditional, one fusion — which makes the
    comparison explicit. Then ***遭遇* (June 2002)**, with **Roby Lakatos**, for the
    violin-and-shamisen collaboration; ***承* (2003)**; ***パッション!* (December 2004)**.
    *ja.wikipedia 木乃下真市.* **[V]**

15. **Contemporary, and the places the music has actually gone:**
    **[V]** **Nitta Masahiro (b. 1984)** — "a number of inventive recordings, including
    collaborations with Okinawan sanshin player **Yonaha Toru**, with **Oyama Yutaka**, and with the
    American tsugaru-shamisen players **Kevin Kmetz, Mike Penny and Kyle Abbott**." **Asano Shō
    (b. 1990)** — three consecutive Hirosaki wins by sixteen; four albums of traditional and
    contemporary music since 2008. **KiKi (Shirafuji Hikari & Takeda Kanami)** — ***2GIRL Shamisen*
    (2012)** and after, "pushing the two-shamisen Tsugaru sound in new directions"; Takeda won the
    Kanagi Nitabō Award in 2011. **Kevin Kmetz** — ***God of Shamisen* (2008)**, a major-label
    heavy-metal-inflected album by a player who grew up next to an American base in Aomori.
    **Wagakki Band** — for **Ninagawa Beni**'s tsugaru-shamisen inside a rock band.
    *McGoldrick 2017, 87–88; `kanagi-gc.net`; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.* **[V]**

#### How to hear the arc in one sitting, if you only have an hour

Shirakawa's prewar/1955 *Jongara kyūbushi* and *shinbushi* (item 3) → the first track of Sony's first
Chikuzan Jean-Jean LP, where he sings to his own shamisen (item 5) → Chikuzan's *Iwaki*, 1979 against
1993 (item 6) → Satō and Zorn (item 8) → *Tsugaru Modern*'s four-way (item 10) → *Ibuki*'s
*Jongara* duet (item 11) → Agatsuma's *TSUGARU* kyūbushi→chūbushi→shinbushi (item 13).
**That sequence traverses accompaniment → the invention of the solo → the concert stage → the
avant-garde → the competition style → the commercial product → and back to the historical forms as
a curated object.**

#### What is genuinely hard to obtain

- **Items 1 and 2** (1918 Nitto; prewar Regal): effectively archival only.
- **Item 3**'s centenary CD: **contest booths and a few shamisen shops only, and of dubious
  legality.**
- **Item 7** (Yamada solo LPs): out of print since the 1980s.
- **Nippon Columbia's five Chikuzan/Narita radio CDs (1999): out of print**, and they are the only
  released record of the duo performing non-Tsugaru min'yō.
- **The 1971 Nippon Columbia Chikuzan LP: never reissued.**
- **Items 8, 9, 10**: small labels, long gone; *We Are the Tsugaru-jamisen* and *Tsugaru Modern* are
  the two I would most want and least expect to find.
- **A rare live Mihashi Michiya / Shirakawa Gunpachirō LP** that, to McGoldrick's knowledge, **has
  never been released on CD.** *McGoldrick 2017, 76 n.23.*
- **Anything by Fukushi Masakatsu**, whose recordings McGoldrick judges "too poor in quality to merit
  re-release." *McGoldrick 2017, 67.*

**[V]** One last practical note on sources: **Daisō, the ¥100-shop chain, briefly sold
tsugaru-shamisen CDs**, and **poorly mastered bootlegs from Korea and China, using Victor's 1971
Chikuzan masters uncredited, circulated in Japan in the 2000s.** *McGoldrick 2017, 72, 79.*
**Provenance on cheap tsugaru-shamisen CDs is frequently worthless.**

## Appendix: the corrections this file makes to the commonly repeated account

1. **Nitabō was not the founder**, and the Japanese reference dictionaries do not even begin with
   him; their lineage for the style starts with **Umeda Hōgetsu** in the twentieth century. The
   sole-originator thesis is **Daijō Kazuo's**, and Groemer argues against it with an earlier
   contradicting source. **[V]**
2. **Nitabō's birthplace is Kanbara (神原), Kanagi — not "Kanizawa."** His father was an **Iwaki
   river ferry keeper**, not a boatman at large. **[V]**
3. **The goze mother is part of the legend**, not the record. **[V]**
4. **Nitabō's credited innovation is the striking attack; *kyokubiki* — the solo art — is credited
   to Shirakawa Gunpachirō**, and the documented mechanism is his lengthening of song preludes in
   the 1930s–40s. **[V]**
5. **"Play your own shamisen" exists in print as a competition motto (「人真似でね、汝の三味線を
   弾け！」) and as a 2004 anime tagline (「人まねでない自分の三味線を弾け!」).** A champion player
   publishing in a Brill volume cites it **to the film's director.** **[V]**
6. **Chikuzan was not Nitabō's pupil** — his teacher was **Toda Shigejirō** — he was blinded by
   **measles**, not smallpox, and he was born at **Kominato on Mutsu Bay, not on the Tsugaru
   plain.** His real name was **Takahashi Sadazō**. **[V]**
7. **Chikuzan's "hiki-shamisen" is the MINORITY style.** Most players today descend from the
   **tataki** line of Shirakawa, Kida and Fukushi. **[V]**
8. **Yamada Chisato founded the HIROSAKI competition (1982) — and even there, "founder" overstates
   it (ATV conceived it).** **Kanagi (1989) is Daijō's**, founded in criticism of Hirosaki's judging.
   **[V]**
9. **津軽小原節 and 津軽おはら節 are one piece.** **[V]**
10. **「十三の砂山」 is usually romanised "Tosa no sunayama"** in the English literature. **[V]**
11. **Tsugaru "Rokudan" is a standardised ensemble arrangement of Jongara-bushi, not the koto
    *danmono*.** **"Sandan" I could not verify at all — do not assert it.** **[U]**
12. **The body is roughly the same size as a gidayū shamisen.** The real differences are the
    **first-string gauge (reportedly 30 against nagauta's 15)**, the **heavy dog skin**, and the
    **small, hard, narrow-bladed bachi.** **[V]**
13. **No Tsugaru piece uses the miyako-bushi / in-sen scale.** Three of the five use the **min'yō
    pentatonic**, Aiya uses **ritsu**, Sansagari is a **hybrid**. **[V on the data; the mapping is
    mine.]**
14. **A Jongara kyokubiki has no regular metre — it is "pulsatile" with duple passages**, and the
    "triple time" of the other pieces is a **long–short–short grouping of unequal beats**, not 3/8.
    **[V]**
15. ***Ma* (間) is defined inside the tradition as "the temporal interval created by the breathing of
    the folk song performer"** — which is why solo-only practice erodes it. **[V]**
16. **The name "津軽三味線" dates from the mid-to-late 1950s**, and a 1956 scholarly anthology does not
    use it once. **[V]**
17. **Rōkyoku (浪花節) substitution is given by Nipponica as one of three causes of the style's
    spread, and as the source of its "show" element.** **[V as a report; independently corroborated
    by ja.wikipedia 浪曲.]**
18. **The competition critique ("vague criteria," "everyone sounds the same") is the scene's own
    self-criticism — Daijō's phrase is "shamisen without heart," 1999 — and the one preregistered
    experiment found listeners weighting SOUND over visuals for shamisen, with no visual-dominance
    effect at the top of the field.** Do not present the critique as a finding. **[V]**
19. **Popularity among the young in the 2000s has been overstated in the Western literature**
    (McGoldrick names Peluse 2005 and Johnson 2005/2006/2009); a 2001 Yoshida Brothers audience was
    **~90% over sixty.** **[V]**
20. **Player numbers: the published estimates are advocacy figures and mutually inconsistent.** The
    best framing is McGoldrick's "more people play it than ever" (2016) *against* Chiba & Savage's
    estimated fall from **~120,000 to ~26,000** combined tsugaru-shamisen-and-min'yō performers,
    1982→2022 — two measurements of different things. **[V]**
21. **The Tsugaru-shamisen Kaikan opened in 2000**, Daijō was central to it, and it is **closed from
    1 December to 31 March.** **[V]**
22. **The centre of gravity is Tokyo, and the second historical centre was Hakodate**, not only
    Tsugaru. **[V]**
