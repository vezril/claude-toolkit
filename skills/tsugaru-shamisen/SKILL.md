# Tsugaru-shamisen

**津軽三味線** is the futozao tradition of the Tsugaru region of western Aomori: loud, fast, percussive, built on striking the first string with full force, and the only shamisen genre that grew through the twentieth century. It is also the one where the popular history is least trustworthy — and this skill exists mainly to say why, and what survives the scrutiny.

For the instrument itself and the wider genre map see [[shamisen]]; for the mainstream playing and notation conventions this tradition departs from, see [[shamisen-performance]].

## Read this first: almost the whole popular history descends from one amateur's reconstruction

**Daijō Kazuo** (大條和雄, b. 1928), a Hirosaki writer and not an academic, was essentially the first person to collect and write down the oral traditions. Japanese Wikipedia's own article credits current understanding to his research and then says this of it:

> 「それもまたあくまで伝承を再構築したものに過ぎず、多分にフィクション的要素も含まれているため、史料としての価値は低い」
>
> *It too is no more than a reconstruction of oral tradition, and because it contains a great deal of fictional element, its value as a historical source is low.*

Daijō's thesis — **Nitabō as sole originator** — is available in English (Suda, Daijō & Rausch, *The Birth of Tsugaru Shamisen Music*, Aomori University Press, 1998), and so is its principal criticism (Groemer, *The Spirit of Tsugaru*, 1999; rev. 2nd ed. Tsugaru Shobō, 2012). **Present the Nitabō question as a live disagreement between two obtainable books, not as settled fact.**

And note the structural point, which is the single most useful corrective here: **the Japanese reference dictionaries do not begin with Nitabō at all.** Kotobank's 津軽三味線 entry collates Daijisen, Seisenban Nihon Kokugo Daijiten and Nipponica, and **none of the three mentions 仁太坊 or 坊様**. Nipponica's lineage for the style as it actually exists runs **Umeda Hōgetsu → Shirakawa Gunpachirō → Kida Rinshōei → Takahashi Chikuzan** — a twentieth-century lineage beginning a generation *after* the supposed founder. Stylistically those entries use **no hedging** for the twentieth-century material, whereas the origins material is hedged, including the founder claim itself (「行き着くとされる」). **The Japanese literature is confident about the twentieth century and cautious about the origins — the inverse of how English popular writing distributes its confidence.**

## How to write the origin paragraph honestly

A three-way reconciliation rather than a founding story:

1. **A plural, undocumented *bosama* milieu** in late-Meiji Tsugaru, competing at festivals and horse fairs, out of which a loud percussive accompaniment style emerged. No individual can be shown to have originated it.
2. **The tradition traces itself to Nitabō**, and the dictionaries mark that as traditional attribution. Nitabō is **absent from the 1956 NHK anthology** (which credits Kase no Momotarō) and appears **once**, as Shirakawa's teacher, in Yamada Chisato's own 1978 book.
3. **Separable, better-documented innovations:** the **futozao striking attack** to Nitabō (traditional attribution, dated by Daijō to 1878); ***kyokubiki*, the solo art, to Shirakawa Gunpachirō**, via his lengthening of song preludes in the 1930s–40s.
4. **The genre as a named, marketed category is mid-to-late 1950s.** Three datings compete (c. 1956 with Mihashi Michiya and Kida; September 1959 at the Yūrakuchō Nihon Gekijō; the Shōwa-40s min'yō boom), and a **1956 scholarly anthology of Aomori folk song does not use the name once**. The category was assembled around players who were already working.

## Five corrections most likely to matter

- **Takahashi Chikuzan's *hiki* style is the MINORITY technique.** Most players today descend from the **tataki** line of Shirakawa, Kida and Fukushi. The player best known in the West is not the archetype. He was also **not Nitabō's pupil** (his teacher was 戸田重次郎), was blinded by **measles** not smallpox, and was born at **Kominato on Mutsu Bay**, not on the Tsugaru plain.
- **No Tsugaru piece uses the miyako-bushi / *in* scale.** Jongara, Yosare and Ohara use the **min'yō** pentatonic, Aiya uses **ritsu**, and Sansagari is a hybrid. The in/yō dichotomy is simply the wrong frame here — see [[shamisen-performance]] for why it is the wrong frame generally.
- **The competition critique is the scene's own self-criticism, not a finding.** "Vague criteria" and "everyone sounds the same" are tagged 要出典 on Japanese Wikipedia; Daijō's own 1999 phrase is **"shamisen without heart."** Meanwhile the one **preregistered** experiment (Chiba, Ozaki, Fujii & Savage, *Collabra* 2023) found listeners weighting **sound over visuals** for shamisen, with **no visual-dominance effect at the top of the field**. Do not dress the folklore up as evidence.
- **The player base is contested, not simply growing.** McGoldrick (2016) says more people play it than ever; Chiba & Savage estimate a fall from **~120,000 to ~26,000** combined tsugaru-shamisen-and-min'yō performers between 1982 and 2022. They measure different things. And **youth popularity in the 2000s has been overstated** in the Western literature — a 2001 Yoshida Brothers audience was **~90% over sixty**.
- **Yamada Chisato founded the Hirosaki competition (1982), not the Kanagi one** — and even there "founder" overstates it, since Aomori Television conceived it for its 25th anniversary. **Kanagi (1989) is Daijō's**, founded in criticism of Hirosaki's judging.

A further seventeen corrections — on Nitabō's birthplace (Kanbara 神原, not "Kanizawa"), the "play your own shamisen" saying and who it actually traces to, 津軽小原節 = 津軽おはら節, the "Tosa no sunayama" romanisation, Rokudan as an ensemble arrangement rather than the koto *danmono*, the body being the size of a gidayū shamisen with the real difference in the first-string gauge, *ma* defined as "the temporal interval created by the breathing of the folk song performer," and more — are in the appendix to `references/institutions-and-modern-era.md`.
