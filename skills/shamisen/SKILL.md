# The Shamisen

The **shamisen** (三味線) is a three-stringed, fretless, skin-headed plucked lute: a square-ish body (**胴 dō**) headed top and bottom with stretched skin, a long **棹 sao** that comes apart into three pieces, a loose **駒 koma** bridge, and a large flat plectrum (**撥 bachi**) that strikes the skin as well as the string. It reached mainland Japan from the Ryūkyūs in the sixteenth century and within about fifty years had become the central instrument of urban commoner music — simultaneously the pit instrument of **kabuki**, the narrative engine of **bunraku**, the chamber instrument of the Kamigata **jiuta** tradition, the professional skill of the **geisha**, the street instrument of itinerant beggar-musicians, and the amateur accomplishment of the merchant class.

That spread is why the genre map is so dense: the genres are **not stylistic variations so much as different social institutions that happened to share an instrument**.

This skill covers the instrument and the history. [[shamisen-performance]] covers how it is played, tuned, notated and taught; [[tsugaru-shamisen]] covers the one tradition that built its own technique and its own twentieth-century institutions.

## Why this skill is sceptical by construction

The English-language account of the shamisen is thin and repeats a small number of errors confidently. The Japanese reference literature is visibly more cautious: it uses 「という」「とされる」「説」 ("it is said", "is held to be", "one theory") at precisely the points where English sources use the indicative. Each reference file therefore tags every claim **[V]** (verified against a named artifact, with the fetch date) or **[U]** (unverified, with what is missing), and each carries an explicit list of corrections.

A representative sample of what that catches:

- **The year 1562 is not a documented date — and it is not even a date of arrival.** Japanese scholarly and encyclopedic sources give the **Eiroku era (1558–70)**, grounded in court performance records; **no** Japanese encyclopedia entry gives a year. "1562" enters through the Edo legend in 『琉球年代記』, as the year a shipwrecked nobleman came home with a **月琴 *gekkin*** — a different instrument — and survives as a *parenthetical alternative* in the **1930 city gazetteer 『堺市史』**. Sakai's tourism board now states it flatly and cites nothing. The gazetteer also says the imported instrument had **two strings**, which marks the passage as tradition rather than record. Ikemiya (1989) argues the transmission was **not a single event** and that the Ryūkyū route has no 確証 (definitive proof).
- **乳袋 *chibukuro* is at the top of the neck, below the tenjin** — not at the shoulders where neck meets body. That is **鳩胸 *hatomune***. It matters because the neck-class width is measured just below the chibukuro.
- **花梨 *karin* is *Pterocarpus indicus*, a legume** — not the quince (*Pseudocydonia sinensis*, a homonym) and not *Chukrasia*.
- **A finished kōki or shitan shamisen is usually *not* CITES-controlled** — but ***Dalbergia cochinchinensis* (Siamese rosewood) is expressly excluded from the instrument exemption**, and the Japanese trade name 紫檀 is loose enough that an owner may not know which species they have.
- **The sawari's derivation from the biwa is a hypothesis, not a fact** — the competing account traces it to the Indian *jawari*, and the only piece of actual comparative analysis in the Anglophone record is Sakata's 1966 article.
- **Neither Kobayashi Haru nor Sugimoto Kikue was a Living National Treasure.** Goze-uta was selected as a 記録作成等の措置を講ずべき無形文化財 — a category whose instrument is *record-making* — which is legally distinct from the Important Intangible Cultural Property holdership the term names. English Wikipedia is wrong on both women.
- **"Samisen is the Kamigata form" is wrong.** しゃみせん is attested in Kyoto in **1580**; さみせん only in **1632**.
- **No Japanese source calls 三味線 *ateji*.** What Nipponica (signed 藤田隆則) actually says is that 「しゃみせん」 is a **転訛**, a transformed Japanese rendering of the Chinese pronunciation of 三絃 — which is a claim about the *sound*, not a claim about the characters. The popular 三昧 ("samādhi") etymology has **zero support**. And "Tsugaru-*ja*misen is rendaku" is **unsettled**, because 蛇味線 *jamisen* ("snake-skin shamisen") is independently attested.
- **Meiji policy was 折衷 (eclecticism), not westernisation** — and the government committee nominated **nagauta** for moral reform and then dropped it, while koto reform went ahead. The Tokyo Music School appointed a **koto** professor in **1937** and a **shamisen** professor only in **1950**.

## Two documents that are worth more than a paragraph of generalisation

The social history is usually told vaguely. It does not need to be:

**The 1580 first attestation.** The earliest appearance of the word 三味線 in a Japanese document is 『御湯殿上日記』, the ladies-in-waiting's duty-diary of the imperial palace, for **Tenshō 8 (1580), 2nd month, 16th day**: "after the dance, in the Palace apartments, a **kawaramono** called Yamashiro was made to play the shamisen." The first named shamisen player in the record is a **riverbed outcast**, and the venue is the **imperial palace**. The instrument enters history already connecting the bottom of the social order to the top. (The ancestor instrument is attested earlier still: **蛇皮線** appears in 『上井覚兼日記』 in **1575**, five years before the shamisen entry.)

**The 1842 confiscation edict.** 『浮世の有様』, a contemporaneous chronicle, carries a section headed 【三味線の禁止】 for Tenpō 13 (1842): *"The shamisen is not to be played except by [blind] priests, geisha, and the theatre."* Street singers of Miyazono, Bungo and Shinnai — "whether outcasts, beggars or amateurs" — had their instruments "confiscated without exception and all burned." That single passage establishes, from a contemporary source, that the shogunate recognised exactly **three licit categories of shamisen player**, that the grounds were sexual morality and lovers' suicide, and that enforcement meant burning the instruments of the poor.

## The present, in numbers

The usual decline narrative is told without figures. These are real, and they do not all point the same way:

- **Participation is up, not down.** Japan's 社会生活基本調査 (Survey on Time Use and Leisure Activities) 邦楽 series runs **1.6% → 3.0%** across 1996–2021 — with an unexplained break at the 2016 wave, and, surprisingly, a **young** age profile.
- **The bunraku troupe is countable: 22 shamisen players, 22 tayū, 38 puppeteers**, of whom **12 of the 22** shamisen players are designated holders. The 義太夫協会 has **63 members**, roughly 30 of them shamisen players.
- **Five individual 人間国宝 shamisen designations currently stand vacant.** That is the sharpest single indicator in the set: the state has seats it cannot fill.
- **Repertoire attrition is the better proxy for genre health**: kouta has **over 2,000 pieces composed and about 200 still performed**; shamisen *kumiuta* survives as **32 pieces in the Nogawa line and about 6 in the Yanagawa line**, and only Nogawa is still transmitted; of goze-uta, Groemer writes that "only a few performers continue to sing the repertory and none of them are visually disabled."
- **Institutional fragility is current news.** The **National Theatre closed in October 2023 with no reopening date**, and a **65,340-signature** petition was presented to the Prime Minister on **30 May 2025**.
- **Tsugaru went the other way** — see [[tsugaru-shamisen]]. Its Hirosaki competition has run since **1982** and reaches its **44th** edition in 2026. The asymmetry is not simply "traditional down, Tsugaru up": it is that tsugaru-jamisen is the one shamisen genre whose twentieth-century institutional form was built by the **entertainment industry** rather than inherited from the Edo guild system — which is why it is the one that could grow.
