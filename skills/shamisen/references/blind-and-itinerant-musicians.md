# Blind and Itinerant Shamisen Musicians: the Tōdōza, the Goze, and the Bosama

Scope: the **people** who carried shamisen music — the **当道座 tōdōza** guild of blind men, the
**瞽女 goze** of Echigo, and the **坊様 bosama** of northern Honshū — and the corrections Gerald
Groemer's scholarship makes to the romantic account of them.

Origins and chronology are in `history.md`; the genre map is in `genres.md`; the instrument is in
`instrument.md`; the Tsugaru tradition that the bosama hand over to is in `tsugaru-shamisen`. The
part numbering (Part 3) is retained from the research so that cross-references between these files
stay valid.

**Verification key.** **[V]** = verified, with the artifact named inline. **[U]** = unverified or
verified only against a weak source, flagged with what is missing. All fetches **2026-11-13** unless
stated. All translations from Japanese are mine and marked as such.

**Standing caveat.** Paywalled anglophone scholarship (JSTOR, Project MUSE, Taylor & Francis,
Brill) could not be read directly; general web search was blocked, so the evidence trail leans
Japanese. Where a key text could not be opened, that is said rather than papered over.

### 3.1 当道座 tōdōza — the guild of blind men

Everything here is from Japanese reference works, because Groemer's article on exactly this subject
could not be read. The strongest sources are **kotobank.jp** entries signed by **加藤康昭 Katō
Yasuaki** (author of 『日本盲人社会史研究』, 未来社, 1974 — the standard Japanese monograph) and by
**平野健次 Hirano Kenji**, plus **大谷貞夫 Ōtani Sadao** in 日本大百科全書.

**[V] What it was.** A **nakama (仲間, fellowship) of blind men** running from the medieval into the
early modern period, descended from the medieval *za* of **琵琶法師 biwa hōshi** who recited the
*Heike monogatari* to the biwa — i.e. from **平曲 heikyoku**. Biwa-hōshi *za* are attested from the
**early fourteenth century**. (日本大百科全書, 当道座, signed 大谷貞夫.) 改訂新版世界大百科事典's
盲人 entry, section 盲人の歴史 (Katō Yasuaki), has it formed from biwa-hōshi groups based at
**Chikushi, Akashi, Kyoto's Yasaka, and Bandō**, unified in the Nanbokuchō period.

**[V] The separation from religion is the key institutional move.** 世界大百科事典 (old ed.),
【当道】, via kotobank/職屋敷: with Muromachi-bakufu patronage the monks who recited heikyoku
「宗教組織から離れて自治的な職能集団を形成し」 — *broke away from the religious organisation and
formed a self-governing occupational group*, distinguishing themselves from the **盲僧 mōsō** who
stayed inside religious organisations, and calling themselves **当道** *(my translation)*. In
Kakuichi's time the organisation was systematised and acquired a character of
**治外法権的 — extraterritoriality**.

**[V] Schools.** 一方 Ichikata (founder 如一) and 八坂 Yasaka (founder 城玄); by the mid-15th century
Ichikata had split into **妙観, 師道, 源照, 戸島**, and in the late 15th–early 16th century Yasaka
into **妙聞, 大山** — the six schools that survive into the Edo period.

**[V] 明石覚一 Akashi Kakuichi (c. 1300–1371)**, a kengyō, said to have compiled the *Heike
monogatari*; a member of the Ashikaga house and therefore under Muromachi-bakufu protection;
日本大百科全書 calls him 「天下無雙の上手」, *an unrivalled master*. He is the figure credited with
organising the tōdō.

**[U] / folklore — the founding legend.** The ancestor is **人康親王 Prince Saneyasu**, the blind son
of **Emperor Ninmyō** (世界大百科事典's 盲人 entry hedges: "Ninmyō or Kōkō"), who took orders,
retired to Yamashina, gathered blind men and taught them biwa, *kangen* and poetry; after his death
the two offices of 検校 and 勾当 were granted to his blind attendants. He is revered as the deity
**天夜尊 / 雨夜尊 Amayo-no-mikoto** (the kanji differ between encyclopedia entries), with festivals
**積塔 shakutō** on 2/16 and **涼の塔 suzumi-no-tō** on 6/19; the **琵琶石** on which he is said to
have played is enshrined at **諸羽神社 Moroha-jinja**. Japanese Wikipedia 当道座 cites 『當道要集』
(*Tōdō yōshū*, **1634**) for the Amayo-no-mikoto cult and itself says the Saneyasu story may be
fiction. **Treat as cult legend, not history.**

**[V] The patron house (本所) was the court family 久我.** And the Japanese encyclopedia itself
doubts the charter. 日本大百科全書 (citing 『久我家文書』): a **rinji of Emperor Gonara, 11th month
1534 (天文3)**, states that the Kuga house has administered "the za of tōdō blind-man hōshi" since
the reign of Retired Emperor Go-Shirakawa — and the entry asks 「はたして史実であろうか」, *"but is
this actually historical fact?"*, noting that the tōdōza was then split into two feuding factions
with Kuga the standard-bearer of one, so the rinji may have been obtained precisely in order to
assert honjo rights. Muromachi-bakufu documents of 11th month **1535** and 11th month **1546**
recognised those rights. **[U] Reading:** Japanese Wikipedia reads the surname **くが Kuga**;
kotobank's 日本大百科全書 glosses it **こが Koga**. I cannot say which is standard here.

**[V] The Tokugawa relationship.** 日本大百科全書, 当道座: **Ieyasu approved the tōdōza's 式目
shikimoku**, recognised its formalities, recognised the **官物／官金 kanmotsu/kankin** payments to
kengyō and kōtō and the **運上** distributions to zatō and below, and ordered the shikimoku revised
(per 「当道大記録」). **3rd month 1634 (寛永11): the 当道式目 was established by 小池惣検校 and
others. 9th month 1692 (元禄5): revised by 惣検校 杉山和一 Sugiyama Waichi and submitted to the
bakufu as the 当道新式目.** The guild fell under the **寺社奉行** but was largely self-governing;
**it held judicial authority (司法権)** and could impose penalties including **不座 fuza
(expulsion)**, though the bakufu kept jurisdiction over serious crimes such as murder.

**[V] National reach.** Katō Yasuaki, in 世界大百科事典's 盲人 entry *(my translation)*: "The tōdōza
(the zatō nakama), with traditions dating from the medieval era, wielded the greatest power: under
the Kyoto 惣検校 it placed a controlling officer in each domain, organised each domain's territory
into several groups, had these governed by **座元 zamoto** and **組頭 kumigashira**, and extended
its organisation nationwide. The tōdō had a shikimoku which regulated in detail the **transmission
of the arts, performance, rank, master–disciple relations, mutual aid, and discipline.**"

**[V] The rank system.** Four offices (四官), highest to lowest: **検校 kengyō > 別当 bettō >
勾当 kōtō > 座頭 zatō** (日本大百科全書; 世界大百科事典's 座頭 entry: zatō is "the lowest of the four
offices established in the tōdōza"). デジタル大辞泉 and 精選版日本国語大辞典 add a further rank
**衆分 shūbun** under 盲官 *mōkan*. Below kengyō the ladder was subdivided into **16 grades and 73
notches (16階73刻)**. Katō: "the distinctive feature is the rank/class system finely subdivided into
73 notches below kengyō, and there was **clear-cut discrimination by rank across members' rights,
dress, and all of daily life**" *(my translation)*. The top ten kengyō by seniority formed the
**十老 jūrō**, who ran the guild by council; the head was the **惣検校 sō-kengyō**, styled **職
shoku**.

**[V] — and this is the fact to lead with: promotion was bought, not earned.** 日本大百科全書
*(my translation)*: "At each promotion the set kankin was paid. For a beginner to advance through
the stages to the **67th-notch 検校晴 kengyō-bare required 719 両 ryō**. Those who wished to advance
could **rise several notches at once as long as they paid the kankin — 売官, the sale of office.**"
Katō is blunter: "**this rank was conferred irrespective of artistic ability so long as a fixed
kankin (licence fee) was paid to the za**" *(my translation)*. Japanese Wikipedia 検校 gives the same
719 ryō figure and notes that **high-interest lending was permitted to blind men from the Genroku
era in order to raise the money.**

**[V] The mutual-aid system was rank-stratified.** Katō *(my translation)*: "high officers such as
kengyō and kōtō had the za's rank-licence income distributed to them annually as **下物 orimono**,
whereas the majority, the low-ranking zatō, had the auspicious- and inauspicious-occasion alms
collected from warrior and commoner households distributed to them as **配当 haitō**. Such class
regulation and livelihood guarantee made the bonds of the nakama firm." **This is the economic
engine of the whole system: licence fees from the many flowed up, alms from society flowed down.**

**[V] The usury scandal, with a famous consequence.** Japanese Wikipedia 検校: 座頭金 / 官金
high-interest lending; in **An'ei 7 (1778) eight kengyō and two kōtō had all property confiscated
and were banished from Edo** for extortion, among them 名古屋検校 (100,000+ ryō) and 鳥山検校
(15,000 ryō). Katō independently notes that 座頭金 was rampant in Edo and some blind men amassed
great fortunes. The concrete case: **Katsu Kaishū's great-great-grandfather 銀一 Gin'ichi**, a blind
man from Echigo, went to Edo, made a fortune in money-lending (an occupation permitted to the
blind), and **bought the top blind office of kengyō**, styling himself **米山検校 Yoneyama Kengyō**.

**[V] 職屋敷 shokuyashiki — the central office.** The clearest entry is 改訂新版世界大百科事典,
signed **平野健次** *(my translation throughout)*: "the common name for the central governing organ
of 当道, the blind men's self-governing organisation; also the premises itself." Located in early
Edo at **Kyoto, Bukkōji-dōri east of Karasuma (仏光寺通烏丸東入ル)**, later at 高倉通仏光寺上ル,
returning to the original site after the **Genji great fire of 1864**; inside is **清聚庵 Seijuan**,
the mortuary-tablet hall of the shoku-kengyō. There "the **職検校 shoku-kengyō** (一老検校, commonly
惣検校 or 総検校) and the other nine up to the 十老検校 — ten in all — carried out the duties of
governing the tōdōza: **granting rank licences (官位免許) and distributing kankin.**" And the hedge
worth quoting: 「職屋敷は覚一検校（1300ころ-71）の時代に始まるといわれるが確証はない」 — *"the
shokuyashiki is said to begin in the time of Kakuichi Kengyō (c. 1300–71), but there is no firm
proof."* **[U]** ブリタニカ instead places the office at 京都東洞院 Higashinotōin and notes the
alternative reading **しきやしき shikiyashiki** — flag the discrepancy.

**[V] The Edo branch and the 1692 split.** **1692 (元禄5): the 惣録屋敷 sōroku-yashiki was
established at Kanda in Edo**, its shogun-appointed **惣録検校** styled sō-kengyō and sometimes
ranked above the Kyoto shoku-kengyō. The mechanism (日本大百科全書): **Sugiyama Waichi Kengyō**, an
acupuncture expert, was summoned by the fifth shogun **Tsunayoshi** and made sō-kengyō in the fifth
month of 1692; thereafter **the Edo office controlled the blind of the Kantō and the Kyoto office
those of Kamigata** — the real reason for the shikimoku revision. **1736 (元文1)** the sō-kengyō
title returned to Kyoto and the Edo sōroku-kengyō governed only Edo and the Kantō, **without
rank-licensing authority**; the sōroku-yashiki moved to 本所一ッ目 in **1741 (寛保1)**.

**[V] Regional and female counterparts** (平野健次): "In some domains there were also **仕置屋敷
shioki-yashiki** as regional governing organs, and **blind women were governed by 瞽女屋敷
goze-yashiki**." Note this is a weaker claim than "a parallel national women's guild" — see §3.2.

**[V] Abolition: 1871 — confirmed from three directions.**
1. 世界大百科事典, 職屋敷 (平野健次): 「1871年（明治4），鶴岡惣検校のときに廃絶した」 — abolished in
   **1871 (明治4), in the time of Tsuruoka Sō-kengyō**; the Edo sōroku-yashiki likewise in 1871, in
   the time of 多喜川惣録検校. ブリタニカ: both 「明治4(1871)年廃止」.
2. 世界大百科事典, 盲人 (加藤康昭): 「明治維新の変革の中で政府は1871年（明治4）当道や盲僧を廃止
   したが、盲人たちは各地で家元、組合、講を再組織し、盲僧も1875年天台宗の下で復活した」 — *amid the
   Meiji Restoration's upheavals the government abolished the tōdō and the mōsō in 1871, but blind
   people re-organised themselves locally as **iemoto, kumiai and kō**, and the mōsō were revived
   under the Tendai sect in 1875* *(my translation)*. **Note that iemoto re-formation: this is the
   bridge from the guild to the modern schools of jiuta and sōkyoku.**
3. **[U] on the instrument, [V] on the year.** Japanese Wikipedia 検校 cites
   **太政官布告第568号「盲人ノ官職自今被廃候事」, 3rd day of the 11th month of 1871 (lunar)** —
   *"the official offices of the blind are hereby abolished from now on"* — and says the hierarchy
   of domination among blind people topped by the kengyō was abolished, so that blind people could
   choose an occupation without being bound to the tōdōza. **The proclamation number 568 and the day
   11/3 I have only from Wikipedia**; the year 1871 is independently solid from 世界大百科事典 twice
   over. Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 independently says a Dajōkan proclamation of the 11th month of 1871
   issued the 盲官廃止令.
   **Afterlife:** "kengyō" survives as a qualification title awarded by koto organisations such as
   the 当道音楽会 — **宮城道雄 Miyagi Michio** held it. The last sō-kengyō is named as
   **鶴岡検校（俊一）**.

**[V] / [U] The monopoly question — state it carefully.** What is attested: 世界大百科事典 (old ed.)
via kotobank/検校, *(my translation)*: "Since the medieval period blind men normally took religious
form and followed occupations such as **Heike biwa, acupuncture and moxibustion, massage therapy,
sōkyoku, and shamisen**. These blind hōshi, **with bakufu official recognition and with the court
family Kuga as honjo, formed the body called the tōdōza, administered the ranks of the blind, and
sought the monopolisation of occupations (職業の専有化).**" And 日本大百科全書's 地歌 entry (signed
**井野辺潔**): jiuta "developed into a major genre of Japanese music by the blind professional
musicians, the kengyō and kōtō, who belonged to the **当道職屋敷制度**." 精選版日本国語大辞典: jiuta
was "composed and transmitted by blind musicians such as kengyō and kōtō, **hence it is also called
法師歌 hōshi-uta**."
**What is NOT attested in anything I read: an explicit legal monopoly on *teaching* jiuta, koto or
shamisen — e.g. a licence requirement binding sighted teachers.** The sources support "the guild
*sought* occupational monopolisation" and "the genres were developed and transmitted *within* the
guild." Nearby and better attested is a **territorial monopoly over alms**: English Wikipedia's
Tōdōza article says the guild assigned members specific territories, preventing non-member blind men
from collecting — **but its sources are a 1997 LSE dissertation, a web page and a blog**, so treat
as [U] for scholarship. **Get the teaching-monopoly claim from Groemer, *MN* 56/3, before printing
it.**
**[V] And do not attribute the circulating membership numbers to Groemer.** English Wikipedia's
Tōdōza article gives "total membership was around 3,000" and growth "from 445 men in 1534 to 2,051
men in 1657." Pulling that article's reference list via the MediaWiki parse API shows those
citations are **Akihiro Sugino's 1997 LSE PhD thesis, a japanpastandpresent.org page, A. D. Bertova
in *Japanese Studies in Russia* (2025), and a blog post**. Groemer appears only in a "Further
reading" line.

**[V] 石村検校 Ishimura Kengyō — a person, with legendary attributions.** This supplements §1.5 of `history.md` of
`history.md`. Across five reference works on kotobank:
- Death date is **confidently and consistently given: 寛永19年9月24日 = 17 October 1642**, in Kyoto
  (ブリタニカ gives the day; デジタル大辞泉, 世界大百科事典 and 日本人名大辞典+Plus give the year).
  **Birth unknown** — 日本大百科全書 says 生没年不詳 outright.
- 世界大百科事典 (**平野健次**): "**founder (創始者) of the shamisen kumiuta**," the first artistic
  song genre of shamisen music, with 《琉球組》 **擬せられている — conjecturally attributed** to him;
  and 「伝説 legends are transmitted」 that he crossed to the Ryukyus and made the shamisen on
  returning to Kyoto, or that he was Ishimaro son of Umezu Shōshō (or alternatively **通世, son of
  久我敦通** — i.e. of the tōdōza's own patron house).
- ブリタニカ: "**There is a legend that he crossed to Ryūkyū around the Bunroku era (1592–96) and
  improved the kokyū into the shamisen, but this is doubtful (伝説があるが疑わしい)**"; he composed
  the 本手組 of the shamisen kumiuta, beginning with 《琉球組》, and 「三味線音楽を芸術化した」 —
  *made shamisen music into art.*
- 日本人名大辞典+Plus: said to have remodelled the Ryukyuan sangen and composed 「琉球組」,
  「**but there are differing accounts (異説もある)**."
- 日本大百科全書 (signed **林喜代弘**) lists the three Edo-period texts behind the attribution:
  **『糸竹初心集』** (the Bunroku Ryukyu voyage), **『琉球年代記』** (Umezu Shōshō, the gekkin, and
  the return in **1562** — see `history.md` §1.2 of `history.md`), and **『野河検校流三線統系序』** (living at
  中小路 in Sakai, improving a two-stringed snakeskin lute from the Ryukyus and naming it the
  *sangen*). He also appears in 『海録』. **References cited: 藤田徳太郎「三絃の免許状」 in
  『東亜音楽論叢』 (山一書房, 1943); 吉川英史「三絃伝来考」, reprinted in 『三味線とその音楽』
  (音楽之友社, 1978).**
- 世界大百科事典 (old ed.), 【琉球組】: transmitted as Ishimura's composition, "**but there are also
  theories that Torasawa Kengyō composed or supplemented it.**"
- **[V]** 日本大百科全書, 地歌: "The oldest genre is the **honte-gumi (表組) of the shamisen kumiuta,
  made by Ishimura and Torasawa Kengyō (?–1654) in the late 16th–early 17th centuries**; this
  comprises **seven pieces including 《琉球組》**. Subsequently Torasawa and **Yanagawa Kengyō
  (?–1680)** produced the **破手組 hade-gumi**."
- **[V] Where English and Japanese accounts diverge — worth stating.** English Wikipedia's "Jiuta"
  article says flatly "**Ishimura-Kengyo is particularly regarded as originator of shamisen
  music**." **Japanese Wikipedia's 地歌 article does not mention Ishimura at all**, naming no
  individual founder and saying only that blind musicians of the 当道座 in Kyoto and Osaka, who had
  traditionally transmitted the Heike biwa, adopted the shamisen and developed shamisen song.
- **Recommended wording:** *Ishimura Kengyō is a historical person whose death date (17 October 1642)
  is confidently given in multiple Japanese reference works, though his birth year is unknown. His
  attributed role in adapting the Ryukyuan instrument into the shamisen rests on three Edo-period
  texts and is explicitly labelled "legend," "conjecturally attributed," "there are other accounts,"
  or outright "doubtful." His attribution as first composer of the shamisen kumiuta — the beginnings
  of jiuta — is reported more confidently, but still hedged in some entries.*

**[V] Other named kengyō, with their guild names** (Japanese Wikipedia 検校) — useful because this
roster *is* the early history of shamisen and koto art music: **虎沢検校 Torasawa** (?–1654,
Ishimura's pupil, Kyoto-born, blinded in childhood); **柳川検校 Yanagawa** (?–1680, certified kengyō
1639); **八橋検校 Yatsuhashi (城秀)**, "father of modern sōkyoku," who also improved the kokyū bow;
**杉山検校 Sugiyama Waichi**, who established the 管鍼法 tube-needle method of acupuncture;
**塙検校 Hanawa Hokiichi (保己一)**, compiler of 『群書類従』 and founder of the 和学講談所;
**荻野検校 (知一)**, compiler of 『平家正節』; **山田検校 (斗養一)**, founder of Yamada-ryū sōkyoku;
**生田検校 (幾一)**, founder of Ikuta-ryū; **松浦検校 (久保一)** and **菊岡検校 (楚明一)**, who
established and developed the 京流手事物; **八重崎検校 (壱岐一)**; **光崎検校 (富機一)**;
**吉沢検校 (審一)**; **鶴岡検校 (俊一)**, the last sō-kengyō.

**[V] Flag as fiction, not history** (same article): **藪原検校 Yabuhara Kengyō** — the villain of
kōdan and kabuki (蓁々斎桃葉's 1893 kōdan; 三代目河竹新七's 1900 kabuki 『成田道初音藪原』; Inoue
Hisashi's 1973 play 『藪原検校』), of whom "there is a theory that there was a model but details are
unknown" — plus 不知火検校, 錦木検校, 髑髏検校, and **座頭市 Zatōichi**. The whole literary
imagination of the blind musician-criminal grows out of this institution; none of these are people.

### 3.2 瞽女 goze — the blind itinerant women

#### Organisation

**[V] There was no national body.** 日本大百科全書, 瞽女 (signed **野村純一 Nomura Jun'ichi**):
「座頭のような全国的組織はもたず」 — *they had no nationwide organisation of the kind the zatō had*;
they organised and controlled groups region by region and each commonly walked a fixed territory
*(my translation)*. Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 agrees and adds the mechanism: independent goze
organisations formed in castle towns, temple-gate towns and post towns as the tōdōza's regional
organisations took shape, **but with nothing binding them together nationally**; the bakufu
entrusted administration to each domain according to local custom, and each domain entrusted
policing to the **zamoto of the local tōdōza organisation**.

**[V] The unit vocabulary.** 山川日本史小辞典 改訂新版: "In the early modern period **master and
disciple lived together and constituted a household (世帯); the master formed a 組 kumi with several
houses; several kumi organised a 仲間 nakama or 座 za.**" Each locality's nakama ran a **year-based
ladder system (年次階梯制)** with the ranks **初心 shoshin → 中老 chūrō → 一老 ichirō**
*(my translation)*.

**[V] It was modelled on the tōdōza, explicitly.** Katō Yasuaki, 世界大百科事典, 盲人
*(my translation)*: "From around the mid-Edo period goze organised nakama led by a **瞽女頭
goze-gashira** or **座元 zamoto**, centred on castle towns, temple-gate towns and post towns along
the Tōkai, Chūsen and Hokuriku highways; the nakama was subdivided into several kumi, and **four or
five masters-and-disciples formed the unit of a tour**. Each nakama had a **由緒書 lineage document
and a 式目 shikimoku modelled on those of the tōdō (当道にならった)**, and fixed ranks such as
shoshin, chūrō and ichirō. At the annual **妙音講 myōonkō** rites they also settled tour
arrangements and punished misconduct."

**[V] Groemer's chapter 2 abstract** (academic.oup.com/book/4543/chapter/146631264) is the
authoritative English summary: two major goze organisations plus many smaller ones **emerged at the
start of the seventeenth century**, one based at **Takada**, the other at **Nagaoka**. Both had a
**hierarchical social structure**, worship of the goddess **Benzaiten**, a **legend of origins**, a
**code of behaviour**, and a **large repertory of songs**. The associations supported goze in daily
life, careers and tours; they **mediated the goze's relationship with the rest of society and gave
legitimacy to the goze way of life.**

**[V] Suzuki Shōei's three-type typology** — the standard one, reproduced in Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女
and in English Wikipedia's Goze article:
- **座本制 zamoto system — Takada.** Disciples became **adopted daughters (養女)** of a house-holding
  master (**親方 oyakata**, called **ヤモチ 屋持**, "house-holder"); **no tuition or 口米 payable**;
  the master with the most years of training became **座元 zamoto**, who kept the **式目** and the
  box (ジョウバコ) it was stored in. Houses formed a kumi; no kumigashira; honke/bunke relations.
  **17 oyakata houses at the end of Meiji, 15 in early Shōwa** (日本大百科全書 says "a dozen-odd
  house-holding goze").
- **家元制 iemoto system — Nagaoka.** Goze lived in the countryside, often in their own homes, after
  apprenticeship. A named leader, the **大親方 / オゴイサマ**, bearing the hereditary name
  **山本ごい Yamamoto Goi**, sat at the top at the Nagaoka **瞽女屋 goze-ya**, with subordinate
  kumigashira masters beneath her. On a Yamamoto Goi's death the kumigashira met and chose a
  successor, who moved into the goze-ya. **Once a year the group assembled at her house for the
  myōonkō, at which their history and the rules of their organisation were read out loud**,
  rule-breakers deliberated on, a celebratory meal eaten, and performances given.
- **頭屋制 atoya/tōya system — Iida (Shinshū) and Niida.** The host role **rotated annually** among
  members.

**[V] Fictive kinship — in Groemer's own words** (JJRS 41/2, 2014, p. 350): "**Most Echigo goze
households comprised a fictive family of women in which a teacher (often called the 'mother')
surrounded herself with pupils, guides, and other auxiliaries. Ritualized religious observances
structured the day, the year, and even the life span of the inhabitants.**"
Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 on the same, *(my translation)*: "The goze master–disciple system, as when
Nagaoka goze called their disciples **'children' (子供)**, constructed a **pseudo parent–child
relationship by seniority**, with disciples relating to one another as sisters. … Disciples'
seniority was set by years since entry; **absolute obedience to senior disciples was the iron
rule**, with strict rules governing forms of address, dress, hairstyle, the order of bathing and of
seating, where luggage was placed, and even the order of picking up chopsticks at meals."

**[V] Apprenticeship terms** (Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女) — **longer than ordinary artisan
apprenticeships, because of the sheer number of pieces to be memorised**: Takada and Dochi groups
**~10 years**; Kariwa (including one year of 礼奉公), Nagaoka and Sanjō groups **21 years** (a full
20); Niida and Agakita goze **18 years**. Later shortened (Sanjō to 10; Shibata 10–12). Terms could
be shortened or extended at the master's discretion, or bought down by a special payment from the
family. Early entry was preferred; entering after menarche was believed to prevent full mastery.

**[V] The rite of passage.** Takada group: in the **seventh year** a **名替え nakae** — a change from
the entry name to an "advancement name" — was held at the master's house. Japanese Wikipedia:
"**an important ceremony that could determine a goze's life, so it was conducted in the same form as
a wedding ceremony in general society, and was also called 祝言 shūgen**" *(my translation)*, with
sanbon-sankudo cups exchanged with the master. Three years after the nakae and ten after entry a
goze could take disciples; she then wore the **丸髷**, blackened her teeth, and was called あねさん.

**[V] Benzaiten and the reading-out of the rules** — Groemer, JJRS 2014, p. 354: Takada goze turned
to the Sōtō-sect **天林寺 Tenrinji** at Teramachi, where they held their **annual general meeting
(myōon-kō) in honour of Benzaiten**, whom the temple enshrined, and at which "**a priest read out a
document relating the origins of the goze (*goze engi* 瞽女縁起) and a list of rules (*shikimoku*
式目) that Benzaiten had commanded goze to follow to the letter.**" Groemer's gloss: "**That
Benzaiten, who also spread her shielding wings over the guild of blind men (*tōdō* 当道), was the
favored object of worship among goze was no accident.** … Yet Benzaiten was neither a god of
blindness nor of sight. Instead of offering the 'worldly benefit' of restored vision, **she
supported goze by bequeathing them a valuable art.**"

**[V] / folklore — the origin documents are not history, and say so.** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女: the
由来書 were called **式目** (Takada, Dochi, Sanjō), **御条目** (Nagaoka, Okō) or **御縁起** (Kariwa),
and comprise three parts — **縁起 / 院宣 / 式目** — listing the ranks 初心/中老/一老 with the years
needed for each, plus rules and penalties including expulsion and 年落とし. And:
「瞽女の由来書は当道座の由来伝承を参考に製作され史実ではない」 — *"the goze lineage documents were
composed with reference to the tōdōza's own origin traditions and are not historical fact"* — but
they served as a spiritual mainstay for socially low-ranked goze, and in some regions goze were held
to be of **high** status, permitted tabi and haori that **geisha were not** *(my translation)*. The
legend itself: goze descend from **相模宮 Sagami-no-miya**, a princess of Emperor Saga born blind, in
whom the Shimogamo deity took up residence, who at seven was told in a dream by the Nyoirin Kannon
of Nachi-san in Kii to make a living from the arts and become head of the blind, and who trained
five lineages of disciples; and lodging with village headmen and receiving money and grain was by
**imperial decree of Emperor Saga**. English Wikipedia's Goze article adds the dating control:
"**no copy of these rules earlier than the late seventeenth century has been found.**"

#### Repertoire

**[V] Groemer's chapter 4 abstract** (chapter/146634438) is the clearest statement of what goze
actually performed: **most of a goze's life was spent travelling.** Beginning shortly after New
Year's they went **house to house offering New Year wishes and collecting a few coins**; once the
snow melted they toured more distant regions. In each village they **first went door to door singing
short songs for a small gratuity, then later gave a concert**, usually including **long pieces
called *danmono* or *saimon matsuzaka***, held at the **home of the village head or another family**.
The chapter translates in full **「葛の葉子別れ」 "The Arrowroot Princess Parts with Her Child,"**
described as **perhaps the most popular *danmono* in the goze repertory**.

**[V] The fuller taxonomy** (English Wikipedia's Goze article, which on internal evidence derives
from Groemer's book — treat as a paraphrase of him rather than an independent source):
- **祭文松坂 saimon matsusaka** — long strophic songs in **7-5 metre**, often on archaic tales,
  sometimes Buddhist, sung to what was probably a variant of the Echigo folk song *Matsusaka bushi*;
  **probably created during the eighteenth century**; usually transmitted only goze-to-goze.
- **口説 kudoki** — long strophic songs in **7-7 metre**, texts usually featuring **double
  love-suicides** or other melodramatic themes; melody a variant of the Echigo folk song
  **新保広大寺 *Shinpo kōdaiji***, which "**did not appear until the mid-nineteenth century.**"
- **門付け唄 kadozuke uta** — a *functional* label for any song used going door to door, with some
  songs reserved for the purpose.
- **民謡 min'yō**, and a **"classical"/"semi-classical"** layer — nagauta, jōruri, hauta, kouta,
  often learned from professional musicians outside the goze community. Plus **popular songs**
  (Sugimoto Kikue added *Sendō kouta* and *Kago no tori* in 1922).

**[V]** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 names **口説節 kudokibushi** (jōruri-influenced narrative, more
song-like than gidayū-bushi), the sekkyō-bushi **『小栗判官』 Oguri Hangan**, and
**『葛の葉子別れ』**. 日本大百科全書: "danmono beginning with *Kuzu no ha ko-wakare* and *Oguri
Hangan*, as well as kudoki and hayari-uta — in short they held a repertory of narratives and many
songs," and notes that "in recent years they have attracted attention as **transmitters of folktales
and gossip**" *(my translation)*. 世界大百科事典, 瞽女 (signed **織田紘二**) names
**《葛の葉子別れ》《山椒太夫》《石童丸》** and observes that goze songs became material for local
bon-dance songs and children's play songs. 百科事典マイペディア: the melodic style goze sang was
popularly called **瞽女節 goze-bushi**.

**[V] Two of Groemer's specific repertoire studies** are in the open-access JJRS article: **春駒
harugoma** (a kadozuke song tied to silk production) and **万歳 manzai** (New Year
luck-bringing), both translated and annotated there, in versions based on a Sugimoto Kikue
recording.

**[V]** There is **no Japanese Wikipedia article at 瞽女唄** (the MediaWiki API returns no extract,
2026-11-13) — a small but telling index of how thin the general-reference coverage is.

#### The instrument — chūzao, and collapsible

**[V]** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女, 道具 section *(my translation)*: 「三味線は三つ折りの中棹タイプを
用い」 — **they used a three-fold (collapsible-neck) 中棹 chūzao-type shamisen**. The skin had to be
replaced about twice a year in heavy touring years; **dog skin (犬皮) was usual but paper-covered
(紙張り) was also used**, in which case burnt chilli was placed inside against damp (paper sounded
slightly worse but lasted longer); Nagaoka goze's paper came from Yamanoda, Mitsuoke and Kokenojima
in Oguni-machi, Kariwa-gun. When not folded it was carried in a bag by the guide. **On becoming
independent a goze could use a tortoiseshell or ivory plectrum** — tortoiseshell was avoided on
winter tours to Gunma because it froze.
The cane is a nice institutional detail: **ズミ or カシ wood, length fixed at 5尺2寸 (~157.6 cm)**;
disciples used oak, those who could sing could use *zumi*; **green bamboo was forbidden as taboo**;
metal ferrules forbidden; new canes were offered to Benzaiten at the myōonkō first. Compare the
tōdōza men's cane: Japanese Wikipedia 当道座 gives gamazumi wood, bamboo prohibited, length
**5尺1寸 (~151.5 cm)** — **[V]** a direct parallel between the two organisations' rules, which
supports the "modelled on the tōdō" claim from a second direction.
**[V]** The **胡弓 kokyū** is also attested as a goze instrument (Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 lead;
Groemer mentions goze singing to shamisen, kokyū and drums).

#### Economic reality — stated without euphemism

**[V] Door-to-door by day, a paid concert by night.** 日本大百科全書, 瞽女 *(my translation)*: "They
toured in parties of three or several. In mountain villages short of entertainment they were greatly
welcomed. **By day they went round doing kadozuke; at night they displayed their art before the
people gathered at the regular lodging (定宿).**"

**[V] Payment in rice and coin, at the bottom of the social order.** Katō Yasuaki, 世界大百科事典,
盲人 *(my translation)*: "**At the bottom of early modern society the figures of zatō and goze were
widely seen — shamisen in hand, singing jōruri and kouta, going round town and country, or standing
at doorways on auspicious and inauspicious occasions, begging rice and coin (米銭を乞う).** The blind
who visited at each season brought entertainment to farming villages worn out by labour, and
villagers **welcomed them by providing lodging and guides.**"

**[V] Lodging as Buddhist merit — the best single piece of testimony.** Groemer, JJRS 2014, p. 354,
quoting **関根ヤス Sekine Yasu (1893–?) of Nagaoka** (Groemer citing Suzuki 1996, 239): "In many
areas people thought that if they let goze stay overnight, this was a Buddhist merit (*kudoku*
功徳). They'd beg you to stay if a dear child had died, or if the day was a memorial day for a
grandfather or grandmother who had passed away. … they thought that letting goze spend the night
profited the spirit of the deceased. When they let you stay for free, this became a virtue for them.
And when we sang our songs on a memorial day, this pleased everyone. **They said the performances
were for the sake of the deceased.**" Groemer's own gloss: "Supplying a room to wayfaring strangers
was generally taken as a virtue with religious implications, **but it also separated the 'insider'
from the 'outsider'** … **Community and exclusion defined and depended on each other.**"

**[V] Magical commerce — goze rice and goze shamisen strings.** Groemer, JJRS 2014, p. 355:
"**Even the rice that goze collected from 'one-hundred people' (*hyakunin-gome* or *hyakunin-mai*)
contained a mysterious spirit endowing it with a higher monetary value than more humdrum variety of
the grain.**" And on strings: silkworm growers believed silk shamisen strings harboured benefit; one
goze touring near Iida in Nagano was "**so persistently pestered for broken shamisen**
[strings]…"; "**Silkworm growers rarely purchased strings from non-goze.**" Ihira Take recalled
listeners wanting her shamisen's pouch: "They'd make clothing for a newborn child out of it, so that
the baby would get strong and not hurt itself." **This is worth a line in the reference document:
a goze's instrument was not only a tool but a source of saleable efficacy.**

**[V] Income was divided equally between master and disciple** (Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女) — "even a
disciple of six or seven who had not yet learned the songs received an equal share if she joined the
tour" *(my translation)*. Masters aged 52 and over who held houses received an equal share even
without joining short tours, but not long-distance ones. Practice varied; absentee masters sometimes
set quotas instead.

**[V] Domain support, where it existed.** 日本大百科全書: some domains (Sunpu, Echigo Takada,
Nagaoka) "gave them **瞽女屋敷 goze-yashiki**, protected them, and had them live communally, **and
thereby controlled them**" *(my translation)* — note the last clause. Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 adds
concrete regional arrangements: many Seto-Inland-Sea goze of the mid-to-late Edo period are said to
have received **扶持 fuchi** (stipends for the visually disabled) from Hiroshima, Chōshū and many
Shikoku domains; **Iida built a 瞽女長屋 goze tenement in 1831 (天保2)** from local donations,
housing 8 groups and 20-odd people, with touring goze issued barley in summer and rice in autumn and
winter; **Suwa's** goze tenement admitted only blind women from Suwa domain, toured twice a year,
lodged at headmen's houses, and received **two handfuls of rice per goze per household**, with the
custom that households brewed tea and greeted them **as equals** even in the busy season;
**Matsumoto** goze did not demand rice or money because the headmen supplied a fixed quantity of
barley or rice as fuchi to the goze-yashiki in spring and autumn, so the tour doubled as a
thank-you round.

#### The rules on sex, and the punishments

This has to be reported accurately, which means reporting both the severity and the function.

**[V] The prohibition, and the admission inside it.** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女, 男女関係 section
*(my translation)*: "**What goze organisations were most on edge about was relations between the
sexes: becoming the wife of a 座頭 zatō, or being found to have had a sexual relationship with a man
or to have married, meant expulsion without argument. That this had to be made a prohibited item is
also an indication that such things were in fact rife as established facts (既成事実として
横行していた). Even in goze organisations whose management was collapsing, the rules on relations
between the sexes remained severe to the very end.**" For the Nagaoka group: disciple-rank goze
**could not attend a drinking party alone**; even when permitted, **pouring sake for a man or
accepting a returned cup was forbidden**, and on returning home she was **obliged to report the whole
party to her master**. In the late period some continued relationships while continuing training by
accepting 年落とし or paying 献金 / 謝り金. Goze who married and then secretly carried on business
were despised as 「バズレ／ハズレ」.

**[V] The punishments.** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女, 処罰 section *(my translation)*: rule-breaking
convened a 寄合. The heaviest penalty was **所払い tokorobarai, banishment** — for grave offences
such as "secretly having relations with a man," the offender's **shamisen was confiscated**, she was
**dressed only in underwear and waistcloth with a rope for a belt and given a green-bamboo cane**,
and **banished beyond a radius of 10–20 ri (c. 40–80 km)**, with fellow oyakata and lodgings
notified to ostracise her; the banished became a **ひとり瞽女, a "solitary goze."** Short of
banishment came **年おとし nen-otoshi**, the reduction of years already served, fixed at **1, 3, 5, 7
or 10 years** by offence, sometimes with a name change and 謝り金 — and because a senior disciple
reduced in years then had to treat her juniors as seniors, "**in the strictly seniority-based goze
society it was a humiliating punishment.**" **縁切り金 enkirikin, severance money, was invariably
demanded of anyone leaving.** Taishō-era Nagaoka rates: **7 yen** for severance (plus return of the
shamisen and a ban on business within 3 ri) versus **21 yen** for voluntary resignation (plus the
shamisen and a full seasonal set of clothing). In Meiji, **corporal punishments such as tokorobarai
were banned by law**, and 謝り金 and 年おとし became the basis; nen-otoshi continued **until the
goze-ya burned down in the war**. The Nagaoka goze-ya had a **仕置場, a punishment place** — a closet
above a storeroom floored with two mats, where disobedient disciples were shut and starved for about
a day; the **仕置縄, punishment rope**, was tied to the pillar from which the scroll of the 御条目
hung. The Takaoka group added **head-shaving** for unauthorised flight and, in the Edo period,
**severe penalties including the cutting off of an arm**.

**[V] How Groemer frames the celibacy rule — functionally, not as pure oppression.** JJRS 2014,
p. 353: "Devout, orderly, and even ascetic practices steeled goze to what awaited them in the
outside world. If goze wished to consolidate their organizations and fortify solidarity within their
ranks they had little choice but to cultivate piety and orthopractical rigidity. … **A reputation of
spotless integrity was the sine qua non for goze, for no self-respecting farmer, not to mention his
wife, would lodge a woman suspected of moonlighting as a hooker or associating with debauched
riffraff. Any hint of impiety or immodesty would have ended a goze's career on the spot.**" And:
"**Far from establishing a bland unity with the 'Japanese people,' goze religious practice permitted
goze to differentiate themselves from social groups with which they, for right or wrong, wished not
to be associated.**"
The same argument spelled out in English Wikipedia's Goze article (**[V]** as a page, almost
certainly paraphrasing Groemer's book): "perhaps the most important rule was celibacy. … **if a goze
did have a lover or if she married, she would have financial support from an outside source, and
thus needed no further charity.** Furthermore, the stipulations were developed to **protect the
image of the goze group as a legitimate non-profit organization and protect it from appearing to be,
or devolving into, a prostitution ring.** … **membership in an association that was recognized as
legitimate and honorable was an important credential which allayed suspicions that the woman might
be a wandering vagabond or prostitute.**"

**[V] Documented individual violence — use with attribution.** Japanese Wikipedia 小林ハル
*(my translation; the article is built on the Kobayashi Haru oral-history literature — 本間章子,
桐生清次 — not on Groemer): her first master **樋口フジ Higuchi Fuji** repeatedly reviled her, called
her 「ぼっこれ薬缶」 ("broken kettle"), and **beat her shins with her cane so badly that on
examination her bones were found to be riddled with countless fissures**. At eighteen, on tour with a
sighted senior goze named **サヨ Sayo**, Haru fell into a ditch; a villager told Sayo to take better
care of her, and Sayo, enraged, **knocked Haru down out of sight of others and repeatedly stabbed at
her genitals with her cane**. The wound would not heal; Haru **told no one the truth**, reasoning
that if it became a police matter and Sayo were removed she could not tour; the doctor told her that
without surgery she would never bear a child; she declined the surgery, which cost 100 yen, and
because she had been told from childhood not to expect an ordinary household. She considered that
she had "**lost her function as a woman**" and never accepted a marriage proposal thereafter.
**[V]** Groemer's chapter 3 abstract confirms that he uses her first-person accounts "to show the
hardships this training involved," and that girls were taught **thousands of lines of text and
countless melodies**, taught to carry themselves as professional musicians, and **tutored in the
locations of lodges on the road**.

#### The Echigo centres

**[V] 高田 Takada** — modern **上越市 Jōetsu-shi** (the former Takada city area). Groemer (JJRS
p. 350) identifies Sugimoto Kikue as "a goze from Takada (present-day Jōetsu-shi in Niigata
prefecture)." Religious base: **天林寺 Tenrinji** (Sōtō) at Teramachi, relocated there from nearby
Naoetsu in the first half of the Edo period. Statutory meetings in the 1st, 5th and 8th months, the
1st and 8th being **大寄合い ō-yoriai** at the zamoto's house, deciding mainly tour destinations; a
Meiji rule revision imposed a **50-sen fine** for unexcused absence. Takada groups had to pair with
a *different* group when touring, which limited their range.
**[V] Documented numbers** (Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女), and notice that Takada was under the tōdōza
but not subsidised by the domain — 「**当道座の支配下**にあり…藩による**政策的な保護や屋敷地の
下賜は行われなかった**」, *under the control of the tōdōza … no policy protection or grant of
residential land by the domain was carried out* *(my translation)*: **1624 (寛永1)**, when
松平光長 was transferred to Takada, the 本寺 of Kawaguchi village had oversight of the Takada goze;
**1742 (寛保2): 16 名替瞽女 and 4 小瞽女 plus a zamoto; 1814 (文化11): 56 goze living across 10 town
blocks; 1901 (明治34): 97; 1922 (大正11): 15 houses, 44 people; 1933 (昭和8): 23.**
**Last touring season: autumn (10th month) 1964.**

**[V] 長岡 Nagaoka** — Echigo's largest goze organisation. **[U] on the founding account, which the
Japanese Wikipedia article itself hedges** (「史実か否かは疑念が残り」, *doubt remains as to whether
it is historical fact*): a blind woman called 「ごい」 born into the **牧野 Makino** house (the
Nagaoka castle lords) became the adopted daughter of the retainer **山本 Yamamoto** house, branched
out to Yanagihara-chō at the end of Genroku, and **in 1725 (享保10) became the goze-gashira governing
five districts (Koshi, Santō, Kariwa, Uonuma, Kubiki) plus Makino lands, taking the name 山本ごい
Yamamoto Goi**. Her goze-ya was the origin of the fire in the **Nagaoka great fire of 1728
(享保13)**, for which she was ordered out of town and rebuilt on 60 tsubo behind Daiku-chō; later,
with permission from the 寺社奉行, she obtained the designation 「カサマ」 and began assembling her
subordinate goze **every 7th day of the 3rd month as the 妙音講** to read out the regulations and
adjudicate petitions. **Yamamoto Goi became a hereditary title.** Meeting calendar per the
regulations: a general meeting with the myōonkō every April; extraordinary meetings in August and
November; and a **身上分け shinshō-wake** meeting of the oyakata over two or three days from
20 October covering accounts, the spring myōonkō, tour schedules, and rewards and punishments for
registered goze — **attendance by proxy not permitted**.
**[V] Peak: over 400 members in the 1880s–90s (明治20年代).** Reorganised from the 大工町瞽女組合 into
the **『中越瞽女矯風会』 Chūetsu Goze Kyōfūkai** — **[U] the date: Japanese Wikipedia gives
"1893年（明治31年）" in two places, which is internally inconsistent (明治26 = 1893, 明治31 = 1898)
and I cannot resolve it.** After the reorganisation membership was restricted in principle to the
blind (sighted goze had previously been admitted), **though in practice sighted goze remained
numerous and indispensable as guides.** Decline from late Meiji; wartime attrition to 70–80; **the
goze-ya burned down in the Nagaoka air raid of 1 August 1945, with one goze burned to death**; and
**in October 1964 the death of the last Yamamoto Goi, a goze-gashira called マス Masu, caused the
Nagaoka goze organisation to collapse.**

### 3.3 小林ハル Kobayashi Haru and 杉本キクエ Sugimoto Kikue — and the Living National Treasure error

**[V] Kobayashi Haru's dates: 24 January 1900 (明治33) – 25 April 2005 (平成17), of 老衰 (senility),
aged 105.** Born at 三貫地, **井栗村 Ikuri village, 南蒲原郡 Minami-Kanbara district**, Niigata — now
**三条市 Sanjō City** (the village name 三貫地新田 survives in the modern address) — youngest of four
children of a farming family of 庄屋 rank with tenants and servants. **Blind from about three months
old, following cataracts.** Died at **黒川村 Kurokawa village, 北蒲原郡 Kita-Kanbara district — now
胎内市 Tainai City, where she is buried.**

**[V] Training and career.** Began goze training at **five, in 1905**, under **樋口フジ Higuchi
Fuji**. **First tour late 1908**, aged about eight. Changed teachers more than once: **初次サワ
Hatsuji Sawa in 1915 (大正4)** — on a 21-year term, with **direct permission from 山本ゴイ Yamamoto
Goi**, who praised her playing, gave her the red half-collar undergarment permitted only to
full-fledged goze, and gave her the goze name **チヨノ Chiyono** — then, after Sawa's death in 1921,
**酒井ツル Sakai Tsuru**; later she led tours herself. She toured all of Niigata except
Nishikubiki-gun, plus the Yonezawa and Oguni districts of Yamagata and the Minami-Aizu district of
Fukushima.
**[V] — and a nuance usually dropped: she retired and then resumed.** She **retired from performing
on 28 May 1973**, on entering a nursing home — **which is what ended the Nagaoka lineage of goze
activity** — and then **resumed after 1977**, on moving to the **胎内やすらぎの家 Tainai Yasuragi no
Ie** home, where she was **recorded and broadcast and took new students**. So the four-way ending
table above needs this fifth line: *the touring tradition ends in 1977, but Haru's performing career
runs on past it inside an institution, as documentation rather than livelihood.*
**[U] Two small discrepancies between the two readings of the Japanese Wikipedia article I have:**
the second teacher's surname appears as **初次サワ** in one reading and **ハツジサワ** in the other
(same reading, different orthography), and the third as **酒井ツル** in one and **坂井ツル** in the
other. Check the article directly; 酒井 is the commoner surname and the better bet.

Sources: ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/小林ハル (read 2026-11-13; the second reading cites revision
111678015), cross-checked against English Wikipedia "Haru Kobayashi."

#### [V] — CORRECTION: she was not a Living National Treasure

This is a category error, and a common one.

- **What she actually held, with the exact date.** Japanese Wikipedia 小林ハル *(my translation)*:
  **瞽女唄 was selected as a 『記録作成等の措置を講ずべき無形文化財』** — "an intangible cultural
  property for which measures such as record-making should be taken" — and **she was recognised as
  its holder (保持者) on 25 March 1978 (昭和53)**. The article states explicitly that she is
  **「人間国宝と呼ばれることもある」, "sometimes called a Living National Treasure,"** because of this
  recognition — i.e. it flags the label as informal.
- **[V] Her other honours, dated:** **黄綬褒章 (Medal with Yellow Ribbon), 29 April 1979**; honorary
  citizen of **Sanjō City, 6 September 2001**; the **36th 吉川英治文化賞 (Yoshikawa Eiji Culture
  Prize), 2002**.
- **The sharper point, and the one to make in the reference document: the instrument the state
  reached for was *record-making*.** 記録作成等の措置を講ずべき無形文化財 is, by its own name, a
  designation for traditions the state has concluded are ending — the policy response is
  **documentation, not transmission**. The 1978 selection of goze-uta is therefore not an honour so
  much as an official death certificate with an archive attached. Groemer's chapter 6 closes the
  loop from the other side: the surviving performers are not blind, and the repertory has fared
  worse in the market than "folk."
- **The category is legally distinct.** Japanese Wikipedia 記録作成等の措置を講ずべき無形文化財: this
  category — also called **選択無形文化財 sentaku mukei bunkazai** — is defined as
  「無形文化財のうち、重要無形文化財以外のもの」, i.e. **intangible cultural properties OTHER THAN
  Important Intangible Cultural Properties**, for which part of the cost of recording, preservation
  and publication may be met from public funds. It is **selected by the Commissioner of the Agency
  for Cultural Affairs under 文化財保護法 art. 77(1)**, and the criterion for performing arts is
  being "valuable for knowing the process of change in our country's performing arts." **56 items
  have been selected in the 音楽 category.**
- **人間国宝 is something else.** Japanese Wikipedia 人間国宝: it is the **informal term for an
  individual certified individually (各個認定) as the holder of an *Important* Intangible Cultural
  Property under 文化財保護法 art. 71(2)**, carrying an **annual special subsidy of ¥2,000,000** and
  a budgeted cap of roughly 116 living holders. **Checking the full 人間国宝 list in the 芸能/音楽
  category, neither 小林ハル nor 杉本キクイ appears.** The music categories listed are 琵琶, 尺八,
  箏曲, 地歌, 長唄, 義太夫節, 一中節, 河東節, 宮薗節, 新内節, 常磐津節, 清元節, 琉球古典音楽 —
  **there is no goze-uta entry.**
- **Nor was it the other designations sometimes claimed.** It was **not** an **重要無形民俗文化財**
  (Important Intangible Folk Cultural Property): goze-uta was selected under the 芸能/音楽 heading,
  not the 民俗文化財 heading, which has its own parallel 記録作成 category. **Nor 選定保存技術**,
  which covers conservation *techniques*.
- **English Wikipedia is wrong on both women.** Its "Haru Kobayashi" article says "She continued
  performing until 1978" (the Japanese article gives retirement in **1973**) and "In 1978, she was
  named one of the **Living National Treasures** of Japan" (wrong category). Its "Goze" article says
  "Sugimoto Kikue of Takada (1898–1983), who was designated a **Living National Treasure** *ningen
  kokuhō* in **1971**" — **wrong category *and* wrong year**; the Japanese article gives
  **17 April 1970**.
- **Recommended wording:** *In 1978 "goze-uta" was selected as an intangible cultural property
  warranting documentation (記録作成等の措置を講ずべき無形文化財), and Kobayashi Haru was certified as
  its holder; she received the Medal with Yellow Ribbon in 1979. This is a lesser and legally
  distinct designation from the Important Intangible Cultural Property holdership informally called
  "Living National Treasure," and neither she nor Sugimoto Kikue appears on that list.*

#### [V] "The last goze" is a simplification — and there are two competing "lasts"

- Japanese Wikipedia 小林ハル, lead: 「数多くの苦難を経て晩年に**「最後の長岡瞽女」、「最後の瞽女」**
  として脚光を浴びた」 — *after many hardships she came into the spotlight in old age as "the last
  Nagaoka goze" and "the last goze."* **The narrower formulation — last *Nagaoka* goze — is the
  defensible one.** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 puts it as 「**最後まで活躍した長岡瞽女**」, "the Nagaoka
  goze who remained active longest," and 「**唯一の長岡瞽女唄伝承者**」, "the sole transmitter of
  Nagaoka goze-uta."
- **杉本キクイ／キクエ Sugimoto Kikui/Kikue is the parallel "last" for the Takada line.**
  **[V]** Japanese Wikipedia 杉本キクイ *(my translation)*: "**Sugimoto Kikui (5 March 1898 –
  30 March 1983), a Japanese goze. In her later years she was called 'the last oyakata of the
  Takada goze' (高田瞽女最後の親方).** From going on tour at eight until she retired in 1973 she
  toured mainly the three Kubiki districts of Niigata and the Ueda district of Nagano." Born at
  東中島, 諏訪村, 中頸城郡 — now **上越市東中島** — eldest daughter of 青木久治 and ウミ;
  **blinded at six by measles through a doctor's misdiagnosis**; formally apprenticed to
  **杉本マセ Sugimoto Mase** on **9 March 1904**, aged seven, and taken on tour to Nishikubiki a
  month later; succeeded to the headship of the Sugimoto house on **17 March 1932** (her senior
  disciple and second-generation oyakata 杉本カツ had died suddenly on tour on 13 March 1919, and
  Mase died 21 February 1932). Her adopted daughter **五十嵐シズ Igarashi Shizu** (apprenticed
  24 March 1922, aged 7) became the last goze of the Sugimoto house; her guide
  **難波コトミ Nanba Kotomi** entered the house in December 1929, aged 16. **Postwar, with the
  collapse of the landlord class that had provided goze lodgings under land reform, the Takada goze
  were reduced to the single Sugimoto house**; Kikui, Shizu and Kotomi **stopped touring with the
  autumn tour of October 1964.** Afterwards: the 20th National Folk Performing Arts Festival
  (Ministry of Education), Nippon Seinenkan, October 1965, as Niigata's representative; Akasaka Hall
  February 1967; **National Theatre August 1969**; **certified holder of the 記録作成等の措置を講ず
  べき無形文化財 「瞽女唄」 on 17 April 1970**; **the first goze to receive the 黄綬褒章, on
  7 November 1973, presented by the then prime minister 田中角栄 Tanaka Kakuei**, a fellow Niigata
  native. Records: 『越後の瞽女唄』 (CBS Sony 1973), 『越後瞽女のうた』 (Columbia 1975), 『瞽女』
  (CBS Sony 1975), 『杉本キクエ 越後瞽女の唄』 (Crown 1977), 『越後高田瞽女唄』 (King 1978);
  Jōetsu City recorded her repertoire 1975–79. **Died 30 March 1983, aged 85**, of cerebral
  infarction at the prefectural central hospital.
  **A closing detail worth the space** (same article, *my translation*): Shizu and Kotomi moved to
  the 胎内やすらぎの家 home on 21 April 1984, where by the 1980s nearly all surviving Niigata goze
  lived, **including Kobayashi Haru of the Nagaoka organisation** — and since goze of the Takada and
  Nagaoka organisations had never had contact, their meeting and living together was
  「**歴史的な光景**」, "a historic sight."

#### [V] The name-reading problem — and Groemer's own ruling on it

Groemer, JJRS 2014, p. 350 n.2, verbatim: "**I give her name as Kikue because she insisted that this
is correct (see Suzuki 1999, 121) and because this is how it appears on her grave marker. Her
household register, however, gives it as 'Kikui' and many scholars prefer the latter spelling. The
sounds 'e' and 'i' are often reversed in Echigo dialect, and the household register may well be
based on an oral transmission to a clerk who wrote down the name as he heard it.**"
So: **Groemer writes Kikue; Japanese Wikipedia titles the article キクイ Kikui; her own published
books use both** (『瞽女の語る昔話 杉本キクヱ媼昔話集』, 三弥井書店 1975; 『わたしは瞽女 杉本
キクエ口伝』, 音楽之友社 1977, new ed. March 1999, ISBN 4-276-21384-3). **Give both and give the
reason** — it is a small, exact illustration of how this material reaches us.
**[V] One apparent Wikipedia error to avoid repeating:** Japanese Wikipedia 瞽女 writes
「杉本キクイ（別名：杉本ハル）」, "alias Sugimoto Haru." The dedicated 杉本キクイ article gives only
キクエ/キクヱ as variants. This looks like a collision with Kobayashi Haru. **Do not write "Sugimoto
Haru."**

**[V] Two other goze worth having by name:** **伊平タケ Ihira Take** — the representative
Meiji-to-early-Shōwa goze, who performed on NHK soon after it opened and cut SP records (Japanese
Wikipedia 瞽女; Groemer quotes her reminiscences, JJRS p. 355, citing Suzuki et al. 1976, 41–42); and
**関根ヤス Sekine Yasu (1893–?)** of Nagaoka, quoted above.

### 3.4 Groemer's corrections to the romantic version

The book's body text is paywalled **[U]**, but the publisher's abstract, every chapter abstract, the
Preface and Introduction extracts, the full open-access JJRS article, and a **verbatim quotation of
the book's Preface in an open-access review** all converge. The review is **[V]**: Gisa Jähnichen,
"Review of *Goze…* by Gerald Groemer, 2016," *Asian-European Music Research Journal* 10 (2022):
85–88, DOI 10.30819/aemr.10-10 — **open-access PDF read in full** at
logos-verlag.de/PDFS/aemr.10-10.pdf. (Publisher-direct PDFs are the trick that works where JSTOR and
MUSE refuse.)

**1. Against essentialism and *nihonjinron* — the sharpest statement, quoted verbatim from the book
in that review:**
> "**This struggle and its effects, not some timeless 'goze mind', enigmatic Japanese essence, or
> immovable 'values', shaped much of the course of goze history. Despite living in a land of
> authoritarian political institutions, patriarchal social structures, Confucianist calls for social
> unity, and Buddhist talk of the 'end of history', from the start of the Edo period goze began to
> actively pursue what I shall call their 'emancipatory interests'.**"

**2. Against Yanagita-style accounts of a unitary "Japanese people."** Groemer, JJRS 41/2 (2014),
pp. 347–349, names his targets: "**The seeming homogeneity and harmony fostered by Japanese
religious practice led Yanagita Kunio to spend his life arguing for a unique religiosity of the
'Japanese people.' In the 1970s the same notions motivated folklorists, including Miyata Noboru and
others, to find in 'folk beliefs' (*minkan shinkō*) a 'traditional religion cultivated from the time
of the birth of an ethnic group'.**" He adds Reader and Tanabe (1998) and Miyake (2001), and
concludes: "**such writers share the view that Japanese religious practice has served primarily as a
force generating social unity, community, and harmony.**" His counter-programme: "**Rather than
turning 'practice' into a bland abstraction that permits 'the Japanese people' to be mashed into a
homogeneous unity, practice becomes a means for highlighting difference and multiplicity.** When
thought and practice turn out to exhibit performative contradictions, analysis may reveal **how
ideals become ideology.**"

**3. Against the mystical wandering-bard image, in favour of social determination.** JJRS 2014,
p. 349: "**Social differences, not degrees of divine efficacy in bringing about desired ends,
determined that the child of a bankrupt farmer would end up a mendicant religious itinerant
performing exorcisms or benedictions at doorsteps, while the local intendant's son who had 'forsaken
the world' would busy himself reading sutras and climbing the ladder of the Buddhist ecclesiastical
order.**" And: "the question to be asked is thus not merely how Buddhas, kami, and other divinities
inspired princes and paupers alike to seek 'worldly benefits,' but **how certain forms of religious
practice were used by various social groups or classes, who benefitted to what degree**."

**4. Against disability-as-victimhood.** The OUP publisher's abstract: "The book presents goze as
**skilled artisans and agents of rural cultural development, not as helpless victims of
disability**." And chapter 1's abstract makes the constructivist case: whether someone counts as
visually disabled is **historically and socially shaped, not objective**; **economic pressure,
religious discourse and medical discourse** are the forces that produced "blindness" in early modern
Japan; together they produced a **"heteronomous and subaltern disabled subject"** — and **goze
contested these determinations in practice by establishing their occupational associations.**

**5. Against the "artful begging at doorsteps" picture.** Jähnichen on chapter 4 (AEMR p. 86 — the
reviewer's wording, not Groemer's, but testimony to the chapter's point): "**He is clearing the
persistent imagination of doing some artful begging at doorsteps. Thus, he is elevating the singing
of richly equipped songs on available stages within village communities as far as it was allowed to
do so.**" Consistent with the book's "professional singers" framing, the legitimacy argument of
chapter 2, and the respectability argument in JJRS 2014. **The usable formulation: goze did do
door-to-door rounds, but the door-to-door round was the advertising; the paid evening concert of
danmono at the village head's house was the business.**

**6. Against identity-based scholarship.** The Preface (academic.oup.com/book/4543/chapter/146630045)
opens on why a non-Japanese sighted man of the twenty-first century would study visually disabled
Japanese women musicians, observes that the question assumes "**only identity can generate interest
or concern**," lists his usual replies (he once made a living performing music for little pay; he has
a minor visual problem; "we are all human") and calls them "**hopelessly feeble**," proposing instead
to seek the connection "on the broader terrain of social and political realities rather than in the
narrow realm of the individual," and to acknowledge "the glaring differences and asymmetries among
us," including that goze "are no more."

**7. On the Meiji state: "Coerced Liberation."** The chapter title does the work. **[V]** Chapter 5
abstract (pp. 188–204): the new regime **outlawed practices it considered "beggarly," and many
regional governments abolished goze organisations** — Niigata prefecture did not impose a wholesale
ban. Goze populations **first grew** as they used the new "freedoms" the shift to capitalism offered;
after about two decades they **could not compete with commercial music spreading through the
countryside**; populations dwindled, associations weakened as members left their teachers, and
**no adequate new educational institutions replaced the old associations**. The Second World War
weakened things further, and goze had "for all practical purposes become extinct."
**[V] Jähnichen's summary of the same chapter** (AEMR p. 87) adds the mechanism she takes Groemer to
be arguing: "the reason for this painful decline might have been not the so-called 'modernization' …
**The comparably low degree of respect towards musical skills perpetuated by state laws that banned
dance and entertainment** and religious views on the working processes in life contributed
remarkably to the decline. … **The few survivors were all concentrated in the hands of new business
people who exploited the traditional artists in a ruthless way.**"
**[V] And the Japanese account of the mechanism, from a non-Groemer source**, Japanese Wikipedia
瞽女 *(my translation)*: "**In the 11th month of 1871 a Dajōkan proclamation issued the decree
abolishing blind offices; blind men were reorganised as a trade association for acupuncture-massage
and for the performing arts, but because goze held no privileged official ranks (特権官位) they were
unaffected — and so, left without any relief measures, they fell behind in adapting to
modernisation, and some turned to begging.**" This is exactly the "liberated into destitution"
shape, arrived at independently.
**[U] Begging-law / 乞食 regulation specifically: I could not verify anything general.** The nearest
attested items are a **domain-level ban** — Groemer, JJRS p. 358 n.14: "**the 1799 ban in Hanpō-shū
2: 319 (law no. 302), which prohibits *eta* both from performing *harugoma* and playing the shamisen
in the Tottori domain. The law was repealed in 1858**" **[V]** — and the 1839 Kantō law-enforcement
bureau's distribution of the Buddhist hymn 「孝行和讃」 as an instance of state intervention in
popular morality. **The volume to consult is Groemer's *Street Performers and Society in Urban
Japan, 1600–1900: The Beggar's Gift*.** (Cross-reference `history.md` §2.3 of `history.md`: the Tenpō 13
(1842) edict in 『浮世の有様』 confiscating and burning the shamisen of 非人 and 乞食 is exactly this
kind of regulation, from a primary source.)

**8. The contrast in two quotations.** The sentimental account is easy to source. **斎藤真一 Saitō
Shin'ichi, 『瞽女』, subtitled 「盲目の旅芸人」 ("Blind Itinerant Performers"), 1972, winner of the
21st Japan Essayists' Club Prize** (デジタル大辞泉プラス via kotobank/瞽女) — the painter-writer's
reportage that did most to form the popular image; he is also listed among those who sent flowers to
Sugimoto Kikui's wake. And **世界大百科事典's own 瞽女 entry** supplies the essentialism verbatim:
the repertoire's 「暗く哀調を帯びた芸風」, *dark, melancholy artistic style*, was created by "**being
women, bearing the hardship of physical disability, and undergoing severe training within the
organisation from childhood to become full-fledged artists — such circumstances, one may think**"
*(my translation)*. Set that beside Groemer's "**not some timeless 'goze mind'**" and the
disagreement is on the page in two sentences.

**[V] A caution about chapter numbers — two conventions are in circulation and they differ by one.**
Oxford Academic's own chapter pages number the **Introduction separately**, so that *The Production
of Visual Disability* = **ch. 1**, *Touring and Performing in Echigo* = **ch. 4**, *Endings: Coerced
Liberation* = **ch. 5** (the page title literally reads "5 Endings: Coerced Liberation"), and *After
the End* = **ch. 6**. Other readings of the same table of contents count the Introduction as chapter
1, giving *Endings* = ch. 6 and *After the End* = ch. 7; Jähnichen's review does this too (her "fifth
chapter … 'Touring and Performing in Echigo' (page 120)" is OUP's ch. 4), and she additionally
describes the first two substantive chapters in the wrong order. **Cite by title and page range, not
by number.** The page ranges are stable and are the ones in the table at the head of this part.

**[V] Groemer's epigraph is worth quoting, because it states his method.** The Introduction opens on
Walter Benjamin, "Theses on the Philosophy of History" (1940): "**Is there not an echo of what has
been silenced in the voices to which we lend our ears?**" — and Groemer notes that **this line is
missing from the standard English translation**, citing Benjamin 2007a:254 against the German
Benjamin 1991:693. The Introduction's visible section headings — "**Early Encounters with the West:
Heine the Observer and Hearn the Interpreter**," "**Japanese Encounters: A Brief Overview**,"
"**Toward the Present: Writing Between Speech and Silence**" — show that the chapter is a
historiographical critique running from Wilhelm Heine and Lafcadio Hearn through the Japanese
folklorists. **[U]** The sections themselves are paywalled.

### 3.5 坊様 / ぼさま / ボサマ bosama — and the handover to Tsugaru

This is where the shamisen's history stops being mine and becomes the Tsugaru file's. Two things are
worth establishing here: that the term is attested in Japanese scholarship and not only in
Wikipedia, and that the standard account of the descent is **explicitly flagged as unreliable by the
Japanese sources themselves.**

**[V] The term is in the reference literature, and it is broader than "Tsugaru-shamisen player."**
改訂新版世界大百科事典, entry 盲人, section 盲人の歴史, signed **加藤康昭** (via kotobank/盲人)
*(my translation)*: "In the Tōhoku region, blind shamanesses called **itako, ichiko, waka** made
their living from spirit-mediumship (口寄せ), prayers and divination, turning *oshira* dolls and
reciting *saimon* for household well-being. **On the other hand, the ボサマ bosama (blind men), who
were in many cases their husbands, developed the Yoshitsune story by weaving in Tōhoku legends.
These bosama narratives were on one hand carried to Kyoto as material for the *Gikeiki*, and on the
other developed into the richly local narrative genre peculiar to this region, 奥浄瑠璃
oku-jōruri.**"
**This widens the picture usefully.** In the standard Japanese scholarly account, **bosama are the
blind male narrative performers of Tōhoku generally**, paired sociologically with the **itako**, and
associated with **奥浄瑠璃**, not specifically or only with Tsugaru-shamisen. **The Tsugaru-shamisen
connection is one regional strand of a larger occupational category.**
**[V] A supporting datum for the itako–bosama marriage pattern:** Takahashi Chikuzan married
**ナヨ Nayo in 1938, who was an itako** (Japanese Wikipedia 高橋竹山).

**[V] Orthography and reading.** 新撰 芸能人物事典 明治～平成 (日外アソシエーツ, 2010), entry
仁太坊, uses **坊様 with the reading bōsama**; Japanese Wikipedia uses **ボサマ / ぼさま**; there is
**no Japanese Wikipedia article at ぼさま**. **Report all three forms.**

**[V] A general-reader gloss.** Japanese Wikipedia 高橋竹山: 「14歳の時、隣村の**ボサマ（盲目の
門付芸人）**であった**戸田重次郎**に弟子入りして三味線を習う」 — *at fourteen he apprenticed himself
to Toda Jūjirō, a bosama (blind door-to-door performing artist) of the neighbouring village, and
learned the shamisen* *(my translation)*.

**[V] 仁太坊 Nitabō, from a published biographical dictionary rather than from folklore.**
新撰 芸能人物事典 明治～平成 (日外アソシエーツ, 2010), via kotobank/仁太坊 *(my translation)*:
"Occupation: **Tsugaru-shamisen player**. Real name **秋元仁太郎 Akimoto Nitarō**. Born **Ansei 4
[1857]**, birthplace **Mutsu province (Kanagi, Aomori prefecture)**. **Born the child of a blind
woman shamisen player**; lost his sight at eight to **smallpox**; lost both parents at eleven.
**Around this time he met a stray goze (はぐれ瞽女) and followed her about, and is said to have
learned the goze art.** By **Meiji 4 [1871], the year of the abolition of the domains, he was at
fourteen already a competent shamisen player, and thereafter made his living as a kadozuke performer
called 「坊様」.** In his art he **broke with old practice to devise a powerful 叩き tataki (striking)
technique using the 太棹**, and further created **八人芸** ("eight-person art," performing eight
people's parts alone). **It is held that tracing the lineage of Tsugaru-shamisen, which today stands
as contemporary music on the shamisen, leads back to Nitabō.** Died **2 January Shōwa 3 [1928]**."
**Note two things for the Tsugaru file: the goze connection is in the dictionary entry itself, and
the entry's own framing of the lineage claim is 「とされる」.**

**[V] The descent from the goze, and the honest statement of the evidence problem.** Japanese
Wikipedia 津軽三味線 *(my translation)*:
> 「**津軽三味線の楽曲の原型は、新潟地方の瞽女（ごぜ）の三味線と言われる。**その他、北前船によって
> 日本海側各地の音楽が津軽に伝わり、津軽民謡は独特の発達をみる。**しかし、津軽地方においては
> ボサマと言われる男性視覚障害者の門付け芸として長く蔑まれていた。そのためもあり、津軽三味線の
> 起源をたどることができる文献はごく僅かで、歴史に関してはおおむね口伝に依る。**」
> — "**The prototype of Tsugaru-shamisen pieces is said to be the shamisen of the goze of the
> Niigata region.** Besides this, music from the various Japan-Sea-coast regions was brought to
> Tsugaru by the **kitamae-bune** trading ships, and Tsugaru folk song developed distinctively.
> **But in Tsugaru it was long despised as the door-to-door art of visually disabled men called
> bosama. Partly for that reason, there is very little literature by which the origins of
> Tsugaru-shamisen can be traced, and its history relies largely on oral transmission.**"

**[V] And the caveat the Tsugaru file must carry, from the Japanese side, not just from Groemer**
(same article, *my translation*):
> 「**多くの口伝を調査・記録し、文書としてまとめたのは大條和雄がほぼ初めてであり、現在認識されている
> 津軽三味線の歴史については、彼の研究成果に依るところが大きい。しかし、それもまたあくまで伝承を
> 再構築したものに過ぎず、多分にフィクション的要素も含まれているため、史料としての価値は低い。**」
> — "**Daijō Kazuo was effectively the first to investigate and record the many oral transmissions
> and compile them in written form, and the currently accepted history of Tsugaru-shamisen depends
> largely on his research. But that too is no more than a reconstruction of oral tradition, and
> because it contains a great deal of fictional element, its value as a historical source is low.**"

**[V] And Groemer is the English-language name for the same objection** — though at one remove.
English Wikipedia's "Tsugaru-jamisen" article: "Some consensus is that the style was developed by
homeless and blind individuals called **bosama**. One scholar, **Daijō Kazuo, proposed that the
genre originated from a bosama named Nitabō based on interviews with musicians and their families.
According to his research, Nitabō acquired and modified a shamisen in 1877**, for which he adopted a
different playing style — he rounded off the plectrum so it was shaped like a rice paddle, held the
shamisen upright, included the area around the bridge as playing area, and incorporated beating and
slapping the strings. **However, other scholars, such as Gerald Groemer, argue that the account
advanced by [Daijō] may not be entirely accurate due to a lack of documentation.**" **[U]** I did not
read Groemer saying this; *The Spirit of Tsugaru* (1999) is where he says it.

**[V] The mechanism by which the instrument changed — Chikuzan's own explanation, which is the best
thing in this whole section.** Japanese Wikipedia 津軽三味線, quoting Chikuzan's book
*(my translation)*: "**if there was a festival, bosama would line up in rows in the shrine
precincts, playing the shamisen as though competing with one another.** In that environment, those
called the masters of Tsugaru-shamisen's founding period came to pursue **louder sound and flashier
technique in order to stand out from the other bosama. The shamisen, which until then had used the
same chūzao or hosozao as the goze, became the futozao**, the plectrum became small for fast
playing, and the music too changed into something complex and high-tempo, partly using the
percussive technique called 'tataki.'" And Chikuzan in his own voice:
「**三味線なんて音が大きく出ればそれで良かった。音が大きいから太棹を選んだんだ**」 — "**All that
mattered with a shamisen was that it be loud. I chose the thick neck because it was loud.**"
**That sentence explains the futozao, the tataki technique and the genre's whole aesthetic as a
competitive adaptation to begging at festivals — which is a far better story than any essentialist
account of "the Tsugaru spirit."**

**[V] The naming.** The music was simply called 「津軽もの」 until the **1960s (昭和40年代)**
folk-song boom, when **三橋美智也 Mihashi Michiya** and others began calling it 「津軽三味線」, after
which the name stuck. (Cross-reference `genres.md` Part 4: 日本大百科全書's 津軽三味線 entry,
signed 竹内勉, dates the breakthrough 曲弾き shows by Mihashi and 木田林松栄 to **around 1956** and
says the name became general from about then.)

**[V] Takahashi Chikuzan, verified.** 初代高橋竹山, **18 June 1910 – 5 February 1998**, real name
**高橋定蔵 Takahashi Teizō**; born at 小湊, 中平内村, 東津軽郡, Aomori (now 平内町); **blinded at
three by complications of measles**; **at 14 apprenticed to the bosama 戸田重次郎**; **independent at
17, doing kadozuke through Aomori, Iwate, Akita and Hokkaidō**; survived the 1933 Shōwa Sanriku
tsunami, which destroyed the inn he was staying in; **1944, with shamisen work no longer viable in
wartime, entered the Aomori Prefectural Hachinohe School for the Blind and Deaf to qualify as
acupuncturist and masseur**; from 1950 toured as accompanist to **成田雲竹 Narita Unchiku**, who gave
him the stage name 竹山; **1963, King Records issued the first-ever Tsugaru-shamisen solo LP,
『津軽三味線 高橋竹山』**, a major hit; **1975, autobiography 『津軽三味線ひとり旅』**; 1986 US tour
of seven cities; died of laryngeal cancer. The article's 著書 section lists **both of Groemer's
books** as the English-language works on him. It also records the 「叩き三味線」/「弾き三味線」
distinction and notes that **the famous attribution of 「弾き三味線」 to Chikuzan arose from his
misunderstanding an interviewer's question** — folklore about the folklore, and a nice caution.
**[V] Nitabō's pupils** (English Wikipedia Tsugaru-jamisen): **Kinobō and Chōsakubō**, and his last
pupil **Shirakawa Gunpachirō**, who performed outside Tsugaru with a folk troupe and in Tokyo
concert halls, popularising the style in the 1920s; it waned during the Second Sino-Japanese War and
revived 1955–65 with migration to the cities; **Gunpachirō's 1959 performance with the enka star
Mihashi Michiya at the Nihon Theatre, Tokyo** gave it mass exposure.

### 3.7 Claims in this part I would NOT print without a better source

- The tōdōza's total membership (~3,000) and its 1534 → 1657 growth (445 → 2,051 men) — English
  Wikipedia, sourced to a 1997 dissertation and a blog, **and not to Groemer**.
- The 職惣検校's standing as "equivalent to a daimyō of about 150,000 koku" — Japanese Wikipedia,
  uncited.
- **Dajōkan Proclamation No. 568** and the day **11/3** for the 1871 abolition — Wikipedia only. The
  year (Meiji 4 / 1871) is independently solid from 世界大百科事典 twice over.
- 1893 vs 1898 for the founding of the 中越瞽女矯風会 — Japanese Wikipedia is internally
  contradictory.
- A **legal** tōdōza monopoly on *teaching* jiuta, koto or shamisen. The sources support "sought
  occupational monopolisation" and "developed and transmitted within the tōdō shokuyashiki system,"
  which is weaker.
- Readings I flag rather than guess: **久我家** Kuga vs Koga; **天夜尊** vs **雨夜尊**; the father of
  **人康親王** — Ninmyō (most sources) vs "Ninmyō or Kōkō" (世界大百科事典); **杉本キクイ / キクエ /
  キクヱ**; **坊様 bōsama / ボサマ / ぼさま**; **職屋敷** shokuyashiki vs shikiyashiki (ブリタニカ).
