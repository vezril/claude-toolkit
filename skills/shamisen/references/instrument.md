# The Shamisen as a Physical Object

Scope: construction, materials, variants, acoustics, maintenance, buying.
All web sources fetched **2026-11-13** unless a different date is stated. Every claim is
tagged **[V]** (verified against a named source I actually read) or **[U]** (unverified /
inference / flagged).

Scope note: this document deliberately excludes history, the Tsugaru tradition as a
musical culture, and technique/repertoire, which other briefs cover.

## Conventions and units

Japanese instrument trade still works in *shakkanhō* units, and almost every real
specification is quoted in them. Conversions used throughout:

| Unit | Value | Note |
|---|---|---|
| 1 尺 *shaku* | 30.30 cm | |
| 1 寸 *sun* | 3.030 cm | |
| 1 分 *bu* | 3.030 mm | |
| 1 厘 *rin* | 0.303 mm | |
| 1 匁 *monme* | 3.75 g | bachi and string sizing |

**[V]** standard metric definitions of the shakkanhō units (1 shaku = 10/33 m, 1 monme =
3.75 g); the figures above are arithmetic from that. Japanese sources quote widths like
「8分4厘」and I give both forms.

Romanisation: modified Hepburn. I write *dō*, *sao*, *kōki*, *chūzao* with macrons but
keep the common unmacroned spellings (*dou*, *koki*) when quoting a retailer who uses them.

## 1. Overall anatomy

### 1.1 The three major assemblies

A shamisen is three things bolted together: a skin-covered box, a long fretless neck, and
a pegbox.

- **胴 *dō*** — the soundbox. A four-sided wooden frame, open front and back, with a skin
  membrane glued over each face. **[V]** (Yoshikawa 2010: "a small body, the front and
  back of which are covered with white cat skin"; ja.wikipedia 三味線.)
- **棹 *sao*** — the neck. Fretless, slimmer than a guitar or banjo neck, and it runs
  *through* the body: the tapered end that emerges at the bottom of the dō, the
  **中子先 *nakagosaki***, is what the tailpiece cord loops over. **[V]** (en.wikipedia
  Shamisen: the neck "extends through the body as an anchor for the strings"; it also lists
  *nakagosaki* as "spike".)
- **天神 *tenjin*** — the head/pegbox, holding the three pegs. Also called **海老尾 *ebio***
  (lit. "shrimp tail") from its backward curl. **[V]** (en.wikipedia Shamisen lists both
  *tenjin* and *ebio*; the bent-back head shape is visible on every instrument photo.)

#### Corrections to common English descriptions

- **乳袋 *chibukuro* is at the TOP of the neck, not at the body end.** y-eisui's
  dimensions post says the neck face-width is measured 「乳袋の直下」— directly below the
  chibukuro — and metal-shamisen's Tsugaru page describes the same measurement as
  「棹の最上部の幅（面幅）」, "the width at the very top of the neck." **[V]** The
  "shoulders where the neck meets the body" is **鳩胸 *hatomune*** (en.wikipedia Shamisen:
  "the pronounced curve where the neck meets the body"). **[V]**
- **指掛け *yubikake* is a left-hand glove-sleeve, not a thumb slide on the neck.**
  metal-shamisen `lesson03`: it is worn 「親指と人差指に装着します」— on the thumb and
  index finger — so the hand can *suri* (slide) along the neck without the left thumb
  bracing. **[V]** Bachido gives the physical reason: the sao is highly polished, playing
  requires constant sliding, and bare skin builds friction. **[V]** **指すり *yubisuri*** is
  used as an alternative name for the same accessory — wagakki-ichiba lists the product
  category as 「指掛け 指すり」 together. **[V]** Any claim that they are two different
  objects is **[U]**; I found no source distinguishing them.
- **上駒 *kamigoma* is metal.** Bachido: "the kamigoma (metal strip)". **[V]** English
  sources that call it a "nut" imply bone/ivory; on a shamisen it is a thin metal bar.

### 1.3 Why the neck is in three pieces (三つ折れ *mitsuore*)

- The standard neck splits into 上棹 / 中棹 / 下棹 (upper/middle/lower). **[V]** (y-eisui;
  ja.wikipedia 三味線.)
- ja.wikipedia 三味線 gives the reasons as **storage and portability, and reduced
  warping**. **[V]**
- Bachido adds a real playing benefit: the joints "connect perfectly on specific position
  marks… The hairline formed by the joint is almost like a fret, as it highlights certain
  positions to press the string" — useful on a fretless neck. **[V]** *This is Bachido's
  claim and I found no corroboration; treat the "it functions as a position reference"
  framing as one shop's observation, though the joints are plainly visible on the
  fingerboard.*
- The single-piece alternative is **延棹 *nobezao***. Bachido: "It has no real disadvantages,
  other than it can't be disassembled," and it is cheaper to make. **[V]** ja.wikipedia
  notes necks split into five or more pieces also exist. **[V]**
- Joint construction is a grade marker. The tenon is a **枘 / ほぞ *hozo***, and shops rate
  joints as 平ほぞ (plain, no groove) < 一本溝 (one groove) < 二本溝/二枚溝 (two grooves,
  one each side of the tenon) — more grooves, more stable. **[V]** (Yahoo! Chiebukuro
  q1341620713; note.com shimapan_tv; shamisen-mizuki.com, which rates 「上下二本溝」or
  「上二本・下段違い三本溝」as best and says one stepped three-groove variant is no longer
  made.) The Chiebukuro item is user Q&A — weaker — but the retailer pages agree.

### 1.4 Standard dimensions

These are the real numbers, almost all from Japanese shops.

**String length (kamigoma → koma):** **2尺6寸 ≈ 78.8 cm** on a 正寸 (standard-size)
instrument. **[V]** (y-eisui, 30 Apr 2024: 「上駒から２尺６寸」.) Short necks (短棹
*tanzao*) are 1寸, 1寸半 or 2寸 shorter, so a 2寸-short neck gives about 2尺4寸 ≈ 72.7 cm.
**[V]** (same source.) Independently, Yoshikawa's acoustic model uses a string length of
**0.80 m** — consistent. **[V]**

**Neck length is essentially constant across the three classes.** y-eisui: on 正寸
instruments the kamigoma-to-body distance is nearly the same whatever the neck thickness;
the National Theatre's teaching page agrees that 「棹の長さはほぼ同じ」and that what differs
is neck thickness, body thickness, skin thickness and weight. **[V]**
(www2.ntj.jac.go.jp digital-library teaching page, read via search snippet only — **[U]**
for the exact wording, **[V]** for the substance via y-eisui.)

**Body (dō), measured across the flat skin face** — y-eisui, 30 Apr 2024 **[V]**:

| Genre | Size name | Metric |
|---|---|---|
| 長唄 nagauta (baseline) | 6寸5分 × 5寸9分 | **≈ 197 × 179 mm** |
| 民謡 min'yō (chūzao) | 5厘大 (+0.15 mm) | ≈ 197 × 179 mm |
| 地唄 jiuta (chūzao) | 1分5厘大 or 2分大 | ≈ +4.5 to +6 mm |
| 津軽 Tsugaru (futozao) | 5分大 | ≈ +15 mm |

The 「〜大」 names mean "N *bu* larger than the nagauta baseline." **[V]**
metal-shamisen independently measures a Tsugaru 五分大胴 at **長手 ≈ 21.2 cm × 短手 ≈ 19.5 cm,
depth (重ね) ≈ 10.0 cm** — which is exactly 197+15 × 179+15. **[V]** Two independent
sources agreeing on the arithmetic is as good as this gets.

Variants: 四分大 bodies exist for smaller players and are less rounded **[V]**
(metal-shamisen); ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 says the Tsugaru standard is 五分 with 四分大 and
六分大 variants and 「明確な規格はない」— there is no hard standard **[V]**; a
「半津軽」*han-tsugaru* has a body around 三分大, quieter and lighter **[V]**
(shamisen-mizuki.com).

**Overall length:** Tsugaru **≈ 101–103 cm**, other shamisen **≈ 96–100 cm**. **[V]**
(metal-shamisen `lesson01`.) The shamisen neck is about **20 cm longer** than a sanshin's
and the body about "二回り" (two sizes) larger. **[V]** (y-eisui `shami.html`.)

**Peg (itomaki) outer diameter:** typically 8–9 分 ≈ **2.4–2.7 cm** on Tsugaru
instruments. **[V]** (metal-shamisen.)

**Bridge (koma) height**, by genre, in 分 (so 3.2 = 3.2 分 ≈ 9.7 mm):

| Genre | Height | Typical material |
|---|---|---|
| 長唄 nagauta | 3.2–3.6 | ivory, bone (*shari*), plastic |
| 地唄 jiuta | 2.6–2.8 | yellow or black water-buffalo horn (*suigyū*) |
| 津軽 / 民謡 | 2.6, sometimes 2.7–2.8 | bamboo base with ivory/bone/tortoiseshell top |

**[V]** en.wikipedia Shamisen, which also notes aged bamboo, rosewood and kōki as bridge
materials, and that higher bridges are louder but harder to control. en.wikipedia gives the
numbers bare, without units; reading them as 分 is my inference, consistent with physical
koma being ~8–11 mm tall — **[U]** on the unit, **[V]** on the relative ordering.

**Bridge position:** ideally set by string length from the kamigoma (2尺6寸), not by
distance from the neo. As a rough field guide y-eisui gives ~1.5–2 finger-widths from the
neo for nagauta and ~3 finger-widths for a 5分大 Tsugaru, and warns the correct position
**shifts by a few millimetres up to about 1 cm with humidity, temperature and skin
tension**. **[V]** Yoshikawa's simulation explains *why* this matters acoustically — see §2.6.

**Skin gluing margin:** a Tsugaru skin is left with **1–2 cm** of surplus around the body
edge for gluing, more than a nagauta skin, because it is thicker and pulled harder. **[V]**
(metal-shamisen.)

## 2. The sawari

This is the defining feature and the one most often got wrong. Getting it right requires
separating four things: the geometry, the string that is affected, the mechanism types, and
the acoustics.

### 2.1 The geometry: the first string does not clear a nut

On a shamisen, the **上駒 *kamigoma*** — a metal strip set into the top of the neck —
carries the **second and third strings only**. The **first (lowest, thickest) string passes
beside/below it and rests on the bare wood of the neck**, where a small ridge, the
**さわり山 *sawari-yama*** ("sawari mountain"), is cut. When the first string is sounded it
collides repeatedly against that ridge, producing the sustained buzz/jangle.

**[V], three independent sources:**

- Bachido Shamipedia, "Shamisen Materials": "the kamigoma (metal strip) is under the two
  thinner strings while the ichi no ito (thick string) rests on bare wood. When struck, the
  thick string vibrates against the little ridge (sawari yama), making the buzzing sound."
- Takahashi Eisui (y-eisui.com `shami.html`): only the 一の糸 lightly contacts the neck and
  produces the ビビリ音 (chatter), while the 二の糸 and 三の糸 resonate sympathetically.
- Yoshikawa 2010, §11.3.2: "Different from the biwa's sawari, the shamisen's nut-sawari is
  restricted to only the first (lowest) string."

The fixed form has two sub-parts. mouneru.com describes **押しさわり / 山さわり**: a shallow
cut where the first string crosses the top of the neck, then a groove roughly **1 cm** wide
(the さわり溝 *sawari-mizo*); the string touches at the cut, lifts clear over the groove, and
touches again at the far end of the groove — the sawari-yama. **[V]** Yoshikawa gives the
same figure for the biwa fret: "The actual sawari width is about 10 mm." **[V]** — so ~10 mm
is the working clearance length in this family of instruments.

### 2.2 How the second and third strings get the effect

They do not touch the sawari. They get it by **sympathetic resonance through the first
string**, and only if the instrument is correctly tuned.

> "when the vibrations of the second and third strings (usually tuned a fifth and an octave
> higher than the first string, respectively) are transmitted to the first string, the
> sawari effect is appreciable as the result of sympathetic resonance **if the tuning is
> correctly done**… Receiving the sawari on the second and third strings serves as a
> reference to judge whether the tuning is correct or not."
> — Yoshikawa 2010, §11.3.2, citing Ando (1996). **[V]**

This is important and under-reported in English: **the sawari is a tuning instrument**. If
you tune by ear and the second and third strings "light up" the first string's buzz, you are
in tune. Yoshikawa adds that the neck wood's excellent vibration transmission (see §4) "serves
very well to receive the sawari on the second and third strings." **[V]**

### 2.3 Relation to the biwa and to the Indian *jawari*

Yoshikawa treats all three as one family of deliberate buzz mechanisms, and classifies them
by where the contact happens and whether it is a surface or a point:

| Instrument | Where | Type | Which strings |
|---|---|---|---|
| Satsuma biwa | nut (*tori-kuchi* / *shōgen*, ivory, slightly curved surface) **and** all four frets | surface | all |
| Shamisen | nut region of the neck | **point** | first only |
| Sitar / tambura | the **bridge** (*jawari* in Hindi) | surface | all |

**[V]** Yoshikawa 2010, Fig. 11.4 and accompanying text: "Shamisen's sawari is not a surface
type but a point type… Although sawari mechanisms appear on the nut or frets in the Japanese
biwa and shamisen, those (jawari in Hindi) appear on the bridge in the Indian sitar and
tambura (Fletcher and Rossing 1998). This interesting difference should be studied from the
standpoints of both musical acoustics and musicology (Odaka and Yoshikawa 2006)."

On the biwa, fret-sawari has regional styles: shave the **bridge side** of the fret (Kyushu
type, better for flexible strings) or the **nut side** (Tokyo type, better for stiff strings,
and said to have been preferred by the great player Kinshi Tsuruta). **[V]** (Yoshikawa,
citing Schmitz 1994 and Tohnai 1998.)

**On whether the shamisen's sawari was *borrowed* from the sitar/biwa: I could not verify
any causal claim.** Kameya's column No. 54 only speculates — "the author wonders whether the
Indian sitar influenced this." Yoshikawa explicitly calls the relationship an open research
question. The resemblance is a fact; the transmission story is **[U]**.

**And there is almost no scholarly literature to appeal to.** The bibliographic pass recorded
in `sources.md` found **exactly one** Anglophone scholarly article on the subject, and it is
sixty years old: **Lorraine Sakata, "The Comparative Analysis of Sawari on the Shamisen,"
*Ethnomusicology* 10(2) (May 1966): 141–, DOI 10.2307/924695.** **[V]** (bibliographic
record only — **[U]** as to contents; it was not read.) The practical consequence for
The honest position: **treat both the biwa-derivation and the Indian-*jawari*-derivation as
hypotheses from the popular literature, not as competing scholarly findings.** Yoshikawa
2010 remains the only quantitative treatment, and he poses the question rather than
answering it.

### 2.4 Fixed sawari vs the adjustable 東さわり / 吾妻さわり *azuma-sawari*

**Fixed (carved) sawari.** The ridge is cut into the neck wood by the maker. Adjustment is
indirect and crude: you press the first string's peg hard with the thumb so the string
"bites" into the groove at the right depth, and if the buzz sustains too long you lift the
string out and reset it. **[V]** (mouneru.com.) Kameya's emergency fix during a recital is
**cellophane tape** over the worn contact area, which widens the contact patch and makes the
sawari more forgiving. **[V]** (e-kameya No. 255.)

**Azuma-sawari.** A small block (or metal plate, or round bar) is let into the neck under the
first string and raised or lowered by a **screw accessible from the back of the neck**. The
kamigoma is then extended so that **all three strings** ride on it, and the azuma-sawari
device sits under the first string alone.

**[V]** on the mechanism: Bachido ("the kamigoma is under all the strings and the device is
installed under the ichi no ito"); mouneru.com ("a screw on the back of the neck raises and
lowers the sawari-yama"); e-kameya No. 255 ("can be raised or lowered with a screw to move
the inner wood up and down"). An independent Japanese player blog
(shamimaster.livedoor.biz) makes the same point from the other direction — once azuma-sawari
is fitted, all three strings sit on the kamigoma and the sawari groove beneath it stops
functioning — though **I could only read this one via a search snippet; the page itself came
back mojibake (Shift-JIS/EUC mis-decoded)**, so **[U]** on its exact wording.

**Setting procedure.** Both mouneru.com and the common shop advice agree: **retract the
sawari fully until the first string does not touch, pluck the open first string, then slowly
raise the screw until a faint buzz just appears.** Target: the ridge just touching or barely
clearing, with a long clear ring; a choked, short note means you have gone too far. **[V]**

**Variants.** metal-shamisen distinguishes an embedded **metal plate** type (his own
purchased unit) from a **metal round bar** type, which he saw on Shirakawa Gunhachirō's
(白川軍八郎) instrument at the Goshogawara Tsugaru-shamisen Museum. **[V]**
metal-shamisen `lesson01` separately says Tsugaru instruments 「必ず」have a **square**
azuma-sawari and that round ones are usually retrofitted. **[V]** The square/round
terminology and the plate/bar terminology may be the same distinction seen from two angles —
**[U]**.

### 2.5 Who invented it, and when — the sources disagree

| Source | Claim |
|---|---|
| **metal-shamisen.com/azuma-sawari**, citing **吉川英史『三味線の美学と芸大邦楽科誕生秘話』p. 103** | Research into sawari devices was active around **Meiji 25–26 (1892–93)**; azuma-sawari was **patented in the Meiji era**; sales advertisements ran in 『音楽雑誌』 in **September and October of Meiji 26 (1893)**. Competing names existed: 『大阪さわり』, 『都さわり』. The author initially assumed the spelling 「東さわり」 but says the book indicates 「吾妻さわり」 is correct. **No inventor is named and no patent number is given.** |
| **Bachido Shamipedia** | "In the 1890s, a mechanical device was made to adjust the amount of buzzing on high quality jiuta and tsugaru shamisen." |
| **Kameya column No. 54 (2014)** | azuma-sawari 「大正時代」(Taishō era, 1912–26) and spread widely only **after WWII**. |

**Resolution I would offer: invented and patented in the 1890s (Meiji 20s), commercialised
slowly, and only general after WWII.** The two independent 1890s attestations (a cited book
and Bachido) outweigh Kameya's Taishō date for the *invention*; Kameya is probably right
about *diffusion*. This reconciliation is **[U]** — my inference, not a sourced statement.

**The inventor's name is unverified.** One Yahoo-search snippet of the metal-shamisen page
mentions 「明治38年頃、長唄の大先生が『さわり』の為にこんな工夫を」(around 1905, a great
nagauta teacher devised something for the sawari) but the page body does not tie that person
to azuma-sawari, and no name is given. **I could not find who invented the azuma-sawari.**

**On the name:** 東 and 吾妻 are both read *azuma*. 吾妻 is a proper name/place name;
東 means "east." The retailer world writes 東さわり far more often. Which is the historically
correct orthography is **[U]**, with metal-shamisen's citation of Yoshikawa Eishi favouring
吾妻.

### 2.6 Why the sawari exists acoustically, and why Tsugaru players care

**It is an energy-transfer device that moves power from the fundamental into high and
inharmonic partials, and lengthens the decay.**

Yoshikawa's simulation of the collision process (for the biwa fret-sawari, same physics)
is explicit: a plucked string's *n*th harmonic amplitude falls as 1/n², so "some mechanism is
needed to create an acoustical emphasis in higher frequencies. The sawari is such a
mechanism creating a reverberating high-frequency emphasis." Repeated string/ridge
collisions generate new corners (kinks) on the string every period; total energy is
conserved, but amplitude falls — "the sawari mechanism **transfers the energy of the
fundamental to that of the higher frequencies** through the string-surface collision
process." **[V]**

For the shamisen specifically, Ando (1996), as reported by Yoshikawa: comparing decay rates
with and without sawari on the open first string of a **gidayū** shamisen, **harmonics above
the 8th are strongly emphasised** (except the 13th). Ando "stressed the importance of the
generation of inharmonic components (above 2 kHz) in the shamisen's aftersound," attributing
them to irregular fluctuation in the sawari contact caused by elliptical string motion plus
feedback from the membrane through the bridge, and called these noise-like components
(which "themselves sound like cymbal tones") **indispensable** to synthesising a convincing
shamisen tone. **[V]**

**Why Tsugaru players in particular care.** The sourced reason is *tuning mobility*, not
taste. Kameya No. 54: because the sawari's correct position shifts with the key, azuma-sawari
"was devised… It is used mostly for **kouta, min'yō and Tsugaru**-style playing, where the
key changes with the singer." Nagauta and jiuta rarely use it and jiuta almost never — those
genres do not transpose much, and many players there think the traditional carved sawari
sounds better. **[V]** Bachido says the same thing from the buying side: "Switching string
gauges or tunings require adjustment to the azuma sawari for maintaining the cleanest buzz,
which can't be done on a low-end shamisen." **[V]** The aesthetic claim that the buzz is
"half the appeal of the Tsugaru sound" is a blogger's joke (mouneru.com) — **[U]** as a
factual statement, though it does tell you how central players feel it is.

### 2.7 Wear: the sawari is a consumable

- On fixed sawari, the string abrades the contact line (the さわり線) and cuts a groove. Once
  the wear is deep, **the sawari catches at only one pitch** — e.g. only at 5本 (C♯) or only
  at 6本 (D) — instead of across a range. A shop finishes the sawari to catch over a wide
  range of keys. **[V]** (e-kameya No. 255.)
- Azuma-sawari wears the same way: its internal wooden block abrades, and raising it with
  the screw does **not** fix the problem; a shop has to re-shave the block. **[V]** (same.)
- wanomanabiya.com (via search snippet) says the neck groove itself wears away in playing
  and needs filling/re-cutting. **[U]** — snippet only.

## 3. Neck sizes and the three classes

### 3.1 The classes and their widths

Class is determined by **face width (面幅) measured just below the 乳袋 *chibukuro***, i.e.
at the very top of the neck. **[V]** (y-eisui 30 Apr 2024; mukouyama.jp; metal-shamisen.)

| Class | 分厘 | mm | Source |
|---|---|---|---|
| **細棹 hosozao** | 8分4厘 (or less) | **25.4 mm** | mukouyama.jp **[V]**; y-eisui 「8分4厘前後以下」**[V]** |
| **中棹 chūzao** | 8分8厘 | **26.6 mm** | mukouyama.jp **[V]**; y-eisui 「8分8厘前後」**[V]** |
| **太棹 futozao** | 9分2厘 (and above) | **27.8 mm** | mukouyama.jp **[V]**; y-eisui 「9分2厘以上」**[V]** |

Two independent shops give the same three numbers. That is the best-attested figure set in
this file.

**But a real Tsugaru neck is wider than 27.8 mm.** Two Tsugaru-specialist sources:

- metal-shamisen `lesson01`: Tsugaru futozao face width is basically **一寸 ≈ 3.03 cm or
  more**, and **重ね (thickness) ≈ 3.4 cm standard**, with measured examples of **3.15, 3.40
  and 3.61 cm**. A small-build chūzao is quoted at **九分五厘 ≈ 2.88 cm**, thickness ~2.6 cm.
  **[V]**
- Shishidō's 棹と胴 wiki page: 「根元の部分の長さが30mmが標準で、28mmが女性用」— **30 mm is
  standard, 28 mm is the women's size**. **[V]**

**Reconciliation:** 9分2厘 (27.8 mm) is the *threshold* above which a neck counts as futozao;
30–31 mm is where Tsugaru instruments actually sit. 28 mm (a women's Tsugaru) lands just
above the threshold. The two source families are consistent once you read 27.8 mm as a floor,
not a spec. **[U]** — my reconciliation.

A caveat the Japanese sources volunteer: shamisen.ne.jp warns that 「細棹・中棹・太棹の定義
は地域やジャンルによって多少異なる」— the definitions vary somewhat by region and genre.
**[V]** (search snippet; the live page was unreachable.) note.com/shimapan_tv gives
futozao as 「約29mm以上」— a third threshold. **[V]** (snippet.) So: ~25.5 / ~26.6 / ~28–30 mm
is the honest answer, with a 1–2 mm grey zone.

### 3.2 Short necks

**短棹 *tanzao*** necks are **1寸, 1寸半 or 2寸 (3.0 / 4.5 / 6.1 cm) shorter** than 正寸.
**[V]** (y-eisui.) Fujimoto-ryū min'yō uses a short neck about **6 cm** shorter. **[V]**
(y-eisui `shami.html`.) Child sizes exist. **[V]**

### 3.3 Genre mapping

Sources are broadly consistent, with real variation at the edges:

| Class | Genres |
|---|---|
| **細棹** | 長唄 nagauta, 小唄 kouta, 端唄 hauta; Yamada-school koto accompaniment |
| **中棹** | 地唄 jiuta, 民謡 min'yō, 新内 shinnai, 清元 kiyomoto, 常磐津 tokiwazu, 小唄/端唄 (overlap) |
| **太棹** | 義太夫 gidayū / 文楽 bunraku, 浪花節 naniwabushi, 河内音頭 kawachi-ondo, **津軽三味線** |

**[V]** composite of mukouyama.jp, y-eisui, wa-gokoro.jp, en.wikipedia Shamisen,
ja.wikipedia 三味線. Note the disagreements: mukouyama lists kouta under hosozao *and*
chūzao; wa-gokoro puts kiyomoto and shinnai under chūzao while y-eisui puts hauta/kouta
there. The "one genre, one neck" mapping is a simplification — mukouyama explicitly says
some players use a chūzao for Tsugaru or a futozao for nagauta, and they once received a
nagauta shamisen with a thin neck from a jiuta player, concluding that outside formal
performance "the neck width and name don't need to be a concern." **[V]**

### 3.4 A possible fourth class: the 柳川三味線 *yanagawa shamisen* (京三味線)

The hosozao/chūzao/futozao scheme may not be exhaustive. **Grove Music Online carries a
separate entry, "Yanagawa shamisen," by Henry Johnson (2015,
DOI 10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.l2281939)** — the Kyoto *yanagawa* / *kyō-shamisen*
used in the Yanagawa school of jiuta, generally described as even slimmer and more archaic
than a standard hosozao. **[V]** that the Grove entry exists and is Johnson's (bibliographic
record from the bibliographic pass in `sources.md`); **[U]** on every physical
detail — **I did not read the entry and found no measurements for a yanagawa neck
anywhere.** Worth flagging as a fourth variant rather than asserting
specifications for it.

Relatedly: **Grove's main shamisen treatment is co-authored** — William P. Malm **and**
David W. Hughes, "Japan, §VII: Instruments and instrumental genres: 'Shamisen'," Grove Music
Online, OUP, 2001, DOI 10.1093/omo/9781561592630.013.90000382380. **[V]** (record only;
Grove was paywalled and unreachable.)

### 3.5 Tsugaru futozao is NOT gidayū futozao

Both are futozao; they are different instruments. Sourced differences:

| | **義太夫 gidayū** | **津軽 Tsugaru** |
|---|---|---|
| Neck shape at the body | has a **鳩胸 *hatomune*** — a smooth swelling curve near the body | **no hatomune** |
| Metal fitting | has the **リンドウ金具 *rindō*** fitting | also has it (shared) |
| Skin (face) | traditionally **cat** skin on the face | **thickest dog** skin, pulled very tight |
| Bachi | the **heaviest and thickest** of all | **小ぶり** — the smallest, almost always tortoiseshell-tipped |
| Bachikawa shape | decorated; half-moon-ish family | **rectangular**, plus a large patch over the body's side |
| azuma-sawari | rare in the traditional genres | essentially universal, square type |
| Body size | metal-shamisen gives no gidayū figures; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 says Tsugaru body dimensions are 「ほぼ同じ」(nearly the same) as gidayū | 五分大 (≈212 × 195 × 100 mm) |

Sources: the hatomune and rindō points and the rectangular bachikawa are **[V]**
(metal-shamisen `lesson01`). Skin assignments are **[V]** (y-eisui `shami.html`: cat belly
skin is used for high-grade hosozao faces, min'yō/jiuta chūzao and **gidayū futozao faces**,
while Tsugaru uses the thickest dog skin; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線: 「犬の皮で、背の部分を
用いる」). Bachi weights **[V]** (en.wikipedia Shamisen: gidayū "the heaviest and thickest",
Tsugaru "the smallest"; shamisen.ne.jp). The "nearly the same body size" claim **[V]**
(ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線) but it sits awkwardly with metal-shamisen's treatment of 五分大 as
the Tsugaru norm — I could not find a gidayū body figure to check it against, so the
body-size comparison is effectively **[U]**.

y-eisui's one-line summary is the best statement of the difference I found: gidayū and
Tsugaru 「音がかなり違う」— sound very different — **because of skin tension, bridge and
bachi**, not because of the neck. **[V]**

## 4. Woods and body construction

### 4.1 The three woods and the value hierarchy

**Cheapest → dearest: 花梨 karin < 紫檀 shitan < 紅木 kōki.** **[V]** (Shishidō wiki 棹と胴
ranks them by price in that order; Bachido: karin "low-end", shitan "the next level in
quality", kōki "the highest quality… the most expensive"; mouneru.com; shamisen-mizuki.com.)

#### 紅木 kōki — *Pterocarpus santalinus* (red sanders / red sandalwood)

**[V]** ja.wikipedia コウキ: 「コウキ (紅木、Pterocarpus santalinus) は、マメ亜科の小高木」;
Kotobank's dictionary entry ties 紅木（こうき）directly to shamisen necks and gives
*Pterocarpus santalinus* L.f.; lacappo.jp: 「紅木は、インド産の学名プテロカルブス・サンタリヌス
と呼ばれるマメ科の植物」. Indian in origin.

**Range and conservation status.** *P. santalinus* is **endemic to the southern Eastern
Ghats of south India** — a single-region endemic in a country that restricts its export,
which is why kōki scarcity is structural rather than market noise. **[V]** (en.wikipedia
*Pterocarpus santalinus*, via the bibliographic pass in `sources.md`.) Its **IUCN status is
contradictory in the record and I am reporting the conflict rather than picking**: the
article's infobox gives **Endangered** citing a 2021-11-13 assessment, while its conservation
section says it was "later reclassified to **Near Threatened** in 2018." **[V]** that the
conflict exists; **[U]** as to which is current.

Properties and claims: very hard; said to give the best tone and to resist **勘べり
*kanberi*** (wear/dishing of the fingerboard under the fingers). **[V]** (mouneru.com;
Shishidō.) Shishidō distinguishes **Indian kōki** (pricier, higher quality) from **African
kōki**. **[V]** Good new kōki is now hard to get. **[V]** (mouneru.com;
shamisen-mizuki.com.) Figured grain (**トチ *tochi***, stripe/curl) is prized, but Shishidō
warns that wood with tochi is usually *softer*, so hard wood that also has tochi is very
expensive. **[V]** — a useful, non-obvious grading point. Note that 紅木 is sometimes filed
under the broad category 紫檀 as **紅木紫檀** even though it is a different genus. **[V]**
(Kotobank.)

#### 紫檀 shitan — *Dalbergia cochinchinensis* (Siamese rosewood), loosely

**[V]** ja.wikipedia シタン lists *Dalbergia cochinchinensis* under the シタン name and says
the wood is used for 三味線の棹; solidwood.jp gives 紫檀 as *Dalbergia* (ダルベルギア属),
species *cochinchinensis*; wood-museum.net says 紫檀 is used for 琵琶や三味線の棹.
**Caveat:** the trade name 紫檀 in Japanese is loose and has covered several *Dalbergia*
species plus, confusingly, *Pterocarpus santalinus* as 紅木紫檀. **[V]** (Kotobank, rafuju.jp.)
Treating any given "shitan shamisen" as specifically *D. cochinchinensis* is **[U]** — and
this matters for CITES (§4.4). shamisen-mizuki.com adds that shitan is "often dyed now."
**[V]**

#### 花梨 karin — *Pterocarpus indicus* (amboyna), NOT the quince, NOT *Chukrasia*

**This is the item in the commonly repeated account that most needs correcting.**

> "The **amboyna wood (*Pterocarpus indicus*; karin in Japanese)** is the best for the body
> and neck of the Japanese three-stringed lute, shamisen."
> — Yoshikawa 2010, §11.1, Table 11.1. **[V]**

Corroboration: Japanese Wikipedia 三味線 places 花林/カリン as a Southeast Asian wood;
hanamokusanpo.jp says the karin used for shamisen necks was インドシタン (*P. indicus*);
fujiihouse.co.jp sells 花梨 as 「シタン属, マメ科」from Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia, Vietnam;
solidwood.jp gives *Pterocarpus macrocarpus* for 花梨 timber. **[V]**

So: **karin in the shamisen trade is a legume (Fabaceae) *Pterocarpus*, most often
*P. indicus* (amboyna/narra), sometimes *P. macrocarpus* (Burma padauk).** It is **not**
*Pseudocydonia sinensis* (= *Chaenomeles sinensis*), the rose-family Chinese quince, which
shares the Japanese name カリン/花梨 as a *fruit tree* and is a different plant entirely —
ja.wikipedia カリン (バラ科) and the Forestry and Forest Products Research Institute both
describe the quince. **[V]** The confusion is a genuine Japanese homonym: one gardening page
even notes that the quince's name derives from the grain of the 花櫚 wood used for shamisen
bodies. **[V]** (beginners.garden.)

**And it is not *Chukrasia*.** I found **no source at all** linking *Chukrasia* (chickrassy,
Meliaceae) to the shamisen. Treat that identification as unsupported. **[V]** (negative
finding.)

**Conservation and range.** *P. indicus* is **IUCN Endangered**, **extinct in Vietnam** and
**possibly extinct in Peninsular Malaysia and Sri Lanka**; its native range **includes the
Ryukyu Islands**, and its Wikipedia article explicitly lists musical instruments among its
uses. **[V]** (en.wikipedia *Pterocarpus indicus*, via the bibliographic pass in `sources.md`.)
Note the irony worth flagging: **the wood every shamisen
body is made from is Endangered, while the premium neck wood everyone worries about is only
partly restricted.** No CITES listing is mentioned in that article — **[U]**, an argument
from absence, so anyone shipping should check the current appendices directly.

Properties: softer and lighter than kōki or shitan; cheap and available; **warps and wears
(kanberi) more**, and the neck can be re-shaved only so many times. **[V]** (mouneru.com;
Bachido; Shishidō.) Yoshikawa's measured wood constants for amboyna make the choice
intelligible: **ρ = 873 kg/m³, E = 20.0 GPa, Q = 155, c = 4,770 m/s** — the densest and
stiffest wood in his whole table (compare Sitka spruce 408 kg/m³ / 10.0 GPa, mulberry
647 kg/m³ / 6.3 GPa), placing it among the *frame-board* woods: poor at radiating, excellent
at **transmitting** vibration. **[V]** Yoshikawa's one-line summary of the shamisen's neck
and body material: "hard and not so vibrant, but… excellent in vibration transmission," and
"also the best material for a long neck to realize a very smooth movement of fingers on the
strings." **[V]** This is the only acoustically grounded account of shamisen wood choice I
found.

#### Dō (body) wood

**All dō are karin**, in varying grades. **[V]** Bachido: "All dou are made from Karin wood
of varying grades… Though a shamisen with kouki wood is referred to as a 'kouki shamisen',
the dou is made from high-grade Karin." ja.wikipedia 三味線: 「胴は現在すべて花林」, with 桑
(mulberry) and ケヤキ (zelkova) used historically. ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 agrees the body is
karin. Dense, figured karin indicates a better instrument. **[V]** (wagakkimedia.com.)

Note the mild disagreement with Yoshikawa, who writes that amboyna is best "for the body
**and neck**" — true for the body and for low/mid-grade necks, but the Japanese trade clearly
treats kōki and shitan as the premium neck woods. Yoshikawa is simplifying. **[U]** on the
reconciliation.

#### Other woods

- **Historically:** necks of カシ (oak), クワ (mulberry), 桜 (cherry), 杉 (cedar). **[V]**
  (ja.wikipedia 三味線; Shishidō.)
- **Occasional/special:** snakewood, ビャクダン (sandalwood), タガヤサン (wenge/*Senna
  siamea*). **[V]** (ja.wikipedia.)
- **糸巻 pegs:** 黒檀 ebony (standard), ivory (premium), acrylic / imitation ivory
  (budget). **[V]** (y-eisui; wagakkimedia; mouneru.) wagakkimedia's verdict: ordinary ebony
  is sufficient. **[V]**
- **Modern alternatives.** Bachido: "amazing shamisen can be made with materials such as
  Maple, Purpleheart, Oak and more… As long as the shamisen is skillfully crafted, it can be
  built from a wide variety of woods," and reports playing a maple shamisen by a master maker
  that "sounded just as good as any shamisen made with 'standard' woods." **[V]** as
  Bachido's position; the comparison is anecdotal, so **[U]** as a general finding.
  Bachido's own DIY kits use **figured bubinga, pomelle sapele and red gum**; its fingerboard
  blanks use "Pua Rosa" and "Laotian Birdseye Rosewood." **[V]** (store listings.)

### 4.2 綾杉彫り *ayasugi-bori* — the carved interior

**What it physically is:** the inside faces of the dō are chisel-cut with a fine, regular
wavy/zigzag pattern (named after the herringbone "ayasugi" cedar-grain motif). A plain,
smooth-hollowed body is called **丸打胴 *marubuchi-dō*** (also 丸胴). A higher grade,
**子持ち綾杉 *komochi-ayasugi*** ("ayasugi with a child"), alternates thick and thin grooves,
or adds a second cut line on top of a single ayasugi.

**[V]**: mouneru.com ("Standard bodies (丸打胴) are plainly hollowed inside. Ayasugi bodies
have wavy, geometric carvings on the four inner walls… 子持ち綾杉 is a higher tier with
alternating thick and thin grooves"); ja.wikipedia 三味線 (「綾杉」is a fine chisel-cut
pattern on the inner face of the body, 「音の響きを良くするとされる」); ja.wikipedia
津軽三味線; wagakki-ichiba (子持ち綾杉 described as improving the sound further);
tanakashoten.com ("綾杉胴というのは音の響きがいいように内側をギザギザに彫ってある胴");
kabuki-bito.jp (「丹精に手で掘られた『綾杉彫り』」, noting the carving **is hidden once the
skin is stretched over the body**).

**Is the resonance claim substantiated? No. Say so plainly.**

- **I found no measurement, no published experiment, no modal analysis, and no acoustic
  paper on ayasugi-bori.** Yoshikawa's chapter — the one source here that does real
  measurement on shamisen — models the dō's skin as the dominant radiator and **never
  mentions the interior carving at all**. **[V]** (negative finding across the full chapter
  text.)
- The Japanese shops that sell it say so hedgingly. mouneru.com: claims that the carving
  affects the sound are **未検証** (unverified); he compares it to taiko-drum interior
  carving and allows that "this may just be craftsmanship." **[V]**
- Where a mechanism is offered, it is hand-waving and the sources contradict each other.
  shamisen.ne.jp: 「綾杉胴は理論、乱反射するため余韻は短くなります」— in theory the diffuse
  reflection makes the *reverberation shorter* — i.e. the opposite of "better resonance."
  **[V]** (snippet; live page unreachable.) wagakki-ichiba and tanakashoten assert
  improvement with no mechanism. **[V]**
- The physics is unpromising on its face: the grooves are millimetre-scale relief inside a
  ~200 × 180 × 100 mm cavity, i.e. tiny relative to the wavelengths that matter
  (the body's dominant behaviour is in the 0.7–1.5 kHz region, λ ≈ 23–49 cm in air).
  **[U]** — this is my reasoning, not a source's.
- The honest reading: **ayasugi-bori is a quality signal and a labour-intensive craft mark
  — real information about how much hand-work went into the instrument, and therefore a
  reasonable proxy for the grade of everything else — with an unsubstantiated acoustic story
  attached.** mouneru.com effectively says exactly this: "When buying used, an ayasugi body
  suggests the neck may also be good, but… at the buyer's own risk," while "beginners don't
  need body refinements." **[V]**

### 4.3 金細 *kinboso* / *kinhoso* — and a correction

**金細 is NOT the interior carving.** It is **metal (gold-coloured, formerly silver) fitted
into the grooves and tenon of the three-piece neck joints**.

**[V]**: mouneru.com ("The neck is split into three sections, and the grooves at the joints
are inlaid with gold… this is 音には関係ない [said to be unrelated to sound]. Its benefits
are easier disassembly and protection of the joints. Higher-grade gold raises the price.");
an Instagram luthier post ("ほぞの両脇に2本の溝が入っている。これを2本溝とか2枚溝と言います")
showing the **ほぞ金** metal tenon insert; ameblo.jp/edo-hauta ("これを金細三味線なんて呼ぶ",
and that silver tenons were used in the past); wagakki-ichiba listing 「二枚溝金細」alongside
平ほぞ and 一本溝 as joint grades; wagakkiichiba-sinkan.com grading instruments as
「花林材、並紅木材、金細」— three tiers, with 金細 at the top. Bachido's store spells it
"Kinhoso." **[V]**

So the usual grading ladder is: **karin → plain kōki (並紅木) → kōki 金細**, and a 金細 /
綾杉胴 instrument is near the top. **[V]** shamisen-mizuki.com's claim that the joint metal
「improves sound and reduces warping from humidity」 is **[U]** — it contradicts mouneru's
「音には関係ない」, and a Yahoo! Chiebukuro answer (weak source) says the gold colour is
preferred because **it doesn't rust**, and is unrelated to sound. **[V]** on the existence of
the disagreement.

*Note on a garbled form in circulation: "金細 kinbosomachine" appears to be a transcription artefact.
There is no such term; the word is 金細, read kinboso / kinhoso.*

### 4.4 Skin attachment, and what holds a shamisen together

- The skin is glued with a **starch / glutinous-rice paste** (糊 / 寒梅粉 *kanbaiko*), i.e.
  a **water-soluble** adhesive. **[V]** (mukouyama.jp: "The adhesive is made from glutinous
  rice… The adhesive is water-soluble, so humidity can loosen it and cause the skin to peel
  off"; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線: the skin is attached with starch paste so it is extremely
  humidity-sensitive; y-eisui lists 膠 (hide glue) and 寒梅粉 as the adhesives.)
- This single fact drives most of §5 and §9: **the glue is the weak point, not the
  membrane.**
- The **dōkake** exists to keep forearm sweat off that glue. **[V]** (Bachido: "the cover
  prevents sweat from your arm from weakening the water soluble glue.")

## 5. Skins

Be matter-of-fact: the traditional materials are dog and cat.

**Scholarly literature on this exact question exists and should be cited in preference to
dealers' blogs.** Two peer-reviewed treatments by **Keisuke Yamada**, supplied by a parallel
bibliographic pass recorded in `sources.md` (records verified via Crossref; **I have not read
either text**, so everything in §5 below remains sourced to the shops and players named):

- Keisuke Yamada, "Shamisen skin on the verge of extinction: musical sustainability and
  non-scalability of cultural loss," *Ethnomusicology Forum* 26(3) (2017): 373–396,
  DOI 10.1080/17411912.2018.1423575. **[V]** (record); **[U]** (contents).
- Keisuke Yamada, "The Political Economy of Nonlife: Biopower, Ontosecurity, and the
  Shamisen Skin Trade in Japan," in *The Oxford Handbook of Economic Ethnomusicology*, OUP,
  2020, DOI 10.1093/oxfordhb/9780190859633.013.35. **[V]** (record); **[U]** (contents).

**Correction to the usual framing:** cat is *not* confined to "finest hosozao / geisha
instruments." y-eisui assigns cat to high-grade hosozao **and** min'yō/jiuta chūzao **and**
the face of gidayū futozao. The real axis is **thin skin for refined, bright, articulate
tone; thick skin for power and for being hit**, not genre prestige. **[V]**

### 5.2 The acoustic reasons

- **Thickness.** Dog is roughly **3× thicker** than cat (iwano.biz), or **3–4×** by y-eisui's
  account, and thickness varies about **2×** within a single hide. **[V]** Yoshikawa's modal
  work shows why thickness and tension dominate: the membrane, not the wood, is the primary
  radiator, so its surface density and tension set the instrument's strongest resonance (§5.6).
- **Tone.** Cat: thin, bright, "crisp… bell-like," tight; more delicate. Dog: harder,
  stronger, deep mid-lows, loud, with a clear attack outline; it goes "bumpy" as it slackens.
  **[V]** (y-eisui; mukouyama; ameblo/duckchan109; clubnagy-music.com; fuku-chan.info.)
- **Pore structure.** Dog skin has **large, coarse pores** which "grip" the bachi; kangaroo
  is smooth with small pores; cat is smooth and light. **[V]** (fuku-chan.info;
  ameblo/duckchan109.)
- **Grading, mercilessly concrete.** The best Tsugaru dog skin by reputation is **female
  Akita-dog skin**, though most skins are now imported. **[V]** (ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.)
  Shops rate skins on a **1–10** scale, best = strongest and most resonant. **[V]**
  (wagakki-ichiba.)
- **Why cowhide is not used:** ja.wikipedia 三味線 notes the skin must be thick *and* match
  the body's size, and cowhide is simply too large (i.e. the usable thickness/area
  combination is wrong). **[V]**

### 5.3 Nipple marks — the authenticity mark, and the forgery

- **Cat belly skin shows the nipple marks; dog back skin does not.** **[V]**
  (wagakki-ichiba.com/sozai.htm, explicitly; multiple shop pages agree.)
- **One cat yields exactly one instrument's worth of skin** (belly only), which is why it is
  rare and expensive. **[V]** (y-eisui; mukouyama; ameblo/duckchan109.)
- **四つ皮 *yotsugawa*** ("four skins") is the standard trade name for cat skin, because
  **four** of the eight belly nipples land on the drumhead. netlaputa.ne.jp: the belly shows
  eight marks, the hide is split in two, and four marks appear on each face. **[V]**
- **八つ乳 *yatsuchi*** ("eight nipples") is the top grade: all eight marks across both
  faces, proving **one cat supplied both the front and back of that instrument**. **[V]**
  (ameblo/duckchan109.)
- **And the fake:** "Dog skins are sometimes marked with moxibustion to imitate this
  feature." **[V]** (ameblo/duckchan109.) A Yahoo! Chiebukuro thread asks exactly this — are
  my four dots real cat, or decorative imitation — which tells you the forgery is common
  enough to worry buyers. **[V]** So: **the marks are evidence, not proof.**

### 5.4 The modern shift: supply, ethics, law, synthetics

**Why the shift.** ja.wikipedia 三味線 states the cause plainly: cost and **animal-welfare
law** mean cat and dog skins have been largely replaced by sheep skin or synthetic leather,
though 津軽三味線 still generally uses dog. **[V]** en.wikipedia Shamisen says animal skins
declined "from around the mid-2000s." **[V]** mukouyama dates synthetic skin's introduction
to **the 1980s**, created as an unbreakable alternative in response to player requests and
animal-welfare concerns. **[V]** ameblo/duckchan109 expects kangaroo to grow "because of
animal welfare concerns." **[V]** I did **not** find the text of any specific Japanese
statute or import ban — the "animal welfare law" attribution is **[U]** as to which law.

the rain story above proves it. **[V]** composite.

### 5.5 Stretching, tension, lifespan, cost

**How it is stretched.** The skin is soaked, pulled over the frame with clamps/cords and
glued at the edges with rice/starch paste, with **1–2 cm of surplus** at the rim on a
Tsugaru. **[V]** (metal-shamisen; mukouyama; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.) I did **not** find a
step-by-step luthier account of the jig and process; the detail above is as far as my
sources go. **[U]** for the mechanics.

**Tension.** The qualitative rule from mukouyama: the skin "must be stretched **almost to
the point of tearing**, which produces the best sound. Even slight slackness degrades the
tone." **[V]** Genre differences: Tsugaru uses the thickest skin **pulled tightest**; gidayū
and Tsugaru "sound very different because of **skin tension**, bridge and bachi." **[V]**
(y-eisui.) Shops sell tension as an option: Bachido Warehouse's **張り上げ強** ("strong
stretch") finish costs **+¥10,000** and "tightens the skin more than the standard method."
**[V]** SEION similarly prices a firmer option higher. **[V]**

**The only numeric tension figure I found** is from Yoshikawa's modelling, not a workshop:
**membrane tension 2,000 N/m**, surface density 0.040 kg/m², membrane wave speed 220 m/s,
on a 0.16 × 0.16 m square. **[V]** (Yoshikawa 2010, Table 11.2, from Takasawa 1993.) Note
0.16 m is smaller than a real nagauta dō face (~0.197 × 0.179 m) — it is a model, not a
measurement of a specific instrument. **[U]** as a real-world tension spec. **I could not
find measured tension values per genre anywhere.**

### 5.6 Why the skin matters so much — the acoustics

The shamisen is best modelled as a **string–bridge–membrane system**, not as "a string
driving a passive box."

> "An averaged spectral envelope obtained from about ten spectra of the shamisen tones
> indicates a triangular shape with a top located at about **700 Hz**… The fundamental
> frequencies of string vibration extend from about **140 to 400 Hz**. Because a roll-off as
> much as **25 dB/oct** is shown in the lower-frequency side of the envelope, the amplitude
> of the fundamental is **remarkably small**… The above peak frequency (700 Hz) probably
> corresponds to the strongest resonance of the overall system, which should be mainly
> determined by **the resonance of the cat-skin membrane**… Viewing an instrument as a
> united system like this is very different from the Western traditional view that the
> instrument body is passively driven by the string vibration."
> — Yoshikawa 2010, §11.3.1 (citing Ando 1996, Ando & Yamaguchi 1983). **[V]**

Takasawa's (1993) finite-difference model of the whole system, Table 11.2 **[V]**:

| | Value |
|---|---|
| String length | 0.80 m |
| String linear density | 0.002 kg/m |
| String tension | **80 N** |
| String wave velocity | 200 m/s |
| String f₀ | 130 Hz |
| Membrane | 0.16 × 0.16 m |
| Membrane surface density | 0.040 kg/m² |
| Membrane tension | **2,000 N/m** |
| Membrane wave velocity | 220 m/s |
| Membrane (1,1) mode | **970 Hz** |
| Membrane (1,2) mode | **1,500 Hz** |
| Bridge mass | **0.002 kg (2 g)** |
| Bridge footprint | 75 × 6.0 mm |

Results: the **whole-system** resonances land near **840 Hz** and **1,370 Hz**, falling
between the 6th/7th and the 10th/11th harmonics of the string, so **harmonics 6–11 are
strongly emphasised** and others weakened by their distance from those resonances. **[V]**

Two consequences that explain real player behaviour:

1. **Bridge mass is critical.** "the decrease in the rate of these frequencies is about
   **10% with respect to an increase of 1 g in the bridge mass**." **[V]** On a 2 g bridge,
   1 g is a 50% mass change — which is exactly why players own a drawerful of koma and swap
   them to change tone. metal-shamisen: 「音の響きが悪い時『駒』を変えただけで良くなることも
   多いです」. **[V]**
2. **Bridge position retunes the body.** Moving the koma toward the membrane edge (longer
   string) raises the first system resonance and lowers the second, narrowing the gap between
   them. Yoshikawa: "it seems to be reasonable that some professional shamisen players
   **adjust the bridge position judging from the weather (particularly, the humidity)** of
   the performance day." **[V]** This is the acoustic explanation of y-eisui's practical note
   that correct koma position shifts by millimetres to ~1 cm with humidity. **[V]** Two
   independent sources, one scholarly and one a working player, converging.

Bachido's shop-floor version of all this — "The skin is the most important part as it
influences about **80%** of the sound quality" — is directionally supported by Yoshikawa but
the 80% figure is **[U]**, Kyle Abbott's estimate, not a measurement.

### 6.4 Why the Tsugaru bachi is small and light

y-eisui states the fact — 「津軽用の撥は、長唄や民謡、地唄の撥よりも小ぶりです」— but
**explicitly does not explain why**; his page "does not explain why the sizes differ beyond
the fact that each genre uses its own bachi." **[V]** (including the negative finding.)

What *is* sourced is the consequence chain, and it supports the usual reasoning:

- **Tsugaru technique strikes the skin as well as the strings.** Yoshikawa 2010, §11.3:
  "The cat skin is usually impacted immediately after the string plucking." **[V]** (He says
  this of the shamisen generally; the Satsuma biwa plectrum does the same — "The plectrum
  often hits the top plate immediately after plucking the string." **[V]**)
- **So the skin and the bachi both take real punishment.** Hence: a glued-on **撥皮
  *bachikawa*** leather patch where the bachi lands, **rectangular** on Tsugaru (vs the
  half-moon elsewhere) and extending over the body's side as well. **[V]** (metal-shamisen;
  e-kameya No. 226.) Neglecting to replace a worn bachikawa scratches the skin. **[V]**
  (YouTube maintenance video title/snippet — weak source, **[U]**.)
- **The tip is a consumable.** y-eisui: it wears and chips; new tips are sharp, chip easily
  **and wear the strings faster**, so he rounds and chamfers a new tip and starts by playing
  softly; wear is uneven and tapering; "when the first string is struck hard, the back side
  can chip away"; reshaping is done with **400–3000 grit** wet-and-dry paper and polished
  with Acrylic Sunday compound. His own bachi started at **~103 mm** opening and was trimmed
  to **~85 mm** as the tip wore; he cuts it back to **~95 mm** when he can. **[V]**
- **Material by neck class — and a hard constraint.** shamisen.ne.jp: hosozao uses plastic,
  wood, sometimes bekkō, and ivory; chūzao uses anything **except** wood; **futozao normally
  uses neither wood nor ivory, "because the bachi may break"** — plastic futozao bachi must
  have a hard, strong tip. **[V]** This is the real physical reason the Tsugaru bachi is what
  it is: the forces involved break wood and ivory, and only bekkō (or a good resin) has the
  flex-plus-toughness to survive. y-eisui on bekkō: it needs "enough thickness not to lose to
  the first string, **and** it needs flex." **[V]**

So the sourced answer to "why smaller and lighter than gidayū's": not weight-saving for its
own sake but **controllability at speed** — shamisen.ne.jp: "a smaller tip gives more speed,
a larger one slows you down" **[V]** — combined with the tip-flex requirement, which caps how
much material you can put out at the end of the lever. The explicit causal claim remains
**[U]**; no source states it.

### 6.5 Materials, tone, cost

- **鼈甲 bekkō (hawksbill tortoiseshell).** Used for jiuta, min'yō, 俗謡 and Tsugaru.
  Withstands strong flex, very durable, gives a tone with a lot of 雑味 (noise/grit) that is
  distinctive. Hardness varies by shell type so you must try before buying. **Good bekkō gets
  harder to obtain every year.** **[V]** (shamisen.ne.jp.) Construction: **single-plate**
  (一枚, cut from one piece, dearer, thinnable) vs **laminated** (two glued pieces, stiffer,
  risky to thin). **[V]** (y-eisui.) **Cost: y-eisui's custom single-plate bekkō bachi from
  Kaihodō (Tokyo) was ~¥45,000 and has risen to ~¥60,000.** **[V]**
- **象牙 zōge (ivory).** Hosozao and chūzao genres. Hard but flexible, thin tip, "a
  distinctive, delicate noise," more refined tone. Good ivory also getting scarcer every
  year. **[V]** (shamisen.ne.jp.)
- **Water-buffalo horn.** Used as the **handle**, with a bekkō tip — so tonally close to a
  full bekkō bachi. Kōki-wood handles exist too. **[V]** (shamisen.ne.jp.)
- **Wood (柘植 boxwood, 樫 oak).** Hosozao genres; gentle tone; cheap; chosen by some
  non-nagauta players because it is small and fits the hand. **[V]**
- **Plastic / acrylic / resin.** Cheapest, most common for beginners; "playability, tone and
  durability are all somewhat lacking." **[V]** (shamisen.ne.jp.) y-eisui: the ~¥4,000 hard
  plastic ones have almost no tip flex, which is the main frustration; pricier plastic has
  some. **[V]** Bachido: "Plastic bachi are the most affordable type, but its rigidity often
  makes the playing experience frustrating (especially as a newcomer)," and recommends a
  wooden bachi as the cheap compromise, or a premium one if affordable — "it glides across
  the strings like butter." **[V]** Bachido's current bachi line includes plastic, premium
  wooden (Ezon), "Signature Bachi (Neoko)", Takayama synthetic, **Faux-Bekko**, and
  wood/acrylic composites (cocobolo, jarrah, lacewood, burl). **[V]**
- **Neo-kō / ネオ甲 ("Neoko").** A resin imitating bekkō, with flex. y-eisui hasn't used it
  extensively but "thinks it is worth considering." **[V]** This is the practical answer for
  anyone who wants bekkō behaviour without the legal problem (§6.6).
- **General rule:** heavier = louder and more forceful; lighter = softer tone. **[V]**
  (shamisen.ne.jp; metal-shamisen.)
- **One odd and charming maintenance note:** store a tortoiseshell bachi **in the light**,
  because the insects that eat it prefer the dark. **[V]** (wa-gokoro.jp.)

### 6.6 CITES and legality for travel — ivory and tortoiseshell

This is the part that can get an instrument seized, so be precise.

- **Hawksbill turtle (*Eretmochelys imbricata*), the source of 鼈甲 bekkō, is CITES
  Appendix I.** **[U]** — I did not read a primary CITES document for this listing (cites.org
  blocked every fetch). It is a long-standing and well-known Appendix I listing, but I am
  flagging it rather than asserting it from memory.
- **Elephant ivory is CITES-listed and commercial international trade is banned**
  (*Loxodonta africana* Appendix I/II, *Elephas maximus* Appendix I). **[U]** — same
  limitation.
- **What I *can* verify is the mechanism that exists for musicians:** a **musical instrument
  certificate (MIC)**. UK Government guidance (gov.uk, *CITES imports and exports*): if a
  CITES specimen is a musical instrument and the movement is **not for commercial purposes**,
  "you can instead apply for a musical instrument certificate," and "There is no fee for a
  musical instrument certificate." **[V]** The page does **not** list which species it covers,
  does **not** give a validity period, and does **not** define non-commercial for this
  certificate; its 3-year validity figure applies to travelling-exhibition certificates, a
  different instrument. **[V]** (so the commonly-cited "3 years" for a MIC is **[U]** here.)
- **Secondary corroboration** that the MIC is the right tool for ivory/tortoiseshell
  instruments: a League of American Orchestras-linked post states that for instruments
  containing CITES-listed material "such as ivory or tortoiseshell," "you will need a Musical
  Instrument Certificate (MIC)." **[V]** as an attestation of that organisation's advice; the
  underlying CITES instrument is **Resolution Conf. 16.8**, which I **could not read** —
  **[U]**.
- **Annotation #15's musical-instrument exemption does NOT apply here.** #15 covers
  *Dalbergia* spp. (except *D. nigra*) and three *Guibourtia* spp. only — see §4.4 below. It
  has nothing to do with ivory, tortoiseshell, or Appendix I material. **[V]** (the
  annotation text itself.)
- **Domestic law bites separately.** The wood-database article's own framing applies equally
  here: CITES is implemented by each country, and national rules (US ESA/AECA and state
  ivory bans, EU rules, Japan's domestic bekkō registration regime) can be stricter. **[V]**
  I did **not** verify the specifics of any national ivory statute — **[U]**.

**Practical upshot, stated as advice rather than as a verified fact:** a bekkō or ivory bachi
is the single most legally dangerous thing in a shamisen case when crossing a border, and the
most replaceable. If you tour, consider owning a **Neo-kō / faux-bekkō** bachi for travel and
leaving the real one at home. **[U]** — my recommendation.

## 7. Strings (糸 *ito*)

### 7.1 Names and materials

- Three strings: **一の糸** (thickest, lowest, nearest the player), **二の糸**, **三の糸**
  (thinnest). **[V]** (ja.wikipedia 三味線; y-eisui; blog.ko6gatsu.net.)
  *(Note: one blog summary I read mis-described the ichi-no-ito as the thinnest. Every
  authoritative source says thickest. **[V]**)*
- **Silk (絹).** The traditional material, dyed yellow with **ウコン** (turmeric).
  **[V]** (ja.wikipedia 三味線.) blog.ko6gatsu.net (from an interview with Hashimoto
  Hidemune, president of 丸三ハシモト, the leading maker): silk gives a bright sound that
  doesn't muffle; it is the **softest** option and so **does not damage the instrument**; it
  breaks most easily and is expensive; and **only silk is made in fractional momme sizes**
  like 12.5-3 and 12.7-3. **[V]** Silk also stretches, which makes tuning harder, but sounds
  better. **[V]** (e-kameya No. 87.)
- **Nylon.** Developed after WWII as an unbreakable alternative. Light sound, cheap, rarely
  breaks — **but its strength can damage the instrument**. **[V]** (blog.ko6gatsu.net.) That
  trade-off is rarely stated in English and is worth knowing: a string that won't break
  transfers the failure somewhere else.
- **Tetron (polyester).** Similar pros and cons to nylon; more muffled. **[V]** (same.)
- **Steel:** used on the *heike* shamisen. **[V]** (en.wikipedia Shamisen.)

**Typical mixed set.** Bachido's own stock rule: "I keep a minimum of two ichi-no-ito
(**silk**), four ni-no-ito (**tetron**), and five san-no-ito (**nylon**)." **[V]**
metal-shamisen: first string usually silk; second silk, tetron or nylon; third usually nylon.
**[V]** y-eisui: the three-no-ito is often nylon or tetron because silk breaks so easily, and
"nearly all Tsugaru instruments use these." **[V]**

### 7.2 The gauge numbering convention

Format **`NN-S`**: **NN is 匁 *monme*, the weight of raw silk thread (原糸) the string is
drawn from; S is the string position (1, 2 or 3).** **[V]** (blog.ko6gatsu.net, from the
Marusan Hashimoto interview; mouneru.com: 「15が太さを表し、-2が二の糸という意味」.)

The clever part: 13-1, 13-2 and 13-3 all start from 13 monme of raw thread and yield roughly
**50, 100 and 200 strings** respectively — same weight of silk, progressively thinner
strings. **[V]** Within one position, higher monme = thicker. Makers adjust raw-thread weight
*and* twist, so **the same spec differs between manufacturers and over time**. **[V]**
Japanese makers use monme, not grams. **[V]** **No source gave diameters in mm** — **[U]**.

### 7.3 Gauge by genre

From blog.ko6gatsu.net, based on 『上達するための道しるべ 三味線読本』with corrections from
Hashimoto Hidemune (Marusan Hashimoto), reference product 寿糸 極上. **[V]**

| Genre | 一の糸 | 二の糸 | 三の糸 |
|---|---|---|---|
| 長唄 nagauta | 15-1 | 13-2 | 12-3, 13-3 |
| 清元 kiyomoto | 17–20-1 | 15–18-2 | 14–16-3 |
| 常磐津 tokiwazu | 18–21-1 | 16–18-2 | 14–16-3 |
| **義太夫 gidayū** | **30-1** | 20–30-2 | 15–18-3, 24–26-3 |
| 地唄 jiuta | 14–15-1 | 14–15-2 | 14–15-3 |
| 小唄 kouta | 17-1 | 15-2 | 13-3 |
| 端唄 hauta | 15-1 | 13-2 | 12-3 |
| 新内 shinnai | 15–18-1 | 15–17-2 | 13–15-3 |
| 民謡 min'yō | 17-1 | 15-2 | 13-3 |
| **津軽 Tsugaru** | **25–30-1** | 15-2 | 13-3, 14-3 |

shamisen.ne.jp independently gives Tsugaru as **25-1, 15-2, 13-3** with the caveat
「糸のサイズは目安です」(sizes are guidelines). **[V]** A Tsugaru school site (tsugaru-wakana)
specifies 15-2 silk by Marusan Hashimoto. **[V]** One shop lists Tsugaru first-string options
up to **28-1, 30-1 and even 85-1**. **[V]** (snippet; the 85-1 figure is startling and
**[U]** — possibly a 浄瑠璃糸 or a different numbering.) Shishidō's wiki reports the Koyama
school's house preference as a 浄瑠璃 (jōruri) first string of medium bore, a 15 silk second
and a 13 nylon third. **[V]**

### 7.4 Why Tsugaru uses heavier strings

**Sourced facts:** Tsugaru's first string at **25–30 monme** is roughly **double** nagauta's
15-1, and matches gidayū's 30-1 — while its second and third strings (15-2, 13-3) are
essentially the same as min'yō's and barely heavier than nagauta's. **[V]** So the heaviness
is almost entirely in the **first string**.

**Mechanical consequence, verified:** Bachido — "The strings made for a tsugaru shamisen
(specifically the ichi no ito, or the first string) is considerably thicker, and **won't fit
into the hole of a nagauta shamisen's itomaki** (without drilling a wider hole)." **[V]**
This is the practical reason you must buy strings for *your* instrument's class, not for the
style you want to play: it is a hole-diameter constraint, not a tonal preference. **[V]**

**Why heavier at all:** the sourced chain is (a) futozao instruments have the largest body
and tightest, thickest skin, (b) the technique strikes the skin, (c) the bachi tip must "not
lose to the first string" (y-eisui), and (d) the sawari needs a string with enough mass and
amplitude to drive the ridge. Each link is sourced; the assembled argument is **[U]**.
Thicker strings are also described as sounding fuller and tighter, thinner ones more
delicate. **[V]** (metal-shamisen.)

### 7.5 Lifespan and which one breaks

- **The third string is the thinnest and breaks most.** y-eisui: the 三の糸 is often nylon or
  tetron precisely *because* silk breaks so easily. **[V]** Bachido's stock ratio — 2 : 4 : 5
  first : second : third — is a direct empirical measure of relative breakage rate. **[V]**
- **Lifespan is use-dependent, not time-dependent.** Bachido: "All strings break eventually
  (how long they last depends on how often it's played and how much force the player uses),
  so it's always good to stock up on extras… if you prefer every string to be silk strings,
  it's safe to order more as silk strings break quicker than tetron or nylon." **[V]**
  **No source gave a figure in hours or weeks** — **[U]**. metal-shamisen simply calls
  strings 消耗品 (consumables). **[V]**
- A sharp new bachi tip wears strings faster. **[V]** (y-eisui.)

## 8. Related instruments, and the name

### 8.1 The instruments

- **三弦 / 三絃 *sanxian* (China).** The ancestor. Three strings, long fretless fingerboard,
  rounded-rectangular resonator **snakeskin-covered on both faces**, curved pegbox with
  side pegs; silk, nylon or steel strings. Northern type "about **122 cm** in length,"
  southern about **95 cm**; small size tuned A–d–a or d–a–d¹, large G–d–g. en.wikipedia notes
  the shamisen's body-and-neck is built from **four pieces of wood instead of one**. **[V]**
  (en.wikipedia Sanxian; Yoshikawa 2010: "The Japanese shamisen… has roots in the Chinese
  sanxian (Odaka 1998)." Yoshikawa adds the striking aside that **there is no plucked-lute
  family in Korea** and no long-neck lute like the shamisen or sanxian there. **[V]**)
- **三線 *sanshin* (Okinawa).** The intermediate step and the shamisen's closest relative.
  **Smaller, rounder body covered in python skin** — traditionally Burmese python, now also
  reticulated python under CITES. The reason it is snakeskin rather than cat or dog is simply
  what was available through Southeast Asian trade: the local **habu viper is "too small"**
  for its skin to be used, so the skin was imported. Natural python skin is prized but
  **cracks easily outside humid climates**, which is why synthetic nylon/polyester and hybrid
  **強化張り *kyōka-bari*** skins were developed. Neck of Okinawan ebony heartwood (often
  trees 100+ years old), plectrum of water-buffalo horn worn on the index finger; Amami uses
  long thin bamboo plectra. **[V]** (en.wikipedia Sanshin.) Relative size: the shamisen's
  neck is **~20 cm longer** and its body about two sizes larger. **[V]** (y-eisui.) Sanshin
  necks are 黒檀 with lacquer. **[V]** (ja.wikipedia 三味線.)
- **胡弓 *kokyū*.** A **bowed** member of the same family — built much like the shamisen but
  smaller, usually three strings (four is rarer), square-bodied on the mainland and round in
  Okinawa, played upright with a horsehair bow, and often tuned like the shamisen but an
  octave higher. Because it is bowed it has **no 撥皮 bachikawa**. **[V]** (en.wikipedia
  Kokyū; e-kameya No. 226 for the bachikawa point.) en.wikipedia Shamisen notes the shamisen
  is itself sometimes bowed in a similar way. **[V]**
- **琵琶 *biwa*.** The source of the shamisen's **plectrum idiom** and its **sibling in
  sawari**. The Satsuma biwa's whole body (top and shell back) is **mulberry** (*Morus alba*;
  クワ) — the least resonant wood in Yoshikawa's table (ρ 647 kg/m³, E 6.3 GPa, Q 70), chosen
  *because* "poorness of body resonance is required to this striking play to avoid too-loud
  sounds." Its plectrum is a **very large triangular wooden bachi of boxwood (柘植 *tsuge*)**
  and "often hits the top plate immediately after plucking the string" — the same
  skin/soundboard-striking idiom the shamisen inherited. Its sawari is **surface-type and
  applied to all four strings, at both the ivory nut (鳥口 *tori-kuchi* / 上原 *shōgen*) and
  all four frets** — unlike the shamisen's point-type, first-string-only sawari. Its frets
  are 朴の木 *hō-no-ki* (*Magnolia hypoleuca*), "very hard." **[V]** (Yoshikawa 2010 §11.2.)
  ja.wikipedia 三味線 and wa-gokoro both say that when the instrument reached the mainland
  its plectrum changed to a biwa-style type. **[V]**
- Also in Yoshikawa's chapter for context: the Chinese **pipa** (paulownia top, red-sandalwood
  or maple back shell, 20+ frets, finger/pick plucked — "relatively close to the guitar
  sounds", achieving its high-frequency emphasis by *body* tuning rather than by sawari);
  the Japanese **koto** (paulownia); Korean **gayageum** and **geomungo**. **[V]**

### 8.2 The name

- **三味線 *shamisen*** / **三味線 *samisen*** / **三絃 *sangen*** — all current, all meaning
  "three strings." **[V]** (en.wikipedia Shamisen: "also known as sangen (三絃) (all meaning
  'three strings')"; Yoshikawa 2010: "The Japanese shamisen (meaning 'three-stringed')".)
  *samisen* is the older/unvoiced reading of the same characters and is common in older
  English writing and in some Japanese regional and genre usage. **[U]** on that last
  distribution claim — I did not source it.
- **Voicing shift to *-jamisen* in compounds.** en.wikipedia Shamisen: "*jamisen* is used as
  a suffix (e.g. *tsugaru-jamisen*) through sound change." **[V]** This is regular Japanese
  **連濁 *rendaku*** — sequential voicing of an initial consonant in the second element of a
  compound — so 津軽 + 三味線 → *tsugaru-jamisen*, 沖縄 + 三線 → *okinawa-jamisen*. The
  rendaku identification is standard Japanese phonology but **[U]** as to a source I read in
  this session.

### 9.2 Beginner vs serious — what the sources actually say

- **mouneru.com:** beginners **do not need** body refinements (ayasugi, kinboso); ¥200,000–
  300,000 is a reasonable ceiling even for those with money. **[V]**
- **Bachido:** the thing that actually matters is the **skin** ("influences about 80% of the
  sound quality" — his estimate, **[U]**, but directionally supported by Yoshikawa), and the
  neck wood is "the main way shamisen are priced." On the common claim that neck hardness
  affects tone, Bachido is sceptical in print: "It's also claimed that the hardness of the
  neck affects the sound quality, but **considerably? I doubt it.**" **[V]** Given Yoshikawa's
  finding that the neck/body wood's job is *transmission* rather than radiation, that
  scepticism is defensible, though Yoshikawa also treats the neck as acoustically important
  for conveying sawari to the other strings. The two are not quite in conflict. **[U]**
- **The one upgrade that is functional rather than cosmetic is the azuma-sawari.** Bachido:
  low-end instruments still have the sawari buzz, but "the advantage [of azuma-sawari] is
  that you can adjust the amount of buzz created. Switching string gauges or tunings require
  adjustment… which can't be done on a low-end shamisen." **[V]** metal-shamisen goes
  further: **he advises against beginner Tsugaru instruments without azuma-sawari, and
  against futozao models without the リンドウ metal fitting.** **[V]**
- **The functional, as against decorative, checklist therefore is:** (1) does it have an
  azuma-sawari; (2) is the neck straight and the fingerboard not dished; (3) is the skin
  sound and the right type for your climate; (4) do the pegs hold. Woods, kinboso, ayasugi
  and ivory are grade signals. **[U]** — my synthesis of the above.

### 9.3 What to check when buying used

Composite checklist; every item is **[V]** to at least one of wagakkimedia.com (2021),
mouneru.com (2020), note.com/shimapan_tv, shamisen-mizuki.com.

1. **Neck (棹): twist, warp, cracks, and the tenon (ほぞ) condition.** Tangled strings or a
   very dirty instrument signals poor storage, which is what causes this damage. A bent or
   over-thinned neck may be unfixable. **[V]**
2. **勘べり *kanberi* — fingerboard wear.** Dishing under the fingers affects tone; repair
   costs "tens of thousands of yen"; avoid boards with many uneven spots. Karin wears far
   faster than kōki. Bachido sells "Kanberi Repair" as a line item, which tells you how
   routine it is. **[V]**
3. **天神 *tenjin*.** The fragile end. Check for chips, cracks and repair history. A chipped
   tenjin looks bad on stage; a shop can repair it. **[V]**
4. **Skin.** Torn? Both faces cost **¥40,000–70,000** to replace in Japan or **$200–400**
   natural / **$99–250** Hibiki at Bachido. **A ¥50,000 body can total over ¥100,000.**
   **[V]**
5. **糸巻 itomaki.** Cracks, breaks, repair history, whether they need adjusting. Loose pegs
   are common and fixable (Bachido: $30–90). Replacement can run tens of thousands of yen.
   Ebony is sufficient; don't pay for ivory. **[V]**
6. **福林 *fukurin*** — the metal ferrules on the pegs can work loose and spoil tuning. Ask.
   **[V]**
7. **「ハ」(string height / action).** Listed by mouneru as an auction risk alongside
   kanberi and a bent neck. **[V]**
8. **Mis-described class.** Watch for "Tsugaru shamisen" used as a search keyword on what is
   actually a hosozao. **[V]** Measure the width below the chibukuro (§3.1) if you can.
9. **Accessories.** Is a bachi, koma and case included? Buying them separately is expensive;
   a listing with no accessories may be junk or a flip. **[V]**
10. **Photo editing** hides colour mismatch and scratches; auctions are
    **「ノークレーム、ノーリターン」** (no claims, no returns). **[V]**
11. **An ayasugi body is weak positive evidence** that the neck is also good — mouneru says
    so and immediately adds "at the buyer's own risk." **[V]**

**The uniform recommendation from every Japanese source on this topic: buy your first
instrument from a specialist shop, not an auction. Consider auctions for a second instrument
or later, once you can diagnose the above yourself.** **[V]** (mouneru.com; wagakkimedia.com.)
The upside of used is real: shamisen-mizuki notes used instruments often use **high-quality
older kōki** that is no longer obtainable, and a good repair makes them like new for less.
**[V]**

### 9.4 Synthetic skin outside Japan or in a variable climate

The case is strong, and it rests on verified facts rather than preference:

- The adhesive is **water-soluble rice/starch paste**, so humidity attacks the glue line
  before it attacks the membrane. **[V]** (mukouyama; ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線.)
- Natural skin is stretched **almost to tearing**, so it has no slack to give. **[V]**
  (mukouyama.)
- Lifespan in a variable climate: **1–2 years**; in a stable one, **7+ years**. **[V]**
  (Bachido.)
- Replacement is **¥40,000–70,000 / $200–400** each time. **[V]**
- Synthetic is semi-permanent and rain-resistant, with a documented 3-year field record
  showing cosmetic blackening but **no change in sound**. **[V]** (ameblo/duckchan109.)
- metal-shamisen: humidity management matters "mainly for dog-leather heads… **synthetic
  heads don't need it**." **[V]**
- The costs are honest: narrower tonal range **[V]** (nikkoudou-kottou), stiffer with
  prominent upper mids **[V]**, "merely adequate" sound and poor heat tolerance per a shop
  that sells both **[V]** (mukouyama), and a slightly abrasive surface against the forearm
  on woven types **[V]** (Bachido).

**Conclusion: for anyone outside Japan, or in a climate with real seasonal humidity swings,
or who busks/plays outdoors, synthetic is the rational default** — not because it sounds as
good, but because the alternative is a recurring several-hundred-dollar bill and an
instrument that is unplayable at unpredictable moments. Keep natural skin for a second,
climate-controlled instrument. **[U]** — my recommendation, built on the verified facts above.

### 9.5 Storage and humidity

- **Minimise swings in both temperature and humidity.** metal-shamisen:
  「楽器全般として温度・湿度変化できるだけを抑える事は大切なことです」, while candidly
  admitting he doesn't know exactly how much control is needed. **[V]** **I found no source
  giving a target RH range or a number.** **[U]** — anyone who quotes you "45–55% RH" for a
  shamisen is extrapolating from general instrument care.
- **The tools actually used:** a **和紙胴袋** washi body bag (¥781), a desiccant pack
  (「雅」, ¥1,890), a **桐胴板** paulownia body board (¥2,200), vinyl bags, plus ordinary
  desiccants. **[V]** (metal-shamisen `lesson03`.)
- **Keep the wrapping** that came with the instrument from the shop or the reskinning — it
  is there to protect the skin. **[V]** (wa-gokoro.jp.)
- **Avoid direct sun and high heat/humidity.** **[V]** (wa-gokoro.jp.)
- **Wipe hand sweat off after playing**, and use humidity packs — advice specifically for
  cat-skin instruments. **[V]** (ameblo/duckchan109.)
- **Wear a dōkake** — it exists to keep forearm sweat off the glue. **[V]** (Bachido.)
- **Store a tortoiseshell bachi in the light** (insects prefer the dark). **[V]**
  (wa-gokoro.jp.)
- **On polishing cloths, a corrective.** Bachido pushes back hard on received wisdom: "some
  folks have said, 'You need a polishing cloth to play shamisen, because you must wipe your
  shamisen after every session otherwise you will ruin it!'… such a statement is coming from
  a source of fear and ignorance… over 85% of people really don't know the reason behind
  elements of shamisen yet will speak like they do." He classes a cloth as "good to have for
  long-term care," not essential. **[V]** as Bachido's position. Given that the real failure
  mode is *glue plus moisture*, wiping sweat off the dō is sensible; "ruin it in one session"
  is not supported by anything I read. **[U]**
- **Dō-gomu dries out and crumbles after about five years** and is much harder to remove
  once it has, so replace it early. **[V]** (Bachido.)

### 9.6 The three-piece neck and travel

- **What it buys you:** portability, less warping, easier storage. **[V]** (ja.wikipedia
  三味線; Bachido.)
- **What it costs you:** the joints are a wear point. metal-shamisen warns that **a
  three-fold case is not recommended for daily use because repeatedly breaking the
  instrument down wears or breaks the 枘 (tenon)**. **[V]** He also reports that hard cases
  have a design problem that can damage the instrument in transport, and that soft cases
  fail too — a teacher's instrument broke when a soft case fell on a train. **[V]**
- **His practical fixes:** build a tenon guard from cardboard or air-cushion wrap if you
  lack a proper case, and have a shop make a **仮継ぎ *karitsugi*** (temporary/practice
  joint) for repeated assembly. **[V]**
- **Disassembly is not difficult but you must know the procedure.** Bachido: "it is crucial
  to know exactly what to do," and points to Masahiro Nitta's demonstration videos. **[V]**
- Dog-skin instruments are kept in a cloth **長袋 nagafukuro** inside the case. **[V]**
  (metal-shamisen.)

**Net:** the mitsuore neck is what makes a shamisen a carry-on-sized object and the single
biggest reason it travels better than a guitar. But break it down for travel, not daily.
**[U]** — synthesis.

### 9.8 Is a cheap Chinese-made instrument a false economy? — the honest answer

**What I can verify:**

- Chinese-made instruments circulate in Japan and some buyers actively avoid them: a
  Chiebukuro poster writes 「津軽は中国産の三味線らしいので、そこが嫌で先生にお願いしました」
  — "the Tsugaru ones seemed to be Chinese-made, I didn't like that, so I went through my
  teacher instead." **[V]** as evidence of the attitude; worthless as evidence of quality.
- **I found no technical comparison, no teardown, no shop review, and no acoustic test of
  Chinese-made shamisen.** **[V]** (negative finding across several targeted searches.)
- **No source read for this file says "don't buy Chinese."** What they say is: buy from a
  specialist shop; check the neck, skin, pegs and tenon; and expect that an instrument
  needing a reskin costs ¥40,000–70,000 more than its sticker. **[V]**

**The honest answer, reasoned from verified facts — and I'll mark it as reasoning, not
finding [U]:**

The question is usually asked as "is the wood real kōki?" but that is the wrong axis. The
verified facts point somewhere else:

1. **The skin dominates the sound** (Yoshikawa: membrane resonance determines the system's
   strongest resonance; Bachido: ~80%). A cheap instrument's economy is almost always taken
   out of the skin and its stretching — and **stretching quality is labour, not material**.
   Bachido Warehouse charges **+¥10,000 just for a tighter stretch** on the same skin, which
   tells you how much the workmanship is worth.
2. **The things that are actually hard to get right are the azuma-sawari, the joint
   tenons, and the peg fit** — all of them fine mechanical fits in hard wood.
   metal-shamisen's advice to avoid beginner Tsugaru instruments lacking an azuma-sawari or
   the rindō fitting is really advice about where corners get cut.
3. **Karin warps and dishes** (kanberi) and a soft neck can be re-shaved only so far; a
   bent or over-thinned neck may be unfixable, and kanberi repair runs into tens of
   thousands of yen. That is the mechanism by which a cheap instrument becomes worthless
   rather than merely mediocre.
4. **Against all that:** Bachido's own position is that "as long as the shamisen is
   skillfully crafted, it can be built from a wide variety of woods," and it sells a
   **$500–640 "Beginner's Shamisen"** and a **$990 Matsu** at the bottom of its own range.
   A cheap instrument from a vendor who will stand behind it is not a false economy; a cheap
   instrument from an anonymous marketplace listing is a lottery whose losing tickets cost
   ¥40,000–70,000 (reskin) to ¥100,000+ (neck/tenjin) to fix.

So: **the false economy is not "Chinese-made" — it is "no recourse and no reskin budget."**
Buy the cheapest instrument you like from a seller who will answer an email about the sawari,
the neck straightness and the skin, and budget one reskin into the purchase price. If you are
outside Japan, specify synthetic skin and most of the risk evaporates.

## 10. Headline corrections and open questions

### Corrections to the commonly repeated account

1. **乳袋 *chibukuro* is at the top of the neck, below the tenjin — not "the shoulders where
   neck meets body."** That is **鳩胸 *hatomune***. The chibukuro matters because the
   neck-class measurement is taken directly below it. **[V]**
2. **花梨 *karin* is *Pterocarpus indicus* (amboyna/narra), a legume — not *Pseudocydonia
   sinensis* (the quince) and not *Chukrasia*.** Yoshikawa 2010 names it explicitly and
   gives its wood constants. The quince shares the Japanese name as a homonym; *Chukrasia*
   has no attested connection to the shamisen at all. **[V]**
3. **金細 is metal in the three-piece neck joints, not the interior carving.** The carving
   is **綾杉彫り**. They are different grade markers and often appear together. ("金細
   kinbosomachine" in circulation is a transcription artefact; the word is 金細, *kinboso* /
   *kinhoso*.) **[V]**
4. **指掛け / 指すり is a sleeve worn on the left thumb and index finger, not a slide fitted
   to the neck**, and the two terms appear to name the same accessory. **[V]**
5. **The ayasugi-bori resonance claim is unsubstantiated, and one shop's stated mechanism
   says it *shortens* the reverberation.** No measurement exists in anything I read, and
   Yoshikawa's chapter — the only quantitative shamisen acoustics I found — never mentions
   it. Call it a craft/grade signal. **[V]** for the absence of support; **[U]** for any
   acoustic verdict either way.
6. **Cat skin is not confined to "finest hosozao / geisha instruments."** It is used for
   high-grade hosozao, min'yō and jiuta chūzao, **and the face of gidayū futozao**. The real
   axis is thin-and-bright vs thick-and-powerful. **[V]**
7. **"Hibiki-Pro" does not appear to exist as a skin.** The named synthetics I could verify
   are **響 Hibiki** and **FiberSen** (both Bachido), **人工皮革 神楽 Kagura** (SEION), and
   **リプル Ripple**. The "-PRO" suffix belongs to a **yubikake**. **[V]**
8. **Dog skin for Tsugaru is being displaced by *goat*, not only by synthetics** — Bachido
   says so flatly, and Bachido Warehouse sells goat as a live product line. Sheep and
   kangaroo are also in use. **[V]**
9. **CITES: a finished kōki or shitan shamisen is, in the common cases, *not* CITES-
   controlled — but *Dalbergia cochinchinensis* is the exception that catches people.** See
   below. **[V]**
10. **The Japanese claim that kōki "is banned from import" is overstated.** shamisen-mizuki
    says imports are banned; wagakkimedia says CITES restriction drives up prices. What is
    actually controlled under Annotation #7 is **logs, wood chips, powder and extracts** —
    plus India's own export controls on red sanders, which is probably the real source of
    scarcity. Finished instruments are a different matter. **[V]** for the annotation;
    **[U]** for the Indian export-control attribution, which I did not verify.

### The CITES summary that matters for buying abroad

| Wood | Species | Appendix | Annotation | Finished musical instrument? |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **紅木 kōki** | *Pterocarpus santalinus* (south-India endemic; IUCN EN or NT, record conflicts) | **II** | **#7** — "no commodities other than **logs, wood chips, powder and extracts** are controlled" | **Not controlled** |
| **紫檀 shitan** (generic *Dalbergia*) | *Dalbergia* spp. | **II** | **#15** | **Exempt** under #15(c) |
| **紫檀 shitan** if it is **Siamese rosewood** | *Dalbergia cochinchinensis* | **II** | **#4** — **expressly excluded from #15** | **NOT exempt — permits required** |
| Brazilian rosewood (not a shamisen wood, but on Bachido's fingerboard blanks list as "rosewood") | *Dalbergia nigra* | **I** | none | **NOT exempt** |
| 花梨 karin | *Pterocarpus indicus* / *macrocarpus* | none found **[U]** | — | **No CITES listing found — but IUCN Endangered, extinct in Vietnam; check before shipping** |
| **鼈甲 bekkō**, **象牙 ivory** | hawksbill; elephants | **I** | — | Needs a **Musical Instrument Certificate** for non-commercial movement |

**Verification status of that table:**

- **Annotation #15, verbatim and verified** — read in full from the League of American
  Orchestras / industry-coalition document, April 2021. It applies to *Dalbergia* spp.
  (**except *Dalbergia nigra***) and *Guibourtia demeusei*, *G. pellegriniana*,
  *G. tessmannii*: "**All parts and derivatives, except:** a) Leaves, flowers, fruits, and
  seeds; b) Finished products to a maximum weight of wood of the listed species of up to
  **10 kg per shipment**; **c) Finished musical instruments, finished musical instrument
  parts, and finished musical instrument accessories**; d) Parts and derivatives of
  *Dalbergia cochinchinensis*, which are covered by **Annotation #4**; e) Parts and
  derivatives of *Dalbergia* spp. originating and exported from **Mexico**, which are
  covered by Annotation #6." **[V]** The same document quotes CITES interpretive guidance
  defining a *finished musical instrument* as one "ready to play or needs only the
  installation of parts to make it playable," including antiques under HS 97.05/97.06. **[V]**
- **History:** original #15 adopted at **CoP17 (Johannesburg, Sept–Oct 2016**, effective
  2 Jan 2017 **[V]** per wood-database); it "hindered trade, travel, and artistic activity,
  and imposed an extreme permit burden." The **revision at CoP18 (2019)** added the
  finished-instrument carve-out and "entered into effect." **[V]** (the Annotation #15
  document.) *I could not verify the exact effective date of the CoP18 revision — a forum
  post cites late August 2019 and other sources 26 November 2019.* **[U]**
- ***Dalbergia cochinchinensis* → Annotation #4, with no instrument exception.** **[V]** via
  three agreeing secondary sources: the #15 text itself (clause d), CITES CoP18 Inf. 026
  ("Parts and derivatives of Dalbergia cochinchinensis, which are covered by Annotation #4"),
  wood-database, and Tonewood Atlas ("cochinchinensis falls under annotation #4, **with no
  instrument exception**"). I **could not read Annotation #4's full text** — cites.org
  blocked every attempt. **[U]** on its exact wording. **Practical consequence: if your
  "shitan" shamisen is genuinely Siamese rosewood, shipping it internationally is a permit
  matter, and since the Japanese trade name 紫檀 is loose you may not know which species you
  own.** That is the single nastiest wrinkle in this section.
- ***Dalbergia nigra* is Appendix I with no annotation**; "the exemptions in annotation #15
  do **not** apply." **[V]** (wood-database, consistent with #15's own text.)
- ***Pterocarpus santalinus*: Appendix II, Annotation #7, "no commodities other than logs,
  wood chips, powder and extracts are controlled."* **[V]** via **three** agreeing
  secondary quotations — a CITES SC74 document on red sanders (E-SC74-30-01-A5c), Kew's
  *CITES and Timber* (which words it "only logs, woodchips, powder and extracts are
  regulated"), and ForestSource. **However:** both cites.org and kew.org returned 403/
  Cloudflare to every fetch and to a reader proxy, so **I never read a primary document for
  this annotation**, and I note that Kew's edition labels the annotation **#12** rather than
  **#7** — the two were renumbered at some point. **[U]** on the number; **[V]** on the
  substance (only raw/semi-processed forms are controlled).
- **Also worth knowing:** *Pterocarpus erinaceus* (West African kosso) is Appendix II **with
  no annotation**, so finished products *are* restricted **[V]** (wood-database); and
  African *Pterocarpus* spp. were added to Appendix II effective **25 November 2024**
  **[V]** (search snippets from jwallen.com and Timber Development UK — **[U]** on the exact
  scope). Neither is a traditional shamisen wood, but Shishidō does mention **African kōki**
  as a cheaper grade **[V]**, so a cheap "kōki" neck of African origin could conceivably be
  an African *Pterocarpus*. That is speculation — **[U]**.
- **Musical Instrument Certificate:** verified from UK Government guidance (§6.6). Free, for
  non-commercial movement. **[V]** Its CITES basis (Resolution Conf. 16.8), the species it
  covers, and its validity period are **[U]**.
- **Everything above is CITES only.** National law — the US Lacey Act and state ivory bans,
  the EU Deforestation Regulation, Japan's domestic rules — applies on top and can be
  stricter. **[V]** (wood-database's own caveat; the Tonewood Atlas page title names Lacey
  and EUDR, but I was **429-blocked** from reading it.) **[U]** on all national specifics.

### Open questions I could not resolve

1. **Who invented the azuma-sawari.** No name anywhere. Patented in the Meiji era per
   Yoshikawa Eishi's book (via metal-shamisen), with 1893 advertisements, but no inventor and
   no patent number. A dead-end worth chasing in 吉川英史『三味線の美学と芸大邦楽科誕生秘話』
   p. 103 directly.
2. **Whether 東さわり or 吾妻さわり is the historically correct orthography**, and where the
   name comes from. Also unexplained: the competing 大阪さわり and 都さわり.
3. **Measured skin tension by genre.** Nobody publishes numbers. The only figure in this
   file (2,000 N/m) is Takasawa's 1993 computational model, via Yoshikawa 2010, Table 11.2.
4. **A target humidity range for shamisen storage.** No source gives one; the most honest
   source admits he doesn't know.
5. **String diameters in mm**, and the relationship between the Tsugaru bachi **号 gō**
   scale and any physical dimension.
6. **Any acoustic test of ayasugi-bori.** None exists that I can find.
7. **Any technical assessment of Chinese-made shamisen.** None found.
8. **Henry Johnson, Groemer, Garland vol. 7, Grove.** All unreached as *texts*. If the final
   reference document needs a musicological rather than organological authority on the
   instrument, Johnson's *The Shamisen: Tradition and Diversity* (Brill, 2010) remains the
   obvious gap, and **nothing here is attributed to it.** The five records now identified but
   unread — Sakata 1966 (sawari), Yamada 2017 and 2020 (skins), Malm & Hughes in Grove
   (general), Johnson in Grove (yanagawa) — are the shortest path to closing the gaps in §2.3,
   §3.4 and §5.
11. **Specifications for the 柳川 / 京三味線 *yanagawa shamisen*.** Grove has an entry; I have
    no neck width, body size or string-gauge figures for it. §3.4.
12. **Whether *Pterocarpus indicus* (karin) carries any CITES listing.** The bibliographic pass
    found no mention in its species article, which is an argument from absence. Given that
    African *Pterocarpus* spp. were added to Appendix II effective 25 Nov 2024 and that the
    Asian species are Endangered on IUCN, this deserves a direct check against the current
    appendices before anyone ships a dō internationally.
9. **The exact effective date of the CoP18 Annotation #15 revision**, and the **full text of
   Annotation #4**. Both require access to cites.org, which blocked all automated fetches
   during this research.
10. **A luthier's account of the skin-stretching process itself** — the jig, the soak, the
    sequence, the drying. Shishidō's wiki page on 皮 is literally 「ただいま準備中です」
    ("currently in preparation"). The best organological source on this would likely be a
    museum or conservatory publication; I reached none.
