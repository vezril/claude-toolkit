# The Shamisen: Origins, Edo Development, and the Modern Era

Scope: the shamisen's **history**, from the sanxian's transmission through the Edo period to the
present, plus the **naming question**. The instrument as an object is in `instrument.md`; the genre
map is in `genres.md`; the blind and itinerant musicians who carried the music are in
`blind-and-itinerant-musicians.md`; technique, tuning and notation are in the
`shamisen-performance` skill; and the Tsugaru tradition is in `tsugaru-shamisen`.

## How to read this file

The part numbering (Part 1, 2, 5, 6) is retained from the research so that the cross-references
between these four files stay valid. **Part 3** (the blind and itinerant musicians) and **Part 4**
(the genre taxonomy) live in their own files, named above.

**Verification key**

- **[V]** = verified. The artifact actually read is named inline (title, URL, and fetch date).
- **[U]** = unverified, or verified only against a weak source. Flagged, with what is missing.

All fetches were made **2026-11-13** unless stated otherwise. Where a Japanese source is the real
authority I have used it and said so; **all translations from Japanese in this file are mine** and
are marked *(my translation)* at first use in each section.

**Method note / what blocked.** General web search was unavailable for this research: DuckDuckGo,
Brave, Ecosia, Mojeek, JSTOR's and Brill's own site search, and Google Books' API all refused or
rate-limited the fetcher. **Yahoo! Japan search worked**, which is why the Japanese-language
evidence trail in this file is considerably richer than the English one. Paywalled
English-language scholarship (Brill, Project MUSE, Taylor & Francis, Scholarly Publishing
Collective) could not be read directly; where I cite it I say whether I verified the
*bibliography* only or the *content*.

**One structural caution about the English-language literature.** The standard English account of
shamisen origins (reproduced in Malm, in the Kodansha encyclopedia, and from there into Wikipedia
and most popular writing) compresses a contested, legend-heavy, and poorly documented Japanese
scholarly debate into one clean sentence with a date in it. The Japanese reference literature is
visibly more cautious: it uses 「という」「とされる」「説」 ("it is said", "is held to be",
"one theory") at precisely the points where English sources use the indicative. That gap is the
most important finding in Part 1.

## 1. Origins and the transmission route

### 1.1 The instrument family: sanxian → sanshin → shamisen

**[V]** The shamisen's ancestor is the Chinese **三弦 sanxian**. The 日本大百科全書 (Nipponica)
entry on 三味線, signed by **藤田隆則 Fujita Takanori**, states that the instrument descends from
the Chinese sanxian and reached **Sakai in Izumi province (modern Osaka) by way of the Ryukyus in
the second half of the 16th century**: 「16世紀後半に琉球(りゅうきゅう)を経由して泉州（大阪）の
堺(さかい)に伝えられたとされる。」 — note the 「とされる」, "is held to have been" *(my
translation)*. Read via kotobank.jp/word/三味線-74657, 2026-11-13.

**[V]** The Ryukyuan link in the chain is the **三線 sanshin**. The Japanese Wikipedia article 三線
identifies its direct root as the 三弦 of **Fujian**, south-east China, and notes that the sanxian
and the sanshin are near-identical in parts, materials and construction, the sanshin differing in
having a shorter neck and a flatter body: 「中国大陸の南東部（現在の福建省）の弦楽器「三弦」を
直接のルーツとする。」 (ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/三線, 2026-11-13). The same article reports that
King **Shō Shin** (late 15th c.) promoted the 三弦 among the Ryukyuan 士族 as part of their
education, and that a tradition placing its arrival before the coming of the Kume 36 surnames in
the late 14th century is tagged 要出典 ("citation needed") in the article itself.

**[U]** Alternative or supplementary routes exist in the Japanese literature and should not be
dismissed. The Japanese Wikipedia 三味線 article reports **原一男's** derivation from the 奚琴
(Chinese *xiqin*), and a chain running through the Persian **setar** and **rabāb** to the Chinese
sanxian and thence to the Ryukyus. I have not read Hara's actual argument and cannot assess it.

**[V] — important and usually dropped in English:** the Ryukyu → mainland route is the **standard
theory, not a proven one**. The Okinawa Prefectural Library's reference case in the National Diet
Library Collaborative Reference Database (case id 1000085462, answered 2011-03-29; read at
crd.ndl.go.jp 2026-11-13) summarises **池宮正治 Ikemiya Masaharu's** essay in 『新琉球史 近世編 上』
(Ryūkyū Shinpōsha, 1989, pp. 231–258) as holding that China → Ryukyu → mainland is the
**定説 (received theory) but that there is no 確証 (definitive proof)** *(my translation)*. Ikemiya
further argues that **the transmission cannot have been a single event** — 「伝来は一回きりでは
なかったはず」 — and that on the documentary record the period is Eiroku rather than Bunroku.

**[U]** A second port of entry, **Hakata**, appears in several Japanese popular and trade sources
(e.g. fukumori-dou.com, akatsukikai-hougaku.com, found via Yahoo! Japan 2026-11-13) alongside
Sakai. I could not trace this to a scholarly source and it should be treated as trade lore until
someone checks Yoshikawa or the Okinawan historiography.

### 1.2 The 1562 question — where the date actually comes from

This was the single most worthwhile thing to chase, and the answer is that **1562 is a convention
with a traceable but weak provenance, not a documented date.**

**[V] No scholarly or encyclopedic Japanese source I read gives 1562.** They give the **Eiroku era
(永禄, 1558–1570)**:

- 日本大百科全書 (Fujita Takanori): "second half of the 16th century." (kotobank, 2026-11-13)
- 精選版 日本国語大辞典: Ryukyu's snake-skin lute was imported to Sakai **during the Eiroku era**
  and remade by biwa hōshi — and the sentence ends 「…という」, "so it is said" *(my translation)*.
  (kotobank, 2026-11-13)
- 改訂新版 世界大百科事典: gives Eiroku-era transmission to Sakai as the **leading theory**
  (有力な説). (kotobank, 2026-11-13)
- Japanese Wikipedia 三味線: 永禄年間 (1558–1569). **The article contains no "1562" at all.**
  (2026-11-13)
- Japanese Wikipedia 三線: narrows it further, to "the start of the Eiroku era (1558 or 1559)":
  「日本でいう永禄年間初頭（1558年または1559年）に泉州（現在の大阪府南部）堺へと伝わり」 — and
  this sentence carries **no footnote of its own**. (2026-11-13)
- 『音楽大事典』 vol. 3 (Heibonsha, 1982), pp. 1083–84, as summarised by the Okinawa Prefectural
  Library reference answer: Eiroku-era arrival is the reasonable reading **on the basis of
  performance records such as 『御湯殿上日記』**. (crd.ndl.go.jp case 1000085462, 2026-11-13)
- 『沖縄の三線』 (Okinawa Prefectural Board of Education, 1993), pp. 373–75, per the same
  reference answer: chronology gives **1558–1570**, i.e. a range, not a year.

**[V] Where 1562 does come from.** Three places — one Edo-period legend and two modern local ones,
none of them a record of the instrument's arrival:

0. **The earliest locus, and the one that matters: 『琉球年代記』 (*Ryūkyū nendaiki*).** As
   summarised in the 日本大百科全書 entry on 石村検校 (signed **林喜代弘**, read via
   kotobank.jp/word/石村検校, 2026-11-13): **梅津少将 Umezu Shōshō** drifted ashore in the Ryukyus,
   learned the **月琴 gekkin**, **returned home in 1562 (永禄5)**, and his son **石麻呂 Ishimaro** —
   who later obtained a blind office and became **石村検校** — improved the gekkin into the shamisen.
   **So 1562 in this source is the year a shipwrecked nobleman came home, in a legend whose
   instrument is a gekkin (a moon-lute, not a sanxian) and whose protagonist's parentage is
   elsewhere given differently** (改訂新版世界大百科事典 notes a rival account making Ishimura the
   son 通世 of 久我敦通 — i.e. of the Kuga house that was the blind guild's own patron). The entry's
   cited references are **藤田徳太郎「三絃の免許状」** in 『東亜音楽論叢』 (山一書房, 1943) and
   **吉川英史「三絃伝来考」**, reprinted in his 『三味線とその音楽』 (音楽之友社, 1978). This is
   almost certainly the ultimate source of the number, laundered over time into a statement about
   the instrument rather than about Umezu's voyage home.

1. **『堺市史』 (*History of Sakai City*), vol. 7, supplementary volume**, published by the Sakai
   City Office in **1930**, entry **(154) 中小路**. Digitised by Sakai City Central Library on
   ADEAC (adeac.jp/sakai-lib/text-list/d100070/ht001740; breadcrumb and catalogue verified
   2026-11-13; body text read via Yahoo! Japan snippet, as the ADEAC viewer is JavaScript-rendered).
   The text reads: 「中小路は堺の人、幼より明を失ひ、琵琶法師となつた。【蛇皮線の舶來】永祿年中
   （或は永祿五年）琉球から、蛇皮を以て張つた二絃の樂器が、堺に舶載された。」 — "Nakakōji was a
   man of Sakai; he lost his sight in childhood and became a biwa hōshi. **[The importation of the
   jabisen]** During the Eiroku era (**or in Eiroku 5**), a two-stringed instrument covered with
   snake skin was shipped to Sakai from the Ryukyus." *(my translation.)* **Eiroku 5 = 1562.** Note
   the parenthetical hedge, and note that the gazetteer says **two strings**, which is wrong about
   the sanshin and marks the passage as tradition rather than record. Publication details confirmed
   via Osaka Prefectural Library's 堺市史 page and the Amagasaki city archive catalogue
   (昭和5年 = 1930, 堺市役所), Yahoo! Japan 2026-11-13.
2. **The Sakai Tourism and Convention Bureau**, "ものの始まりなんでも堺" page
   (sakai-tcb.or.jp/about-sakai/origin/main.html, read 2026-11-13), which states the year flatly as
   **永禄五年 (1562)**: a sailor back from the Ryukyus brought a "サムシェン" home as a souvenir, gave
   it to a biwa hōshi in Sakai, and a monk named **中小路法師** changed it from two strings to three,
   squared off the round body, and replaced the snake skin with cat skin — 「これが三味線の
   はじまりです」, "this is the beginning of the shamisen" *(my translation)*. The page notes in
   passing that some hold the Ryukyuan instrument already had three strings. **It cites no source
   whatsoever.**

**[V]** One Japanese secondary source attributes the narrowing to **吉川英史 Yoshikawa Eishi**,
saying his research holds for Eiroku-era arrival at Sakai with **1562 as the most likely year**
(yo.rim.or.jp/~kosyuuan/kosyuan/iida/iida18.htm, title and snippet captured via Yahoo! Japan
2026-11-13; **the page itself refused connection and I could not read it**). Yoshikawa's actual
paper is real and locatable: **吉川英史「三絃伝来考」『東洋音楽研究』 no. 14–15 (1958), pp. 29–53**,
DOI 10.11446/toyoongakukenkyu1936.1958.29, published by the 東洋音楽学会 (verified via CiNii
Research opensearch API, cir.nii.ac.jp, 2026-11-13; **the J-STAGE article page returned HTTP 500
and I could not read the paper**). **[U]** So: that Yoshikawa prefers 1562 is unverified. This is
the highest-value single thing left to check in this section — anyone with library access should
read pp. 29–53 of that article and settle it.

**Bottom line for the reference document.** Write: *"The shamisen is generally held to have
reached the mainland at Sakai from the Ryukyus during the Eiroku era (1558–70). The frequently
repeated year 1562 (Eiroku 5) is not a date of arrival at all: it enters the literature through the
Edo-period legend in 『琉球年代記』 as the year a shipwrecked nobleman returned from the Ryukyus with
a moon-lute, and survives in the 1930 Sakai city gazetteer as a parenthetical alternative to 'the
Eiroku era.' It rests on no contemporary document, and Japanese scholarship treats the transmission
as a process rather than an event."* Do **not** write "1562" without that qualification. No
Japanese encyclopedia entry on the instrument gives it.

### 1.3 What the documents actually show

The documentary floor is much later than the Eiroku story and much more interesting.

**[V] Earliest attestation of the word 三味線 in a Japanese document: 1580.** 『御湯殿上日記』
(*Oyudono no ue no nikki*, the rotating duty-diary kept in kana by the ladies-in-waiting of the
imperial palace, running 1477–1826), entry for **天正8年 (1580), 2nd month, 16th day**:
「まいののち、宮の御かた、御かはらの物、山しろといふ、しやみせんひかせらるる」 — "after the dance,
in the Palace apartments, a kawaramono called Yamashiro was made to play the shamisen" *(my
translation)*. This is the headword citation for 三味線 in the 日本国語大辞典 citation apparatus,
reproduced at sanabo.com/kazoekata/ct_sa/si/syamisen/ (read 2026-11-13) and corroborated with the
same reading by ittosya.net/syamisen (read 2026-11-13) and by the J-STAGE article 「沖縄の音楽文化」
in 日本音響学会誌 61(2) (the Yahoo! Japan snippet of which quotes the 天正8年（1580）2月条 and the
phrase 御かはらの物（河原者）山しろ; **the PDF itself is a scanned image and could not be read**).

Two things to notice. First, the earliest named shamisen player in the Japanese record is a
**河原者 kawaramono** — a riverbed outcast. Second, the venue is the **imperial palace**. The
instrument enters the written record already connecting the bottom of the social order to the top.

**[V] Next earliest: blind professionals, 1587–1592.** 『言経卿記』 (*Tokitsune-kyō ki*, the diary
of the courtier **山科言経 Yamashina Tokitsune**), entries for **天正15 (1587) 4/1; 天正17 (1589)
5/14; 天正20 (1592) 8/15 and 8/24**, record three **座頭 zatō** (blind professional musicians)
named 梅一, 恵城 and 福仁 performing *Heike monogatari*, **jōruri**, and shamisen. (ittosya.net/
syamisen, read 2026-11-13 — a shamisen dealer's history page, not a scholarly source, **[U]** for
the exact dates until someone checks an edition of the diary, but **[V]** that the claim is made
with specific citations.) The significance: by 1592 blind musicians are already pairing the
shamisen with jōruri recitation — which is the seed of the entire narrative-genre half of the
taxonomy.

**[V] 1603–04: the instrument is in the first Japanese–European dictionary.** The 『日葡辞書』
(*Vocabulario da Lingoa de Iapam*, Jesuit press, Nagasaki, 1603, supplement 1604) carries the
headword **Xamixen**, glossed in the standard modern Japanese edition as 「三絃の或る種のヴィオラ」
("a kind of viola with three strings") *(my translation)*. Verified via seikonagata.com/2020/05/07/
nippojisho-viola/blog (read 2026-11-13), which works from 『邦訳 日葡辞書』, ed. 土井忠生・森田武・
長南実 (Iwanami Shoten, 1980); corroborated independently by the 日国 citation list at sanabo.com.
The same dictionary glosses **biwa** with the bare Portuguese *viola* too, so "viola" here means a
plucked lute, not a bowed instrument.

**[V] The 日国 citation sequence** (sanabo.com, read 2026-11-13) — useful as a rough curve of the
word's spread into print: 御湯殿上日記 (1580) → 日葡辞書 (1603–04) → 虎明本狂言「昆布売」 (late
Muromachi–early Edo; the Toraakira kyōgen manuscript is 1642) → 仮名草子『恨の介』 (c. 1609–17) →
俳諧『類船集』 (1676) → 浮世草子『傾城禁短気』 (1711). The 日国 dates the alternative reading
**さみせん** *samisen* only from **1632** (仮名草子『尤双紙』), and the Sino-Japanese reading **三弦
さんげん** *sangen*, used of the shamisen, only from a **1678** 評判記 — see `history.md`, Part
6.

**[V] — AND EARLIER STILL, BY FIVE YEARS: 1575, for the snakeskin instrument.** The 精選版日本国語
大辞典 entry on **蛇皮線 jabisen** (kotobank.jp/word/蛇皮線, read 2026-11-13) gives as its earliest
citation 「**琉球人しやひせんとも曳候て**」 from 『**上井覚兼日記**』, **1575** — the diary of the
Shimazu retainer Uwai Kakuken, recording Ryukyuans and their *shahisen*. **So the earliest Japanese
documentary trace of this instrument family is not the shamisen at all but the Ryukyuan snakeskin
lute, five years before the first shamisen entry — which is exactly what the Ryukyu-route theory
predicts, and is the best single piece of documentary support it has.** The same entry gives the
variant reading **じゃみせん** and the alternative spelling **蛇味線** — with the same phonetic 味 as
in 三味線. That has consequences for the *jamisen* question; see `history.md`, §6.4.

**[V] Earliest surviving instrument and earliest dated image.**
- The oldest extant shamisen is called **「淀」 Yodo**, made in **1597** for Toyotomi Hideyoshi's
  consort Yodo-dono by the Kyoto craftsman **神田晴光 Kanda Harumitsu**; the Nagahama City Hikiyama
  Museum's exhibition page adds that within roughly **40 years of arrival** the instrument's form
  was already fully Japanised and essentially the modern shape
  (nagahama-hikiyama.or.jp/exhibition/091/, read 2026-11-13; the exhibition drew on loans from the
  大阪音楽大学音楽博物館 and a lecture by 五島邦治 of Kyoto University of Art and Design). The
  Japanese Wikipedia 三味線 article independently reports the survival of 「淀」. **[U]** I have not
  seen a catalogue record for the instrument itself.
- **Miniature shamisen excavated in Osaka, dated 1620**, cited on the same museum page as evidence
  of the instrument's wide early-Edo popularity. **[U]** no excavation report read.
- **[V]** The **彦根屏風 Hikone Screens** — official designation 紙本金地著色風俗図, designated a
  **National Treasure in 1955**, acquired by Hikone City in 1997, held at the **Hikone Castle
  Museum**, painted section 94.0 × 271.0 cm — are dated by the museum to the **Kan'ei era
  (1624–44)**, with the setting presumed to be a **Kyoto pleasure quarter**, and the museum's own
  description names the **三味線** among the depicted motifs (read in Japanese at
  hikone-castle-museum.jp/exhibition_old/11291.html, 2026-11-13; designation details from
  Chūnichi Shimbun, 2023-05-10, via Yahoo! Japan). This is a firmly dated, nationally designated
  image of the shamisen *in the pleasure quarters* within roughly two generations of its arrival.

### 1.4 The biwa hōshi adaptation

The claim that **琵琶法師 biwa hōshi** — blind lute priests, the professional reciters of
*Heike monogatari* — are the people who turned the sanshin into the shamisen is universal in both
languages. It is better evidenced for some changes than others.

**[V] The plectrum.** The Japan Arts Council's 文化デジタルライブラリー instrument guide
(楽器図鑑, 太棹三味線 page, www2.ntj.jac.go.jp/dglib/contents/learn/edc6/edc_new/html/233_futozao.html)
states that the sanshin was first played in Japan by biwa hōshi in the 16th century and that they
**repurposed the biwa's plectrum** — 「琵琶の撥(ばち)を流用し」 *(my translation)*. **Caveat on the
artifact:** the page body is JavaScript-rendered and WebFetch returned only navigation; I read that
phrase in the Yahoo! Japan index snippet of the page (2026-11-13), not in the page itself. This is
the most authoritative institutional source I found for the claim, and it is worth re-reading in a
browser. The contrast it implies is correct and well attested elsewhere: the Okinawan sanshin is
played with a **爪 tsume**, a claw or fingerpick worn on the index finger (ja.wikipedia 三線,
爪・撥 section, read 2026-11-13: typically 5–15 cm, curved, used mainly for downstrokes, buffalo
horn for the good ones), whereas the shamisen is struck with a large flat **撥 bachi**.
**[U]** The further claim that this specifically reflects the *heikyoku* plectrum — i.e. that the
transfer came through the Heike-recitation repertoire rather than from blind-priest biwa generally
— is stated as 「言われる」 ("it is said") in the Japanese Wikipedia 三味線 article and I found no
source that establishes it. One biwa specialist's history page runs the influence the *other* way
for a later instrument, saying the early Chikuzen biwa's plectrum was modelled on the shamisen's
(guignardbiwa.com/biwahistory/, via Yahoo! Japan snippet, 2026-11-13) — a reminder that borrowing
between these instruments was two-directional.

**[U] / contested — the sawari.** The buzzing-drone device is commonly said in English to have been
introduced by the biwa hōshi in imitation of the biwa. **This is one of two competing theories and
is not settled.** The Japanese Wikipedia article さわり gives both: that the sawari was first
"discovered" in Japan on the biwa (Satsuma biwa in particular is noted for a strong sawari), and
that the origin is the **javari of the Indian vīṇā** (ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/さわり, read 2026-11-13).
That article is flagged on Wikipedia itself as resting on a single source — Tōru Takemitsu,
*Confronting Silence: Selected Writings* (Fallen Leaf Press, 1995, ISBN 091491331X) — from which it
takes Takemitsu's observation that the word *sawari* carries both 「触れる」 ("to touch") and
「差し障り」 ("obstruction, hindrance"). A shamisen maker's blog explicitly labels the biwa-influence
account his own 私見 (personal view) (e-kameya.com/blog/?p=3366, via snippet 2026-11-13). **Report
the biwa derivation as a hypothesis, not a fact.** Note separately that in *jōruri* usage さわり
means something entirely different — the emotional high point of a piece, the "bit worth hearing" —
which is the origin of the modern Japanese idiom; don't let the two senses contaminate each other.

**[U] The skins.** The explanation that snake skin was replaced by cat and dog skin because Japan
has no snake large enough to head a shamisen body is repeated everywhere and I could **not** find a
scholarly source for it. What I have: the Okinawan sanshin uses **python** skin (ja.wikipedia 三線
notes an import record for snake skin in 1767, citing 安里進 et al., eds., 『沖縄県の歴史』 県史47,
Yamakawa Shuppansha, 2004, p. 195); and *Tokyo Shimbun*, 2022-05-14 (tokyo-np.co.jp/article/177215,
via snippet 2026-11-13), states that because python is hard to obtain on the main islands, nearby
dog and cat skin was used. That is a newspaper, not scholarship. The Japanese Wikipedia 三味線
article gives the same account and adds that cat belly skin and dog skin have been the traditional
materials and that tsugaru-jamisen uses dog skin almost exclusively. Treat the *reason* as the
standard explanation, plausible and unverified. (The physical-instrument file covers the skins
properly; this entry exists only to flag that the historical *causal* claim is unsourced.)

### 1.5 Named early figures — and the name the question asked about

**The name "Nakakogaki Ryūzaemon" is not attested and I believe it is a garbling.** [V] I searched
specifically for personal names of the form 中小路○右衛門 in connection with the shamisen's
introduction and found nothing (Yahoo! Japan, 2026-11-13). The real name in the tradition is
**中小路 / 仲小路**, with no given name attached.

**[V] 仲小路 / 中小路 — and the strong possibility that it is a place, not a person.** This is the
most interesting correction in the section.

- 改訂新版 世界大百科事典 (entry on 石村検校 signed **平野健次 Hirano Kenji**, a major figure in
  Japanese music scholarship) names **two candidates for the first improver of the instrument** —
  「最初の改良者として，琵琶法師の仲小路（なかしようじ）説，石村検校説」 — and reports as the
  **leading view (有力)** that they are **the same person**, because 石村検校 is recorded as having
  **lived at Nakashōji in Sakai**: 「堺の中小路（なかしようじ）に住み，三味線の改良者の中小路という
  琵琶法師とは同一人であるという説が有力。」 *(my translation.)* Read via kotobank.jp/word/石村検校
  (and the variant page id 30642), 2026-11-13.
- The source cited for the residence detail is 『野河検校流三線統系序』, which per the kotobank
  digest of the 石村検校 entries says he **lived at 中小路 in Sakai**, improved the two-stringed
  snake-skin instrument that had come from the Ryukyus, and named it 三線.
- 『堺市史』 (1930), by contrast, treats 中小路 straightforwardly as a man: 「中小路は堺の人」.

So the chain is plausibly: *a street or quarter in Sakai called Nakashōji* → *the blind musician who
lived there, known by it* → *a separate legendary "biwa hōshi named Nakakōji"*. I cannot prove that
reading, but it is what the leading Japanese encyclopedia's own reasoning implies. **[U]** Nobody I
read states the place-name derivation outright. Note also that the **reading is unstable**:
なかしょうじ (世界大百科事典), なかのこうじ (Japanese Wikipedia 三味線), and the characters appear
both as 中小路 and 仲小路. Romanise with a note, or avoid.

**[V] 石村検校 Ishimura Kengyō — a real but hazy figure, and the most important early name.**
Compiled from the dictionary entries on kotobank.jp/word/石村検校 (read 2026-11-13):

| Dictionary | Dates given | Credited with |
|---|---|---|
| デジタル大辞泉 (Shōgakukan) | b. unknown – **1642** | Kyoto man, biwa hōshi turned shamisen player; composer of the 本手組 *honte-gumi* |
| 日本大百科全書 | dates unknown | blind musician of the late Muromachi (later 16th c.) who **is said to have** moved from biwa to shamisen |
| 改訂新版 世界大百科事典 (Hirano Kenji) | d. **1642 (寛永19)** | blind musician of the early Edo period; **founder of 三味線組歌**; the 《琉球組》 is **attributed** to him |
| ブリタニカ国際大百科事典 | d. **24 Sept 1642**, in Kyoto | composed the 本手組; **"made shamisen music into an art"** |
| 日本人名大辞典+Plus (Kōdansha) | b. unknown – 1642 | 琵琶法師; composed 《琉球組》 **"so it is said, but there are other accounts" (異説もある)** |

The legends attached to him, all explicitly hedged in the dictionaries:
- That in the **Bunroku era (1592–96)** he crossed to the **Ryukyus**, saw an instrument there, and
  on returning to Kyoto made the shamisen. 日本大百科全書 attributes this story to **『糸竹初心集』**
  and **ブリタニカ flags it as 疑わしい ("doubtful")**.
- That 梅津少将's son 石麻呂 adapted a **月琴** into the shamisen — from 『琉球年代記』.
- The 中小路-residence account from 『野河検校流三線統系序』, above.
- That 《琉球組》 was actually composed or revised by **虎沢検校 Torazawa Kengyō**.
- He is also named as the **first player of the 胡弓 kokyū**.

**[V] 『糸竹初心集』 (*Shichiku Shoshinshū*, 1664)** is a real and important text and the proximate
source of the Ishimura origin legend. Compiled by the blind **中村宗三 Nakamura Sōzō**, published in
Kyoto by Akitaya on Teramachi-dōri in **1664 (寛文4)**, reissued 1672; it is a self-instruction
primer for **一節切 hitoyogiri shakuhachi, koto and shamisen** and is described as the oldest
*published* such book and the earliest printed source for early-modern Japanese music notation. It
contains the line 「文禄のころほひ、石村検校と云法師あり」 ("around the Bunroku era there was a
priest called Ishimura Kengyō") and the story that he saw an instrument called 「小弓」 in the
Ryukyus, took it for a modified biwa, learned it, returned to Kyoto, and devised the shamisen *(my
translation)*. Bibliographic details and the quotation gathered from Yahoo! Japan snippets of
idolapedia.sakura.ne.jp, note.com/junjunjunpiano, and www5c.biglobe.ne.jp/obara/tenji/nenpyo00.html
(2026-11-13); **[U]** I did not read a facsimile or scholarly edition. Note the date logic: the book
is **about 70 years after the events it describes and roughly a century after the Eiroku arrival**.

**[V] 柳川検校 Yanagawa Kengyō** — blind musician, early Edo; became 検校 in **1639 (寛永16)**, died
**11 July 1680 (延宝8)**; founder of the **柳川流** of jiuta shamisen in Kyoto, from which the
narrow-necked **柳川三味線** (the oldest surviving instrument form) and its small *kyō-bachi* take
their name; credited with composing 破手組 pieces. His teacher is disputed — Ishimura Kengyō in one
account, 山野井検校 in another — and the dictionaries hedge the compositions with 「といわれる」.
(kotobank.jp/word/柳川検校, read 2026-11-13.)

**[V] 沢住検校 Sawazumi Kengyō** — blind biwa hōshi and shamisen player active in Kyoto in the
**late 16th century**, dates unknown; pupil of Torazawa Kengyō; **traditionally the founder of
jōruri shamisen**, i.e. the first to accompany jōruri recitation on the instrument. 改訂新版
世界大百科事典 grounds this on **『色道大鏡』** (*Shikidō ōkagami*, 1678) and adds that 薩摩浄雲
came out of his line and that the 「沢」 element in gidayū-school surnames derives from him. Every
dictionary marks the achievement with 「という」/「とされる」 — none treats it as established.
(kotobank.jp/word/沢住検校, read 2026-11-13.)

**[V] 三味線組歌 shamisen kumiuta — the oldest art repertoire.** Also called 三味線本手; so named
because the lyrics of popular songs and folk songs were *assembled* (組) into single pieces. Held to
be **the oldest genre of artistic shamisen music**. Split into **本手組** (the old-type pieces) and
**破手組** ("breaking the honte pattern" — more complex, irregular hands). Later organised
pedagogically into four grades, 表・裏・中・奥, each with its own licence (許し免状), hence the
terms 表許し and 奥許し. Ishimura Kengyō is said to have begun it around **Bunroku (1592–96)**, with
《琉球組》 the oldest piece; Torazawa and Yanagawa Kengyō then expanded and systematised it. Two
lineages: the **柳川流** of Kyoto, whose texts are preserved in 『松の葉』 (1703) and of which **only
six pieces survive**, and the **野川流** of Osaka, founded by 野川検校, of which **all 32 pieces
survive** — and the Nogawa line is **the only one still transmitted**. The shamisen songs handled
by blind musicians came to be called **地歌 jiuta**, and the kumiuta became the normative
professional repertoire of jiuta; "野川流" doubles as a jiuta school name.
(kotobank.jp/word/三味線組歌, read 2026-11-13, drawing on 精選版日本国語大辞典, 改訂新版世界大百科
事典, ブリタニカ国際大百科事典小項目事典.)

**[V] The jōruri fusion, c. 1600 — the hinge of the whole history.** From kotobank.jp/word/
人形浄瑠璃 (read 2026-11-13), which collates デジタル大辞泉, 精選版日本国語大辞典, 日本大百科全書,
ブリタニカ, 山川日本史小辞典 and 改訂新版世界大百科事典:

- Dating of 人形浄瑠璃's emergence varies by dictionary: **Keichō (1596–1615)** per 大辞泉,
  **Bunroku–Keichō (1592–1615)** per 日国, **around 1590** per ブリタニカ.
- The mechanism: puppeteer troupes based at the 百太夫社, a subsidiary shrine of the Nishinomiya
  Ebisu Shrine, known as **夷舁 ebisu-kaki**, toured the country; ブリタニカ holds that 人形浄瑠璃
  arose when these Nishinomiya puppeteers brought **jōruri and the shamisen together**.
- 日本大百科全書 names as the traditional originators **目貫屋長三郎 Meganeya Chōzaburō**, a pupil of
  Sawazumi Kengyō, together with the Nishinomiya ebisu-kaki **引田淡路掾 Hikita Awaji-no-jō** — while
  noting rival names (監物, 次郎兵衛, pupils of 滝野勾当) and that nothing is settled.
- 日本大百科全書 states plainly that it was the **joining of jōruri to the shamisen** that
  established jōruri as a new musical form in its own right.
- **薩摩浄雲 Satsuma Jōun**, of Sawazumi's line, went down to Edo in the **Kan'ei era (1624–44)**;
  his vigorous, martial delivery suited Edo taste and his school produced 桜井和泉太夫, founder of
  **金平節 kinpira-bushi**. **杉山丹後掾 Sugiyama Tango-no-jō** went from Kyoto to Edo at the end of
  Keichō and gave **the first jōruri performance in Edo in 1615 (元和1)**.

**Synthesis for the reference document.** Within roughly fifty years of arriving, the shamisen had
(a) been re-plectrumed and re-skinned into a Japanese instrument, (b) been taken up by the blind
professional musicians' guild, who built the first art repertoire (*kumiuta*) on it and used it to
turn jōruri recitation into a musical genre, and (c) appeared in the pleasure quarters, at court,
and in the hands of riverbed outcasts. Every one of the named individuals in that story — Nakakōji,
Ishimura, Torazawa, Yanagawa, Sawazumi — is attested only in sources written **60 to 200 years
later**, and the Japanese reference literature marks all of them as tradition. The *institution*
(blind guild musicians in Kyoto and Sakai, c. 1570–1640) is solid. The *individuals* are not.

## 2. Edo-period development (17th–19th c.)

### 2.1 The shape of the thing

Across the Edo period the shamisen became the central instrument of urban commoner music in a way
no other Japanese instrument did: it was simultaneously the pit instrument of **kabuki**, the
narrative engine of **bunraku**, the chamber instrument of the Kamigata **jiuta** tradition, the
professional skill of the **geisha**, the street instrument of itinerant beggar-musicians, and the
amateur accomplishment of the merchant class. That spread is why the genre map (Part 4) is so
dense: the genres are not stylistic variations so much as **different social institutions that
happened to share an instrument**.

Two structural drivers:

1. **Demand from the townspeople.** **[V]** The Japanese Wikipedia article 家元 dates the 家元
   system as we know it to **the mid-Edo period onward**, explicitly because the cultural
   population expanded with the wealthy 町人 class (ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/家元, read 2026-11-13).
   The standard study is **西山松之助『家元の研究』 (Yoshikawa Kōbunkan, 1982)**, cited in that
   article's bibliography (bibliography verified; **[U]** book not read).
2. **Supply from the margins.** The people who taught, performed and transmitted this music were
   disproportionately blind professionals, outcast and itinerant performers, and women in indenture.
   See Part 3, and 2.4 below.

### 2.2 The iemoto (家元) system — and a correction worth making

**[V]** From ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/家元 (read 2026-11-13; the article's footnotes cite 大和滋・
岩淵潤子 eds., 『「旦那」と遊びと日本文化』, PHP研究所, 1996, and 廣田吉崇『近現代における茶の湯
家元の研究』, 慧文社, 2012):

- The word **家元** is first attested in **1757 (宝暦7)**, in **馬場文耕『近世江都著聞集』**. Earlier
  hereditary art-house arrangements exist (the article reaches back to Heian poetic houses and Nara
  gagaku), but the *system* is mid-Edo.
- The mechanism is financial and licensing: the head of a school holds the **sole authority to
  issue certificates (免状)**, and money flows upward — the article's example is a branch issuing a
  certificate and remitting **half the accreditation fee** to the headquarters. Between the iemoto
  and the pupils sit intermediate ranks (**名取り**, 師範).
- Legitimacy is secured by **monopolising the house art through the transmission of secret
  techniques (秘技秘伝)**.
- Criticisms the article records: the system is structurally undemocratic, since those funding and
  staffing the school have little say; and succession generates gift- and inheritance-tax burdens
  that have caused house costumes and transmission documents to be dispersed.

**[V] — the correction.** The same article records a remark by the nagauta musician **松島庄十朗
Matsushima Shōjūrō** that in the performing arts, calling *oneself* an iemoto only became clear-cut
**after the issuing of 「技芸者之証」 under the National Mobilization Law** — i.e. in the late 1930s
to 1940s *(my translation)*. **[U]** I have not traced Matsushima's statement to its original
publication. But it is a useful corrective to the English-language habit of describing the music
guilds as timeless hereditary institutions: the *licensing economy* is mid-Edo, and some of the
**iemoto branding is twentieth-century**.

### 2.3 Censorship and sumptuary pressure — this is what actually shaped the genre map

This is the most under-told part of the English account, and it is well documented.

**[V] The Bungo-bushi affair, 1731–1743 — the single clearest case of censorship creating genres.**
From kotobank.jp/word/豊後節 (read 2026-11-13, collating デジタル大辞泉, 精選版日本国語大辞典,
日本大百科全書, 改訂新版世界大百科事典, ブリタニカ, マイペディア, 旺文社日本史事典 and the old
世界大百科事典):

- **豊後節 Bungo-bushi** was founded by **宮古路豊後掾 Miyakoji Bungo-no-jō (d. 1740)**, a pupil of
  **都太夫一中 Miyako-dayū Itchū** (founder of 一中節). He went independent around **1721 (享保6)**
  as 宮古路国太夫半中; dictionaries differ on the founding date of the style itself (late Kyōhō per
  大辞泉; 1731 per マイペディア).
- He went to Nagoya in **1734 (享保19)** and on to Edo the same year, where the style became a craze.
- **1731 (享保16)** and **1736 (元文1)**: practising it **at home** was prohibited.
- **1739 (元文4)**: it was **banned outright** for corrupting public morals, and performance outside
  certain licensed theatres was strictly forbidden. The stated reason: a wave of **心中 / 情死**
  (lovers' double suicides), and the disorder of relations between men and women, was **blamed on
  Bungo-bushi**.
- Enforcement relaxed from about **1743 (寛保3)**.
- **Its descendants are most of the Edo narrative genres.** 常磐津節 **1747 (延享4)**, founded by the
  pupil 文字太夫; 富本節 **1748 (寛延1)**, by 小文字太夫; 清元節 **1814 (文化11)**, split off by
  延寿太夫. Those three are collectively the **豊後三流**. Also descended: **新内節** (a collective
  name covering the 富士松薩摩掾 and 鶴賀若狭掾 lines) and **宮薗節** (from 宮古路薗八 in Kamigata,
  c. **1762 (宝暦12)**).

Note the orthography: the correct form is **宮薗節**, though **宮園** is also written.

**[V] The Tenpō Reforms, 1841–43.** Two strands, both documented.

*Theatres.* The Japan Arts Council's 文化デジタルライブラリー (www2.ntj.jac.go.jp/dglib/contents/
learn/edc17/jidai/kabuki/index02.html, read 2026-11-13) states that the relocation of the
**江戸三座** was completed **between 天保12 (1841) and 天保14 (1843)**; the three were the
**中村座, 市村座 and 河原崎座**; the destination, **猿若町** in Asakusa, also received the
**人形浄瑠璃** houses. The hardline reformers around **水野忠邦 Mizuno Tadakuni** wanted kabuki
abolished outright; the moderates around **遠山景元 Tōyama Kagemoto** opposed this, and the
compromise — reached when a fire gave the occasion — was exile to the then-suburban Asakusa.
**[U]** The widely repeated figures that over 500 *yose* existed in the Kasei–Tenpō period and that
only **15** were permitted to stay open, and that permitted *yose* content was narrowed to four
didactic categories (神道講釈, 心学講話, 軍事講談, 昔咄) with **shamisen and drums banned inside
storytelling** and **women barred from entering yose**, come from Yahoo! Japan snippets of
history.kaisetsuvoice.com and ittosya.net/kinsan (2026-11-13); I could not verify them against an
institutional source.

*The instrument itself.* **[V]** 『浮世の有様』 (*Ukiyo no arisama*), a contemporaneous chronicle of
edicts, prices, customs and rumour covering **1806–1846**, carries a section headed
**【三味線の禁止】** ("the prohibition of the shamisen") among notices of the fifth month of the
tiger year, i.e. **Tenpō 13 = 1842** (the surrounding list dates a related ordinance to
天保十三年三月). Read in the 『国史叢書』 edition, ed. 矢野太郎, 国史研究会, **1917**, transcribed at
ja.wikisource.org/wiki/浮世の有様/5/分冊2 (read 2026-11-13). The text:

> 「三味線は法師・芸妓・芝居の外は弾くべからず。」
> "The shamisen is not to be played except by [blind] priests, geisha, and the theatre."

> 「非人・乞食・素人に限らず、人家軒下に立つて、宮園・豊後・新内等唄ひ候に、何れも淫・奔情死等の
> 事にして、大に風儀に相拘はり候事故、」
> "Whether outcasts, beggars or amateurs, those who stand under the eaves of houses singing
> Miyazono, Bungo, Shinnai and the like — all of these being matters of lewdness, elopement and
> love-suicide, and gravely affecting public morals —"

> 「斯様の者共の所持せる三味線残らず御取上げに相成り、悉く焼棄てとなる。」
> "the shamisen possessed by such persons were confiscated without exception and all burned."

*(All three my translation.)* The adjacent sections 【芸人の取締】 and 【大坂芸人江戸にて困窮】
describe Osaka performers in Edo being barred from socialising with amateurs and from taking money,
and then falling into destitution unable to get home.

This single passage is worth more to the reference document than a paragraph of generalisation. It
establishes, from a contemporary source: that the shogunate recognised exactly **three licit
categories of shamisen player** (the blind guild musicians, the geisha, and the theatre); that
**street performance of 宮薗・豊後・新内 was the thing being suppressed** — i.e. the *nagashi*
practice that is central to shinnai (Part 4); that the stated grounds were sexual morality and
lovers' suicide, the same grounds as 1739, a century earlier; and that enforcement meant
confiscating and **burning the instruments of the poor**.

**[V] Women performers.** Successive prohibitions, from the beginning:
- **1629 (寛永6)**: the shogunate banned **女歌舞伎, 女舞 and 女浄瑠璃** as disorderly. (Reported
  consistently across Yahoo! Japan results, 2026-11-13; corroborated by kotobank.jp/word/女浄瑠璃,
  which states that women's jōruri was popular in the early Edo period and **banned in the Kan'ei
  era (1624–44)**, and that from mid-Edo the term refers to 女義太夫.) **[U]** I did not read the
  edict text.
- **[V]** The National Diet Library's own exhibition page on 女義太夫 (ndl.go.jp/kaleido/entry/34/3.html,
  read 2026-11-13) states that 女義太夫 — women chanting gidayū-bushi, normally as **素浄瑠璃**
  (recitation without puppets or actors) — gained popularity in **Bunka–Bunsei (1804–30)**, was
  **repeatedly prohibited** on public-morals grounds, and that **36 women gidayū performers were
  arrested during the Tenpō Reforms**, after which activity stalled. It then revived strongly: with
  **竹本京枝** (b. c. 1846) coming up from Nagoya, then **竹本東玉 (1851–1917)** and **竹本綾之助
  (1875–1942)** from Osaka, a 女義太夫 boom took Tokyo in the **1890s**; an 1892 ranking book,
  『桃鵆千種霞：女義太夫品評』, lists **115 women gidayū performers**. The NDL page lists its
  primary sources with call numbers (『風俗画報』, 『万朝報』, 平出鏗二郎『東京風俗志』 下 (富山房,
  1902), etc.) — an unusually well-sourced popular-history page.
- A footnote with modern resonance: the fan clubs known as **どうする連 / 堂摺連** who shouted at the
  climaxes and chased the performers' rickshaws between *yose*, and the 1900 *Yorozu Chōhō* exposé
  series 「娘義太夫荒し　青年の腐敗」 which **printed fans' personal details**. (Same NDL page.)

### 2.4 Social status — stated plainly

The music's transmitters were concentrated in three marginalised populations, and the point should
be made without either romance or euphemism.

**[V] Outcast performers.** The earliest documentary shamisen player in Japan is identified in
『御湯殿上日記』 (1580) as a **河原者** — a riverbed dweller, an outcast-status performer (§1.3). The
1842 edict quoted above names **非人 (hinin, "non-persons") and 乞食 (beggars)** as the people whose
instruments were burned. These are legal status categories, not insults invented later.

**[V] Blind professionals.** The 座頭 of the 1587–92 court diary entries (§1.3); the 検校 who built
the first repertoire (§1.5); the whole 当道座 system (Part 3). The 1842 edict's first licit category,
**法師**, is these men.

**[V] Women in indenture.** From ja.wikipedia.org/wiki/芸者 (read 2026-11-13 — the article carries
its own "insufficient sources / possible original research" banner, so **[U]** on specifics; its
footnotes cite 草間八十雄, 松崎天民『運命の影に』 (1917), 権田保之助『娯楽業者の群』 (1923), and
谷岡優子, 「地方花柳界における〈芸〉と〈色〉」, 関西学院大学先端社会研究所紀要 12 (2015)):

- The 芸者 profession flourished from **the mid-Edo period**, after 太夫遊び declined. In **Kyoto**
  its origin is given as the 茶立女 of the 水茶屋 imitating kabuki with **shamisen and dance**
  *(my translation)* — i.e. the shamisen is constitutive of the profession, not an accessory to it.
- In the Edo period, geisha were frequently called in **to fill the time while clients waited for
  the 遊女 to arrive** — a materially different position from the post-Meiji one.
- Terminology: in Edo-period Kyoto and Osaka **芸者 meant a man** (the 幇間 / 太鼓持ち) and women
  were **芸子**; 芸者 ceased to denote men after Meiji.
- The division of labour: **地方 jikata** play and sing — nagauta and the like, shamisen and
  percussion — and are typically senior geisha who have graduated from **立方 tachikata**, the
  dancers.
- The honest part: the ideal was 「芸は売っても体は売らぬ」 ("we sell art, not our bodies"), but the
  reality from the Edo period on was **年季奉公 indenture against 前借金 (advance debt)**, and the
  pleasure districts were a site of **human trafficking and prostitution** *(my translation)*. Legal
  separation came late and partially: the 1933 Child Abuse Prevention Law classed employing such
  girls to attend on drinkers as abuse, and the 1956 Prostitution Prevention Law hit the
  establishments that had traded on it.

**[V] Low status as a property of the instrument itself.** The 1842 edict's structure — the
shamisen permitted *only* to blind priests, geisha and the theatre, and otherwise confiscated — is
the hardest available evidence that the shogunate regarded the instrument as intrinsically
disreputable. The contrast with the koto, the respectable accomplishment of samurai-class women, is
real but I did not find a single citable source stating it; **[U]**, and it matters again in Part 5,
where it is the likeliest explanation for why the koto was rehabilitated into Meiji music education
and the shamisen was not.

**[U] A Kansei-era austerity note.** A Diamond Online article on the 1789 棄捐令 and Kansei
retrenchment describes an Edo in which 「三味線の音さえ聞こえない」 ("you couldn't even hear a
shamisen") (diamond.jp/articles/-/358617, via snippet 2026-11-13). Journalism, and an evocative
rather than a documentary claim; use only as colour, if at all.

## 5. The 20th century

### 5.1 The Meiji rupture, institutionally

**[V] The decisive event for shamisen music is not a music-education policy but the abolition of the
guild.** The **1871 (明治4) dissolution of the 当道座** removed, at a stroke, the licensing economy,
the rank ladder, the mutual-aid system and the occupational protection that had carried jiuta,
sōkyoku and kokyū music for two and a half centuries (§3.1 of `blind-and-itinerant-musicians.md`). Japanese Wikipedia 地歌 states the
consequence directly: 「1871年（明治4年）の当道座廃止によって、地歌・箏曲・胡弓楽を支えてきた
近世以来の制度的基盤は失われた」 — *the institutional basis that had supported jiuta, sōkyoku and
kokyū music since early modern times was lost* *(my translation)*, and transmission moved outside
the guild system.

**[V] And what replaced it was the iemoto school.** Katō Yasuaki, in 改訂新版世界大百科事典's 盲人
entry *(my translation)*: after the 1871 abolition "**blind people re-organised themselves locally
as iemoto, kumiai and kō**" — and the title 検校 survives to this day as a qualification conferred by
koto organisations such as the **当道音楽会**, held for example by **宮城道雄 Miyagi Michio**. **This
is the single most useful thing to say about Meiji in this section:** the modern hereditary schools
of Japanese music are not an ancient inheritance so much as the **successor structures of a
dismantled disability guild**. Cross-reference `history.md` §2.2: the nagauta musician
Matsushima Shōjūrō's remark that self-designation as *iemoto* in the performing arts only became
clear-cut after the **技芸者之証** certificates issued under the National Mobilization Law, i.e. in
the late 1930s–40s. The chain runs: guild (to 1871) → local associations and schools → wartime state
certification → the postwar iemoto system.

**[V] A second, parallel dissolution with a direct musical consequence.** The same year, **1871**,
saw the dissolution of the **普化宗 Fuke sect**, which had trained shakuhachi players. The Japan Arts
Council's 三曲 page gives the combined effect: with both the tōdōza and the Fuke sect gone, **each
instrument was opened to the general public, and the newly freed shakuhachi players actively sought
ensemble work** — which is why the Edo-period **sangen + koto + kokyū** trio was displaced after
Meiji by **sangen + koto + shakuhachi** (`history.md`, §4.1 of `genres.md`, jiuta). **One decree reshaped the standard
chamber ensemble of Japanese music.**

**[V] Taxation and licensing of performers continued into the modern state.** A Dajōkan notice of
**April 1881 (明治14)**, 『地方税中営業税雑種税の種類及制限』, grouped **遊芸師匠 (teachers of the
accomplishments) and 遊芸稼人 (those who earned a living by them)** together with sumo wrestlers,
actors and *hōkan* under the miscellaneous-taxes heading, and excluded geisha from police control
(quoted in a secondary source, kieta3.hatenablog.com, via Yahoo! Japan 2026-11-13). Performers
required a **遊芸稼ぎ人 licence (鑑札) until around 1945** — a point the rakugo performer
**桂米朝** made in an interview: 「昭和20年まで、芸人は鑑札がなかったら商売できなんだ。昔は、
遊芸稼ぎ人と書いてあった。」 — *until 1945 a performer could not do business without a licence; in
the old days it said "one who earns a living by the accomplishments"* *(my translation)*.
**[U]** These are blog and secondary-source quotations of the documents, not the documents; the
commonly cited "Meiji 5 (1872)" start date for the 遊芸稼人 licence **I could not verify** — the
earliest instrument I found a quotation of is the 1881 notice.

### 5.1b Meiji music-education policy — the real story is better than the cliché

**[V] The committee, with dates** (ja.wikipedia 音楽取調掛, read 2026-11-13): the **音楽取調掛
Music Investigation Committee** was established **7 October 1879** 「音楽教育の調査と研究のため」.
**伊沢修二 Izawa Shūji** was initially 音楽取調御用掛 and became **取調掛長 on 26 October 1881** —
**he was not its head from the start**, as English accounts often imply. **Luther Whiting Mason**
(ルーサー・ホワイティング・メーソン) was invited from the United States by Izawa, **arrived 2 March
1880 and returned home in July 1882**; the page credits him with directing the importation of
Western music and with work on the 唱歌 collection, and **does not describe him as a co-head**. The
body was renamed 音楽取調所 in February 1885, reverted in December 1885, and was reorganised as the
**東京音楽学校 Tokyo Music School on 5 October 1887**.

**[V] 『小學唱歌集』, 1881–84**: 初編 November 1881, 第二編 March 1883, 第三編 1884 — **[U]** the
month of the third volume is contested (the 音楽取調掛 page says June 1884, the 小学唱歌集 page says
29 March 1884). **91 songs** in total, and **Japan's first music textbook in staff notation**
(「日本初の五線譜による音楽教科書」). **59 melodies came from German and American school
collections**, roughly 22 of them hymn-related, under a tenth Scots or Irish folk tunes, and **only a
few were composed by Japanese.**

**[V] — and here is the correction. The committee did not simply choose Western music.** Its declared
policy was the **折衷 (eclectic, blended) option** — 「東西両洋の音楽を折衷して唱歌を作成する方針」 —
chosen from three on the table (Western only / develop indigenous music / blend) *(my translation)*.
Its mandate was to survey **洋楽・雅楽・俗楽・清楽** and **transcribe all of it into staff notation**
(「すべて五線譜に採譜する方針」). One stated research purpose was to allay the fear that importing
Western music would destroy Japanese music, by showing that the two were not different in kind.
**So "Izawa and Mason chose the West over hōgaku" is too strong. The anti-hōgaku outcome is real,
but it is an outcome of selective follow-through, not of a stated exclusion.**

**[V] The instrument decision, verbatim** (ja.wikipedia 音楽取調掛, §概要):
> 「そして、日本音楽の教育には箏が、西洋音楽の教育にはオルガンがそれぞれ適しているとの結論に
> 達した。」
> — "And they concluded that the **koto** was suited to teaching Japanese music, and the **organ** to
> teaching Western music." *(my translation)*

**三味線 and 尺八 are not mentioned anywhere on that page.**

**[V] The "pleasure-quarters" claim — partly verified, and in a far more usable form than the English
version.** The committee's two criticisms of Japanese music were that it **has no harmony**
(「日本音楽は和声を持たないこと」) and that its **texts are lewd** — and on the second ground it
proposed reforming *both* koto music **and a shamisen genre**:
> 「詞章が淫媚なものが多いのでそれを改良すべきとし、箏曲、**長唄**の改良を提唱した。」
> 「このうち箏曲については実際に改良が行われた。」
> — "Holding that many of the texts were lascivious and should be reformed, they advocated the
> reform of *sōkyoku* **and *nagauta***. Of these, reform was actually carried out for *sōkyoku*."
> *(my translation)*

**This is the precise finding.** The lewd-lyrics objection is documented and is not an English
invention; **nagauta was explicitly nominated for "improvement" and then dropped.** What the record
shows is **selective follow-through, not categorical ineligibility**. The committee published
**『箏曲集』 (文部省編輯局, October 1888)** — a koto repertory book — and **no shamisen equivalent**.
Corroboration from ja.wikipedia 邦楽: modern hōgaku education 「1887年（明治20年）に設立された
東京音楽学校の師範科で箏曲が取り入れられたことに始まる」 — *begins with koto music being taken into
the Tokyo Music School's teacher-training course in 1887.*
**[U]** No source I read states the brothel association as the stated reason for dropping nagauta.
**Report the strong English version ("the shamisen's pleasure-quarter association made it ineligible
for rehabilitation") as unsourced.** What *is* fair to say is that the late-Edo state had already
classified the instrument institutionally as disreputable — the Tenpō 13 (1842) edict restricting
the shamisen to blind priests, geisha and the theatre (`history.md`, §2.3) — so the Meiji outcome is
continuous with documented earlier policy even if its stated grounds were textual rather than social.

**[V] The decisive datable asymmetry — this is the hard evidence for the differential fate, and it
is arithmetic on verified dates:**

| | Tokyo Music School 講師 | 教授 | Note |
|---|---|---|---|
| **宮城道雄 Miyagi Michio (koto)** | **1930** | **1937** | 新日本音楽: the name 「新日本音楽大演奏会」 was coined by 吉田晴風 at a joint recital with 本居長世 at Tokyo's 有楽座, **November 1920** |
| **山田抄太郎 Yamada Shōtarō (長唄三味線方, 5 Mar 1899 – 8 Jun 1970)** | **1941** | later (year not stated); **邦楽科 professor at 東京藝術大学 on the school's elevation to university status in 1950** | **人間国宝 1955** |

**So: the koto got a lecturer at the national music school in 1930 and a professor in 1937; the
shamisen got a lecturer only in 1941 and a nagauta professorship in 1950 — a ten-to-thirteen-year
lag, and 63 years after the school was founded.** (ja.wikipedia 宮城道雄 and 山田抄太郎, read
2026-11-13. The inference is mine; neither page frames it this way.) **[V]** The framing is
independently visible in the title of the standard English article on the subject: **William P. Malm,
"Yamada Shotaro: Japan's First Shamisen Professor," *Asian Music* (1998), DOI 10.2307/834263**
(verified via Crossref; **[U]** text behind JSTOR and unread). A scholarly English source on the
committee also exists: **Berger, "Isawa Shuji and Luther Whiting Mason: Pioneers of Music Education
in Japan," *Music Educators Journal*, October 1987, DOI 10.2307/3401254** (**[V]** citation,
**[U]** contents). One useful detail from the Miyagi page: **he taught koto using staff notation and
wrote beginners' primers for both koto *and shamisen*** — so the shamisen was not wholly absent from
the modernising project, only from its institutional apex.

### 5.2 The preservation apparatus

**[V] The legal framework, with the dates corrected.** The **文化財保護法 Law for the Protection of
Cultural Properties** took effect in **1950**, but it was the **1954 amendment** that created the
designation-plus-holder-certification system, and the **first designations and certifications were
made on 15 February 1955**. The **保持団体認定** route (certification of a holding *body*) was added
by the **1975 amendment**. (ja.wikipedia 人間国宝, read 2026-11-13.)

**[V] 「人間国宝」 is journalistic, not legal — confirmed from the Agency for Cultural Affairs
itself.** The legal term is **重要無形文化財の保持者**; ja.wikipedia states 「「人間国宝」は通称で
あるが」 and that the Act 「「人間国宝」という文言はない」 — *contains no such wording* — and that the
nickname attaches specifically to **各個認定** (individual) holders. **文化庁's own page**
(bunka.go.jp/seisaku/bunkazai/shokai/mukei/, read 2026-11-13) introduces the term only as
「いわゆる「人間国宝」」, *the so-called "Living National Treasure,"* and never presents it as legal.

certification routes **各個認定 / 総合認定 / 保持団体認定**, and gives the individual holder's
special grant as **¥2,000,000 per year**.

**[V] The designation that is *not* a Living National Treasure, and why it matters here.** The
parallel and lesser instrument is **記録作成等の措置を講ずべき無形文化財** (also **選択無形文化財**),
defined as intangible cultural properties **other than** Important ones, selected by the Commissioner
of the Agency for Cultural Affairs under art. 77(1), for which public funds may meet part of the cost
of **recording, preservation and publication**; the criterion for performing arts is being "valuable
for knowing the process of change in our country's performing arts," and **56 items have been
selected in the 音楽 category**. **瞽女唄 goze-uta is one of them (1978).** The contrast is the
cleanest statement of twentieth-century Japanese cultural policy toward shamisen music: **the
theatre genres with institutional homes got transmission support; the itinerant and disability
traditions got tape recorders.** (§3.3 of `blind-and-itinerant-musicians.md`.)

**[V] Bunraku is the best-documented case of the apparatus at work**, and the dates make a clean
sequence (Japanese Wikipedia 文楽, oldid 111115838, read 2026-11-13; the article's footnotes for the
1963 and 1984 items cite the Osaka Arts Council and the Japan Creator Support Fund):
- **1955** — 文楽 designated an **重要無形文化財** under the 1950 law.
- **1963 (昭和38)** — the **財団法人文楽協会 Bunraku Association** established by the national
  government, Osaka Prefecture, Osaka City, **NHK** and the Kansai business community, unifying the
  troupe. *(The split it healed — the 因会/三和会 rupture — is outside what I verified.)*
- **1 November 1966** — the **国立劇場 National Theatre** opens in Tokyo (Japanese Wikipedia 国立劇場:
  「11月1日に国立劇場本館が開場した」), with bunraku among its programmed arts.
- **1984 (昭和59)** — the **国立文楽劇場 National Bunraku Theatre** opens at Nihonbashi, Osaka.
- **2003** — 「人形浄瑠璃文楽」 proclaimed a **UNESCO Masterpiece of the Oral and Intangible Heritage
  of Humanity**; inscribed on the Representative List in **2008**, with first registration under the
  Convention in **2009**.

(www.ntj.jac.go.jp/training.html, read 2026-11-13). The courses relevant here:
- **歌舞伎音楽 (kabuki music): 竹本, 鳴物, and 長唄** — i.e. the state trains kabuki narrative
  chanters, percussionists and nagauta singers/shamisen players.
- **文楽 (bunraku): 太夫, 三味線, 人形遣い** — a dedicated **bunraku shamisen** course.
- **組踊 (at 国立劇場おきなわ): 立方 and 地方**, the latter being the instrumentalists.
- Plus 歌舞伎俳優, 大衆芸能 (寄席囃子, 太神楽), and 能楽 (ワキ方, 狂言方, 囃子方).

- **1902** — the **研精会 Kenseikai** founded, making nagauta independent of kabuki as pure listening

  童謡研究会 ed. 『日本民謡大全』 (1909), the Ministry of Education's 『俚謡集』 (1914).

  (kotobank 民謡). Groemer's 1994 *Asian Folklore Studies* article "Fifteen Years of Folk Song

  10.2307/1178643) is the English-language account of what it produced.
- **December 2024** — **2代目京山幸枝若** becomes the **first Living National Treasure in 浪曲語り**.

**The synthesis, stated carefully.** The traditional genres' transmission runs through the iemoto
schools — themselves the successor structures of the dismantled 当道座 (§5.1) — and, since the 1970s,
through a small state apprenticeship pipeline; their original social settings are gone or vestigial,
their apex designations are going vacant, their umbrella bodies publish no numbers and in one case
admit decline, and their flagship venue is shut. Tsugaru-shamisen went the other way, and the reason
is institutional rather than aesthetic: **it has no iemoto hierarchy to shrink**, it became a solo
concert and recording form in the mid-1950s to 1960s (ニッポニカ dates the breakthrough 曲弾き shows
to around 1956 and the general adoption of the name 「津軽三味線」 to the same moment), its first solo
LP was a hit in 1963, and it entered the market through records, television and the folk-song boom
rather than through a licensing hierarchy. **So the asymmetry is not simply "traditional down, Tsugaru
up." It is that tsugaru-jamisen is the one shamisen genre whose twentieth-century institutional form
was built by the entertainment industry rather than inherited from the Edo guild system — which is
why it is the one that could grow.** *(Verified components as cited; the synthesis is mine.)*

## 6. The naming question

Short version, before the evidence: **shamisen** is the general and the modern standard form;
**samisen** is an older reading, not a regional one, and it survives as the OED's headword;
**sangen** is the in-house term of the Kamigata chamber tradition; **jamisen** probably does not need
rendaku to explain it, because there is an independently attested 蛇味線 *jamisen* in the record that
is five years *older* than the first attestation of *shamisen*; and **no Japanese reference work
calls 三味線 ateji**, though the standard etymology makes it one in substance.

### 6.1 shamisen vs samisen — and a correction to the standard English claim

**[V] Dictionary status** (kotobank.jp/word/三味線, read 2026-11-13; raw page also fetched and the
dictionary blocks extracted directly):
- **デジタル大辞泉**: main headword **しゃみ‐せん【三味線】**; **さみ‐せん【▽三味線】** exists only as
  a cross-reference pointing back ("⇒しゃみせん"). The alternates given under definition 1 are
  「三弦。さみせん。」
- **精選版 日本国語大辞典**: **both** しゃみ‐せん and さみ‐せん are headwords, the latter reading
  simply 「＝しゃみせん（三味線）」. Its alternate-name list under しゃみせん is
  「**さみせん。三弦。しゃみ。さみ。ぺんぺん。**」 — note the colloquial **ぺんぺん**.
- **改訂新版 世界大百科事典**: 「**〈さみせん〉とも発音し，三絃，三弦と書かれることが多い。**」 —
  *also pronounced samisen; it is often written 三絃 or 三弦* *(my translation)*.

**[V] The dated attestations, and they cut against the usual story.** 精選版日本国語大辞典's
初出の実例, read verbatim:
- **しゃみせん, 1580**: 「御かはらの物、山しろといふ、**しやみせん**ひかせらるる」 — 『御湯殿上日記』,
  天正8年2月16日.
- **さみせん, 1632**: 「小うたにのせては**さみせん**をひく、平家に合てびわを引」 — 仮名草子
  『尤双紙』 (1632), 下.

**So in the standard historical dictionary, しゃみせん is attested 52 years earlier than さみせん —
and the 1580 attestation is from the diary of the women of the Kyoto imperial court, i.e. a Kamigata
source.** That is direct counter-evidence to "samisen is the Kamigata / western-Japan form." (This is
the same 1580 entry that anchors `history.md` §1.3; the two strands of research converged on it
independently.)

**[V] What the Japanese sources actually label it.** ja.wiktionary 三味線 gives both readings with
平板型 [0] accent — しゃみせん [ɕa̠mʲisẽ̞ɴ], さみせん [sa̠mʲisẽ̞ɴ] — with the note
「**「さみせん」はやや古い読み**」, *samisen is a somewhat old reading*, and **attaches no regional
label**. Its synonym list: 三弦, 三絃, 三つの緒, 三線（さんしん）.

**[V] VERDICT — the Kamigata/western-Japan attribution is unsupported and probably wrong.** None of
精選版日本国語大辞典, デジタル大辞泉, 改訂新版世界大百科事典, 日本大百科全書 or ja.wiktionary
attaches any regional label to さみせん; the only label any of them gives is "somewhat old." The
English claim traces to **en.wikipedia's Shamisen article**, where the sentence "In Western Japanese
dialects and several Edo period sources, it is both written and pronounced as samisen" carries **no
citation at all** (checked 2026-11-13). **Keep the defensible half — *samisen* is the older
Edo-period and literary reading — and drop the regional attribution, or mark it explicitly as a
repeated-but-unsourced claim.**

**[V] Older English-language scholarship — strongly verified for Piggott, and it is a lovely
exhibit.** I downloaded and searched the full text of **Francis Taylor Piggott, *The Music and
Musical Instruments of Japan*** (archive.org `musicmusicalinst00pigg`, the New York Public Library
copy of the **1909** edition; the 1893 edition is also held, as `musicandmusical00soutgoog` and
`musicmusicalinst00pigg2`):
- **"Samisen" occurs 57 times; "shamisen" occurs 0 times.**
- His etymology, verbatim: "Yamahashi Kengyo is regarded as the father of modern **Samisen**
  players: he gave the instrument the name **San sen—'three strings,'** which was afterwards
  **converted into the three-character word Sa-mi-sen, or 'three tasteful strings.'**"
  *(Note the mangled name — "Yamahashi Kengyo" is presumably 柳川検校 or 八橋検校 — and note that
  Piggott, taking 味 at face value, has produced "three tasteful strings." That is a 19th-century
  Englishman reading semantic content* into *a phonetic spelling, which is itself evidence that the
  spelling is phonetic.)*
- His chapter heading is "**SAMISENS, FIDDLES, AND STRINGED INSTRUMENTS WITHOUT FRETS**," and the
  index has "THE JAMISEN … 145" as a separate entry (see §6.4).

**[U] Malm's own spelling practice could not be verified.** archive.org holds *Japanese Music and
Musical Instruments* (1959) and *Traditional Japanese Music and Musical Instruments* (2000), but all
copies are **lending-restricted with no full text** and the search-inside API did not respond.
**Do not assert that his 1959 work used "samisen" and his 2000 revision "shamisen."** Two indirect
data points: his 1998 *Asian Music* article is titled "Yamada Shotaro: Japan's First **Shamisen**
Professor," and his Grove article (with Hughes) is headed "**Shamisen**" — so **by the late 1990s
Malm was writing *shamisen*** (Crossref metadata, 2026-11-13).

**[V] Register today — a clean split between lexicography and musicology.**
- **The OED headword is "samisen, n."** (DOI 10.1093/oed/5501008893, dated 2 March 2023; resolves to
  oed.com/dictionary/samisen_n). **[U]** The entry body is paywalled, so the variant list and first
  quotation are unverified.
- **Grove Music Online's headword is "Shamisen"** (DOI 10.1093/gmo/9781561592630.article.25576, 2001;
  plus "Japanese Instruments and instrumental genres: 'Shamisen'" by Malm & Hughes). **[U]** body
  paywalled.
- **So: lexicography still lemmatises *samisen*; musicology has standardised on *shamisen*.**
- **Live Japanese institutional romanisation is split**: the Bunraku Association romanises its own
  section as **bunraku.or.jp/samisen/** while the Hirosaki competition's domain is
  **tsugaru-shamisen.jp**.

**Practical recommendation for the reference document:** use **shamisen** throughout; mention
*samisen* once, as the older reading still used in English lexicography and in some Japanese
institutional romanisation; do not call it a western-Japan form.

### 6.2 sangen 三絃 / 三弦 — fully verified

- **[V] 日本大百科全書 (ニッポニカ), signed 藤田隆則**: 「中国の**三絃**(さんげん)に由来する楽器で…
  また三絃をそのまま音読みして「**さんげん**」とよぶことも一般化している。」 — *an instrument
  deriving from the Chinese 三絃; and reading 三絃 straight off in its Sino-Japanese reading as
  "sangen" has also become common* *(my translation)*.
- **[V] デジタル大辞泉** (kotobank.jp/word/三弦): 三弦 is another name for the shamisen, and
  **「地歌・箏曲では正称とされる」** — *in jiuta and koto music it is held to be the proper name.*
- **[V] ブリタニカ国際大百科事典**: in 地歌 and 箏曲, **三弦 is often what is written on concert
  programmes.**
- **[V] 山川日本史小辞典**: the jiuta tradition uses **三弦（さんげん）** for the instrument.
- **[V] 改訂新版世界大百科事典**: 「三絃，三弦と書かれることが多い。」
- **[V] 精選版日本国語大辞典** dates it: the **earliest attestation of 三弦 meaning the shamisen is a
  1678 評判記.**
- **[V] Living institutional usage is the clincher.** The 公益社団法人 **日本三曲協会** (Japan
  Sankyoku Association), sankyoku.jp/about.php: its formal purpose statement and chairman's greeting
  use **三絃 exclusively** — 「箏、三絃及び尺八」 and 「箏・三絃・尺八・胡弓」 — while **三味線 appears
  only in the page header and navigation labels**. The home page glosses the instrument as
  **「箏・三絃（三味線）・尺八」**, i.e. **三絃 as the term, with 三味線 as the parenthetical gloss for
  outsiders.**

**Conclusion, verified:** **三絃 / 三弦 (sangen) is the in-house and more formal/literary term of the
jiuta–sōkyoku–sankyoku (Kamigata chamber) world; 三味線 is the general-public word.** The two graphic
variants coexist, with **三絃** the more literary (and the association's own choice) and **三弦** the
commoner. This is also why `genres.md` Part 4 notes that the Japan Arts Council files 地歌 under
三曲 rather than under 三味線音楽: the chamber tradition does not think of itself as "shamisen music."

### 6.3 Rendaku, and which reading of 津軽三味線 is standard

**[V] Rendaku itself** (ja.wikipedia 連濁): 連濁 is the change by which 「後ろの語（後部要素）の語頭の
清音が濁音に変化する」 — a voiceless initial on the second element of a compound becomes voiced. It
applies **mainly to 和語**, is **less common in 漢語 and rare in 外来語**. **Lyman's Law**
(ライマンの法則), published by **B. S. Lyman in 1894** and found independently by 賀茂真淵 and
本居宣長 in the eighteenth century: 「複合語の後部要素にもとから濁音が含まれている場合、連濁は
起こらない」 — no rendaku if the second element already contains a voiced obstruent. The し→じ
examples the article gives are 盛塩 もりじお, 引っ込み思案 ひっこみじあん, 中島 なかじま, 金色
こんじき, 用心 ようじん.
**[V] But the article does not mention 三味線 or じゃみせん anywhere.** So "tsugaru-jamisen is
rendaku" is a plausible analysis that is **not attested in the standard Japanese description of
rendaku**. And note the friction: **三味線 is a 漢語-shaped word, i.e. in exactly the class where
rendaku is least expected** — which makes the alternative in §6.4 more attractive, not less.

**[V] Which reading is standard — a clear answer.** ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 opens:
「**津軽三味線（つがるしゃみせん、つがるじゃみせん）は、津軽地方（現在の青森県西部）で成立した
三味線音楽。**」 — **しゃみせん first, じゃみせん second** — and the article **never says which is
local, nor discusses 連濁**. The official competition's domain is **tsugaru-shamisen.jp** and its
Japanese pages use 津軽三味線 without furigana. By contrast **English Wikipedia's article is titled
"Tsugaru-jamisen"** and leads with that form (uncited).
**Verdict: both occur; つがるしゃみせん is the form Japanese reference and institutional sources put
first, while つがるじゃみせん is the form English-language writing has standardised on.** The English
preference is most likely an artefact of English usage — and specifically of Groemer's *The Spirit of
Tsugaru* (1999), which ja.wikipedia 津軽三味線 cites — rather than of Japanese practice. *(The usage
pattern is verified; the causal claim about Groemer is inference.)*

**[V] A note on 太棹, in case it gets used as a rendaku example.** 太棹 is **ふとざお**, not
ぶとざお — the first element does not voice. What *does* show rendaku in these words is the **second**
element: 棹 さお → ざお in 太棹 / 中棹 / 細棹. Use it that way or not at all.
**[V] And while we are here, the neck list from the same ニッポニカ entry (藤田隆則), verbatim**:
「太棹は**義太夫節**、**津軽三味線**、**浪曲**（浪花節）に、中棹は常磐津節、清元節、新内節、地歌
などに、細棹は長唄、荻江節、小唄などに用いられる」 — with the caveat that the three-way split is
「非常に大まかなもの」, *very rough*, and that classifying by genre and school is more accurate.
(Cross-reference the reconciled table in `genres.md` Part 4.)

### 6.4 The characters 三味線 — is it ateji?

**[V] What the Japanese reference works actually say — the etymology is phonetic, and route (a)
is the consensus.**
- **日本大百科全書 (ニッポニカ), 藤田隆則**, verbatim: 「「**しゃみせん**」という呼称は、中国の
  「**三絃**」の発音が日本に入って**転訛**(てんか)したものと考えられる。」 — *"The name shamisen is
  thought to be a corruption, on entering Japanese, of the pronunciation of the Chinese 三絃."*
  *(my translation.)*
- **改訂新版世界大百科事典**: 「その祖型は，中国の三弦であると考えられている。」
- **精選版日本国語大辞典**: 「永祿年間（一五五八‐七〇）琉球の**蛇皮(じゃび)線**が大坂の堺に輸入され、
  **琵琶法師によって改造された**という。」
- **日本大百科全書**: the Eiroku-era Ryukyu→Sakai route is 「現在もっとも有力である」, *currently the
  strongest theory.*

**So the derivation from Chinese 三絃 *sanxian* by way of a Ryukyuan/Chinese pronunciation is
verified Japanese scholarly consensus.** And the 1603–04 日葡辞書 headword **Xamixen** (`history.md`
§1.3) fixes the pronunciation at that date as already three syllables and already distinct from
*sangen*.

**[V] Route (b) — a reading borrowed from the Buddhist term 三昧 sanmai/zanmai — has ZERO support.**
三昧 is **not mentioned** in any of the twelve dictionary blocks on kotobank's 三味線 page
(デジタル大辞泉, 精選版日本国語大辞典, 日本大百科全書, 世界大百科事典, マイペディア, ブリタニカ,
音楽用語ダス, 山川日本史小辞典, 旺文社日本史事典, 日本文化いろは事典 and others), nor on weblio
(which aggregates デジタル大辞泉, ja.wiktionary and 隠語大辞典), nor on ja.wikipedia 三味線.
**Do not repeat the 三昧 story.** (Also: 語源由来辞典 gogen-yurai.jp **has no 三味線 entry at all** —
both /shamisen/ and /samisen/ return 404.)

**[V] / [U] Is 三味線 ateji? — defensible as analysis, not available as a sourced Japanese claim.**
**No Japanese reference work I read uses the word 当て字 of 三味線** — checked across all twelve
kotobank dictionary blocks, weblio, ja.wiktionary and ja.wikipedia 三味線. What they say instead is
that the word is a **転訛** of the Chinese reading of 三絃, and that **三絃/三弦 are the forms "often
written."** Given that the standard etymology is phonetic and that **味 contributes no sense
whatever**, calling 三味線 a phonetic spelling is sound analysis — **but present it as analysis, not
as a sourced Japanese claim.** The strongest supporting exhibit is Piggott's 1909 gloss "three
tasteful strings" (§6.1): a reader taking 味 at face value produces nonsense, which is what a
phonetic spelling does to anyone who reads it semantically.

**[V] — AND HERE IS THE LOAD-BEARING FINDING, which answers the *jamisen* question without rendaku.**
**蛇皮線 and 蛇味線 *jabisen / jamisen* are independently attested Japanese names for the snakeskin
instrument, and the second of them uses the same phonetic 味.**
- **ja.wikipedia 三線**: the unified name 「**三線（サンシン）**」 is in wide use in Okinawa; the
  **mainland** names are given verbatim as 「**ジャビセン（蛇皮線）**」「**ジャミセン（蛇味線）**」,
  used to distinguish the snakeskin instrument from the cat/dog-skin 三味線, and
  「ただし、この呼称は沖縄では殆ど使われない」 — *these names are hardly used in Okinawa itself.*
  **Note 蛇味線: the same 味.**
- **kotobank 蛇皮線**: headword **じゃび‐せん**; 精選版日本国語大辞典 lists the variants
  「**じゃみせん。じゃひ。**」 and defines it as 「沖縄の代表的な弦楽器三線(さんしん)の俗称」, arriving
  from China's 三弦子 in the late fourteenth century. **Earliest citation: 「琉球人しやひせんとも
  曳候て」, 『上井覚兼日記』, 1575.** デジタル大辞泉: 「**室町末に本土に伝わり、改造されて三味線と
  なる。**」 世界大百科事典 treats it as a general term for snakeskin-both-sides plucked lutes
  including the Chinese 三弦, used on the mainland specifically for the Okinawan/Amami 三線.
- **[V] So しやひせん / じゃびせん is attested in a Japanese diary in 1575 — five years before the
  first attestation of しやみせん (1580).**
- **[V] Piggott (1909) treats "Jamisen" as a separate instrument**: a Chinese snakeskin lute with an
  oval hardwood body 6 × 5 inches, snakeskin front and back, a neck two inches shorter than the
  samisen's, played with a small tortoiseshell plectrum and tuned to honchōshi; he explicitly
  contrasts it with the samisen's hollow rectangular frame and says the two "both sprang from the
  same source." He also lists a "COREAN SAMISEN: almost identical with the Jamisen."

**Conclusion for §6.4, and I think this is the most interesting single thing in Part 6: the *jamisen*
in *tsugaru-jamisen* does not need rendaku to explain it.** There is a pre-existing **じゃみせん**,
written **蛇味線** with the same phonetic 味, sitting in the Japanese record — and the
しやひせん/じゃびせん family is attested from **1575**, earlier than しやみせん itself. **Rendaku and
the 蛇味線 route are two live explanations.** The standard Japanese description of rendaku does not
cite 三味線, and 三味線 sits in the 漢語-shaped class where rendaku is least expected. **Present both;
do not assert rendaku as settled.**

**[U] One further wrinkle to be aware of:** ja.wikipedia 三線 flags, with 要出典, a popular but
unverified claim that **三味線 was shortened to 三線 in the Meiji period** — i.e. folk etymology
running the other way. Ignore it, but know it is out there.

### 6.5 三つの緒 mitsu-no-o — verified, with dated citations

**[V]** ja.wikipedia 三味線 gives 「みつのお（**三つの緒**）」 as the **雅称**, the elegant or poetic
name; ja.wiktionary lists it among the synonyms. **精選版日本国語大辞典** (kotobank.jp/word/三つの緒)
gives two senses, each with a dated example:
1. A Chinese three-stringed instrument (also 三弦子 さんげんす, 三弦). Citation:
   「しらべ合せし**三つの緒**を」 — 浄瑠璃 『聖徳太子絵伝記』 (**1717**), 三.
2. **The shamisen** (glossed 三弦). Citation: 「調子合せて**みつの緒**の〈略〉連れ三味線の手もたゆく」
   — 浄瑠璃 『津国女夫池』 (**1721**), 二.

**So 三つの緒 is a genuine literary name for the shamisen, attested in jōruri texts of 1717 and
1721.** Use it as the poetic register, not as a historical name.

### 6.7 Claims to fix in Part 6

1. **"Samisen is the Kamigata / western-Japan form."** Unsupported by any Japanese dictionary, and
   contradicted by the fact that the earliest attestation of しゃみせん (1580) is from a Kyoto court
   diary, 52 years before the earliest さみせん (1632). The English claim traces to an uncited
   sentence in en.wikipedia. **Say "older reading," not "western form."**
2. **"三味線 is ateji for 三昧."** No support whatever in ~15 Japanese dictionary and encyclopedia
   entries. **Drop it.**
3. **"三味線 is ateji" tout court.** True in substance — the standard etymology is a 転訛 of the
   Chinese reading of 三絃 and 味 carries no sense — but **no Japanese reference work applies the
   label 当て字**. Present it as your analysis.
4. **"Tsugaru-jamisen shows rendaku."** Possible, but the standard Japanese account of rendaku does
   not cite the word, 三味線 is 漢語-shaped where rendaku is least expected, and there is an
   independently attested 蛇味線 *jamisen* from 1575. **Give both explanations.**
5. **"Malm's 1959 book says samisen and his 2000 revision says shamisen."** Could not be verified —
   the archive.org copies are lending-restricted. **Do not assert.** What is verified is that by the
   late 1990s Malm was writing *shamisen*.
6. **"The Yoshida Brothers' debut sold 100,000+ copies and sparked a shamisen boom."** Unsourced as
   far as could be verified; only the **1999 debut with 『いぶき』** is confirmed (§5.3f).
