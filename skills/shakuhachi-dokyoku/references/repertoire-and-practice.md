### 6.2 松巖軒鈴慕 Shōganken Reibo — the piece Watazumi called 風林, renamed in the mid-1980s

`[V]` From Lee 1992 (read 2026-11-13), citing Yokoyama by personal communication (1989). This
is the most precisely documented transmission event in the repertoire and it is **absent from
every English account I have seen**:

1. Watazumi played a piece he called **風林 *Fūrin*** ("wind forest"), and said **no one taught
   it to him** (Yokoyama 1989, in Lee).
2. Lee's preliminary analysis showed 風林 as played by Watazumi to be "**quite similar to
   'Shôganken reibo' of Jin's lineage**" — i.e. the lineage of **Jin Nyodō 神如道**.
3. **Yokoyama learned 風林 from Watazumi.**
4. **"Yokoyama further stated in a personal communication with me (1989) that in the mid 1980s
   he began using the title 'Shôganken reibo' for the piece Watazumi played as 'Furin' because
   he recognized both pieces to be the same."** `[V]`
5. Lee notes a loose end: "It is not clear why Yokoyama does not, in his discussion of
   'Shôganken reibo' in his book, precisely point out that his own 'Shôganken reibo' is
   basically a variant of Watazumi's 'Furin'." He conjectures Yokoyama adopted the name because
   it is "much more famous than the title 'Furin'."

**Consequences for the practitioner.** The 松巖軒鈴慕 in Volume 3 is, by Yokoyama's own account,
**Watazumi's 風林 under a borrowed name**, and it is a variant of Jin Nyodō's 松巌軒鈴慕 rather
than a direct inheritance of it. `[V]` **風林 is track 1 of Watazumi's CD 「即音乱調・霊慕」** in
the KSK's own shop — so the two titles sit in the same catalogue, four pieces apart, and the
shop does not say they are the same piece. `[V]` Lee also reads Watazumi's renaming as the
mechanism of the "no teachers" position: "In this assertion [no teachers, no lineage, no problem
of authenticity] may be found the explanation of Watazumi calling what appears to be a close
variant of Jin's 'Shôganken reibo' by the unique name 'Furin'."

### 6.3 The 鈴慕 *Reibo* family, and why "the several Sanya" is the wrong question to ask

`[V]` The *Reibo* problem is much larger than the *Sanya* problem, and Lee 1992 documents it
(read 2026-11-13). **Reibo 鈴慕 means "yearning for the bell"** — it is the source of Lee's
thesis title — and the titles 鈴慕 *Reibo*, 鈴法 *Reihō*, 恋慕 *Renbo* and 臨門 *Rinmon* are
variants of one another. Lee reports three overlapping enumerations:

- **Tukitani (1990b:11) gives fifteen representative *Reibo* pieces**: 九州鈴慕 Kyūshū Reibo;
  京鈴慕 Kyo Reibo; 紫鈴慕 / 紫野鈴慕 Murasaki(no) Reibo; 伊豆鈴慕 Izu Reibo; 葦草鈴慕 Igusa
  Reibo; 越後鈴慕 / 北国鈴慕 Echigo (Kitaguni) Reibo; 奥州鈴慕 Ōshū Reibo (= plain 鈴慕);
  宮城鈴慕 Miyagi Reibo; 陸奥鈴慕 Mutsu Reibo; 波間鈴慕 Namiwa Reibo; 手解鈴慕 Tehodoki Reibo;
  真跡鈴慕 Shiseki Reibo; 霧海篪鈴慕 Mukaiji Reibo; 虚空鈴慕 Kokū Reibo; 巣鶴鈴慕 Sōkaku Reibo.
  `[V]`
- **Takahashi (1979:269–306) lists nineteen** under the 鈴法 *Reihō* / 恋慕 / 臨門 spellings.
  `[V]`
- **Chikuhō-ryū alone has eleven.** `[V]`

`[V]` Lee's explanation of the variant names is the useful part: pieces had two performance
contexts, "a strictly ceremonial context and the loosely prescribed context of pilgrimage", and
the spellings may track them — **礼法 *Reihō* (manner + ritual) for the ceremonial**, **恋慕
*Renbo* ("intense yearning or longing") for the informal**, with **鈴慕 "yearning for the bell"
readable either way**.

**On "九州鈴慕 Kyūshū Reibo":** `[V]` it is a real and
standard title in the wider *Reibo* family (Tukitani's list, Takahashi's list, and the
Chikuhō-ryū repertoire all contain it) — **but it is not in the KSK's published three volumes**.
`[U]` Whether it is taught in the lineage informally I could not verify.

`[V]` Similarly for **Sanya**: the KSK transmits **三谷 *Sanya*** (Vol 1 #2) and **山谷
*Yamaya*** (Vol 2 #16) as two separate pieces, and a third related title **三谷菅垣 *Sanya
Sugagaki*** appears on the CD 「三界輪転」 `[V]`. **The "several Sanya" in this lineage are:
三谷, 山谷, and 三谷菅垣.**

### 6.4 The 古伝三曲 question

`[V]` Lee 1992 (read 2026-11-13) identifies **虚霊 or 虚鈴 *Kyorei* "Empty Bell", 虚空 *Kokū*
"Empty Sky", and 霧海篪 *Mukaiji* "Flute in a Misty Sea"** as "**the three most venerated
pieces in the koten honkyoku repertoire**", citing Tukitani (1990b:51) on the distinction
between austere, formal temple pieces (of which these are the examples) and the more elaborate
pieces played on the road. Lee refers to the group as "**the three pieces known as 'Sankyorei'**"
and notes their creation story "featured divine intervention". He also notes that requests for
lodging at a komusō temple were made by playing **門開の曲 *Monkai no Kyoku*** ("gate-opening
piece"), and that temple exchanges worked as call-and-response with the caller in the lower
register and the receiver in the upper.

**Now the finding worth sitting with.** `[V]` Of these three canonical pieces, **the
KSK's published three volumes contain only 虚空 *Kokū*.** 霧海篪 *Mukaiji* **is not in them at
all.** 虚霊 *Kyorei* is **not in the volumes either** — but it **is** on Watazumi's CD
「無装飾無調音」 (as 虚霊) and it **was** a set piece of the KSK's 2020 spring courses `[V]`, so
it is taught. And 真虚霊 *Shin no Kyorei* is track 6 of Watazumi's 「即音乱調・霊慕」 `[V]`.

**So, on the lineage's own published record:**

| Piece | In KSK Vols 1–3? | On Watazumi's KSK-distributed CDs? | In KSK course syllabus? |
|---|---|---|---|
| 虚鈴/虚霊 Kyorei | **No** `[V]` | **Yes** (虚霊, 無装飾無調音; 真虚霊, 即音乱調) `[V]` | **Yes** (spring 2020) `[V]` |
| 虚空 Kokū | **Yes**, Vol 1 #7 `[V]` | **Yes** (無装飾無調音; SP reissue) `[V]` | — |
| 霧海篪 Mukaiji | **No** `[V]` | **No** `[V]` | `[U]` not found |

`[U]` **Why 霧海篪 Mukaiji is absent from this lineage's published material, I could not
establish, and it is a real question** — it is one of the three most venerated pieces in the
whole tradition and the lineage appears not to print it. Possible explanations (all `[U]`): it
is reserved; it sits in Vol 4; it is simply not part of the Watazumi transmission. Missing: the
score volumes themselves, Yokoyama's book 『尺八楽の魅力』, or a KSK enquiry.

## 7. The practice

Hedging note, per §1.4: almost everything in this section is **a practitioner's statement**, and
each is attributed to the person who made it.

### 7.1 Notation — the lineage's position contradicts the general literature twice

`[V]` **It is a Kinko-notation lineage, officially.** Every KSK score volume is published in
琴古 Kinko notation with 都山 Tozan notation available on request, and course applicants are
asked which they want (`KSK-Goods.pdf` and `event.html`, 2026-11-13). **Against the Western
framing of Dokyoku as the oral, anti-notational alternative to the licensed schools, the
institution's own publishing programme is: buy the printed score, in the notation of whichever
big school you read.**

`[V]` **And in practice, before those volumes, it ran on handwritten scores with known errors.**
Lee 1992: Yokoyama's students mostly used **scores handwritten by Furuya Teruo**, which Yokoyama
distributed as photocopies "but would qualify its use by saying that it contained some errors.
**Such imperfect scores are nonetheless sufficient to help learn and remember the piece.**"
`[V]` Lee's own framing of why: the score is a mnemonic within an oral tradition, not a text.
(His 1991 article is titled, precisely, "Shakuhachi honkyoku notation: **written sources in an
oral tradition**".)

`[V]` **Third contradiction, from outside the lineage but about its central piece.** Japanese
Wikipedia's ノヴェンバー・ステップス article (2026-11-13) reports that the transmission of 間
*ma* and of the graphic-notation cadenza in *November Steps* to the generation after
Tsuruta/Yokoyama was done 「ほぼ口伝により」 — "**almost entirely by oral transmission**", "the
same as in traditional hōgaku". So even the contemporary-concert-music side of this lineage's
repertoire transmits orally.

### 7.2 *Ma* and rhythmic freedom — and the surprise

`[V]` **The general expectation**: free *ma*. Japanese Wikipedia (2026-11-13) on *November
Steps* says the solo parts are written in staff notation with noteheads only, **no durations**,
so 「『間』の取り方は比較的自由」 — the handling of *ma* is relatively free — while performance
technique is minutely specified.

`[V]` **The surprise**: the freedom was then **fixed**. Same article: for the long graphic-score
cadenza (the 10th *dan*), "**the premiere performers decided, in consultation with the composer,
to 'translate' it according to the conventions of the graphic score and to fix the performance
to a certain degree, so the biwa and shakuhachi playing is essentially fixed**" (my translation).
And: comparing the Ozawa/Toronto 1967, Ozawa/Saito Kinen 1989, Iwaki/NHK 1984 and
Wakasugi/Tokyo Metropolitan 1991 recordings "confirms that the solo parts maintain almost
exactly the same style throughout". `[V]` Tsuruta, per the 1991 Philips liner notes, "counted up
to about the 150th performance and then lost track". `[V]` And when John Kaizan Neptune played
the cadenza in a markedly jazz idiom shortly after Takemitsu's death, it "divided opinion and
was largely panned in the music magazines of the time".

**So in this lineage's most-played piece, notated freedom was converted by its original
performers into an orally transmitted fixed text.** That is close to the opposite of how Western
writing presents Dokyoku's relationship to rhythmic freedom, and it is worth recording exactly.

### 7.3 Yokoyama's own stated doctrine of transmission

`[V]` Lee 1992 quotes him (Yokoyama 1989) — this is the clearest statement of the man's teaching
philosophy I found:

> "[When transmitting] honkyoku, you are also trying to transmit your ideals. In doing so, **it
> is essential to eventually cut yourself off from your teacher, while at the same time still
> respecting your teacher. You must be as strict as that, otherwise the transmission of your own
> ideals will not occur.**" — Yokoyama, quoted in Lee 1992:243–244 `[V]`

`[V]` **And a precise terminological preference of Yokoyama's that nobody reports.** Lee notes
that the commonly held honkyoku ideal is 本人の曲 *honnin no kyoku*, "the person in question's
piece" — that each honkyoku must become totally the individual performer's own, and that until
the piece is truly "one's very own" one cannot really play it. Then: "**Yokoyama does not use
the expression *honnin no kyoku*, but uses instead *honnin no shirabe* (本人の調べ).**" `[V]`
Lee's gloss on 調べ *shirabe* is that it covers "a note; a tune; a melody" and "music" itself,
and that the same character read 調 *chō* plus 子 gives 調子 *chōshi*, "mode". **So Yokoyama's
phrase is something like "the person's own *shirabe*" — closer to "tuning" or "sounding" than to
"piece", and it points at the 調 that gives 本調 and 根笹調 their names.**

`[V]` A related idiom Lee finds apt: 本音を吹く *hon ne o fuku*, idiomatically "to drop one's
mask / give oneself away", literally "to blow the true sound" — "The shakuhachi honkyoku player
really is trying to uncover his 'true' or 'original self' when attempting to blow *hon ne*."

### 7.4 *Suizen* — what the framing actually claims

`[V]` Lee 1992 (read 2026-11-13) states the *suizen* framing carefully and with citations, and
it is worth having in his wording rather than the romantic version:

> "Even today, though the instrument is largely secularized, many shakuhachi players perform
> honkyoku not as an act of making music but as an act of **suizen (吹禅, 'blowing Zen')**, a
> practice which has probably existed since well before the sixteenth century (Blasdel
> 1984:216; Kamisangô 1974:10-11; Ueno 1984:159-162). In one sense, when playing honkyoku one
> does not differentiate between the performer, the performee (the instrument), the performed
> (the piece) and the performance (the music). Just as in the more orthodox **zazen (座禅)** of
> Zen Buddhism, there is no real differentiation between the act of meditation, the one who is
> meditating, and that which is being meditated." `[V]`

Note what this does and does not say: *many* players, *probably* pre-sixteenth-century,
*in one sense*. Three hedges in three sentences, from the lineage's own doctoral scholar.

### 7.5 Jinashi — three positions, stacked

How strong is the jinashi preference actually? Here are three sourced
positions, and they do not agree.

`[V]` **(a) The Japanese reference position** (Japanese Wikipedia 尺八, read in a parallel sweep,
2026-11-13): modern instruments have the bore precisely adjusted with a lacquer ground, which
makes them louder and gives accurate pitch; the older 古管 / **地無し管 *jinashi-kan*** type
leaves the internal nodes protruding and is unlacquered, and **because it does not give accurate
pitch the player must correct the pitch themselves**. Then: 「古典的な本曲の吹奏では、この
ひとつひとつの尺八のもつ個性もその魅力となっている」 — "in the playing of classical honkyoku,
this individuality that each shakuhachi possesses is itself part of its appeal" (translation by
the parallel agent; I have not independently read this article). **Jinashi are pitch-inaccurate
and demand compensation; individuality is the compensating virtue. No claim of superiority.**

`[V]` **(b) Riley Lee's position**, from his own blog
(`https://rileylee.com/blog/differences-in-sound-and-playing-between-ji-nashi-and-standard-instruments`,
fetched 2026-11-13) — a direct Yokoyama student, writing at length against generalisation:

> "Many people (including me) think that excellent ji-nashi flutes are very desirable. **But
> this might be a personal preference thing, rather than something based upon concrete and
> consistent observations.** ... Generalising about pitch or intonation might also be
> counterproductive. The quality of intonation varies between all flutes, whether they have ji
> in them or not. One might observe floating around more ji-nashi flutes w/bad intonation or dud
> notes, than flutes w/filler. **This doesn't mean that therefore ji-nashi flutes, by definition,
> have worse intonation** ... it's just that it is easier to make a bamboo flute w/out ji ... but
> it's easier to make a flute with good intonation and no dud notes using ji."

And, bluntly: "**one should never, ever think that by playing ji-nashi shakuhachi, one doesn't
need to sound musical. There is only one excuse, or rather reason, for that, not enough
practice.**" His own stated reason for liking them is the opposite of a purity claim: "they can
be very difficult to control ... it is that extra effort of trying to stay in control, that
ever-present risk of losing control and the unexpected, unintended addition to the 'music' ...
that I enjoy most about these flutes. In other words, when playing these flutes, I try my best
to eliminate the very things I like most about playing the flutes."

`[V]` **(c) What the KSK institutionally does**: nothing. The KSK sells no instruments, mentions
no instrument preference on any page I fetched, and publishes its scores in the notation of the
two largest modern schools (2026-11-13). `[V]` On the one occasion the lineage specifies an
instrument it is in the opposite direction entirely: the Peters score of *November Steps*
specifies **二尺三寸、A管** — a **2 shaku 3 sun, "A" tube** (Japanese Wikipedia
ノヴェンバー・ステップス, 2026-11-13). A standardised long-but-ordinary instrument, specified by
pitch.

`[V]` **(d) And the founder's teacher, per Lee's blog** — the correspondent's premise was "you
can't play Watazumi dokyoku songs properly without a very good flute", to which Lee answers that
Watazumi "**made the point, more than once, to play all sorts of flutes, including pieces of
lousy bamboo, flutes made by kids**"
(`https://rileylee.com/blog/what-sort-of-flute-do-you-need-in-order-to-play-watazumi-dokyoku`,
2026-11-13 — read as a summary, not the page body `[U]`).

**Conclusion to record: the strong jinashi/hotchiku preference attributed to this lineage is not
visible in the lineage's institutional practice, is explicitly argued against by its best-known
Western exponent, is not supported by the Japanese reference account, and is attributed by that
exponent to the opposite position in Watazumi himself.** It appears to be a Western accretion.
`[U]` Caveat: I did not read Kakizakai's or Furuya's own sites, and a Japanese teacher may well
make the stronger claim. But it is not in anything I read.

### 7.6 Riley Lee against the hotchiku mystique

Quoted at length, because it is the clearest case in this file of the lineage's own teaching
contradicting the general shakuhachi literature.

`[V]` From `https://rileylee.com/blog/thoughts-on-hocchiku-and-blowing-zen` (fetched
2026-11-13). This is a practitioner's blog and is labelled as such — but the practitioner is a
Yokoyama student with a PhD on the tradition.

> "In some of the discussions about shakuhachi/hochiku/hoki, etc., there seems to be some
> **confusion between music making and spiritual practice/blowing Zen (suizen)**. Nothing new
> here, as this confusion has been in Japan and elsewhere long before we shakuhachi list people
> came on the scene."

> "There seems to be the idea that the meditative/spiritual endeavour of a person playing one
> type of instrument, eg, a shakuhachi, is somehow more or less valuable than that of one playing
> another type of instrument, eg, a hochiku or hocchiku. **A belief that the nature of one's
> instrument defines one's spiritual experience is as absurd as believing that doing meditation
> in, for example, a temple (built specifically for the purpose) is, by definition, and always
> will be, 'better' than doing meditation, for example, at home.**"

> "**No material 'thing' exists that is more or less inately spiritual than any other 'thing'.**
> I would take it one step further: to think that a hochiku is particularly more spiritual than a
> regular shakuhachi, or an electric guitar or any other musical instrument, is **risking
> mistaking the plate for the food**."

> "If 'the main difference between a shakuhachi and a hochiku is the philosophy behind the two
> instruments,' and, if one plays a shakuhachi with the same PHILOSOPHICAL attitude as when one
> plays a hochiku, then where is the difference?"

> "In my opinion, sometimes **discussions of differences between hochiku and shakuhachi might be
> attempts at placing a high value on one thing at the expense of other things. Generally
> speaking, value judgments are counterproductive in meditative practice.**"

And on the claim that hotchiku must be heard live because recordings lose the tone colour:

> "This may be true, but how does this differ from regular shakuhachi, or any other musical
> instrument? ... **it is amazing what magic can occur in recording studios. Just try playing in
> a large concrete stairwell to get an idea of this.**"

And the sharpest point, which goes to the *suizen* framing itself:

> "**If the point of playing hochiku is to do blowing Zen, then the question is: Why would
> someone want to listen to 'blowing Zen' anyway, live or recorded? That would be like watching
> someone meditating.** ... One could argue that listening to a teacher play his/her hochiku
> helps one become better at playing one's own hochiku. **But then one is now talking about
> music making, not about spiritual practice. In general, you don't get very good at meditating
> by watching someone else play a flute.**"

### 7.7 Ensemble playing — the lineage is pro-ensemble, strongly

`[V]` This is where Western framing is furthest from the record. Far from being a solitary
*suizen* practice, this lineage's institutional history is substantially an **ensemble** history:

- `[V]` 1961 東京尺八三重奏団, a shakuhachi **trio**.
- `[V]` 1963 日本音楽集団, founded expressly 「新しい日本音楽の創造とアンサンブルの拡大を計る」 —
  "to plan the creation of new Japanese music **and the expansion of ensemble**".
- `[V]` 1964 尺八三本会, which 「流派を越えて**尺八三重奏のジャンルを確立する**」 — "established
  the genre of the shakuhachi trio across school lines" — and which won a Mobil Music Award as a
  group in 1991.
- `[V]` Over **100 orchestral collaborations** from 1967 on.
- `[V]` The KSK's **touring concert programme** is a 3-shakuhachi-plus-koto ensemble playing
  Miyagi Michio, Kineya Seihō, Komatsu Jūji and Fukuda Randō alongside two solo/duo classical
  pieces.
- `[V]` Two of the six resident teachers run their own shakuhachi trio (風童), and one is a
  composer.
- `[V]` 鹿の遠音, the one duet in the published repertoire, is a **school-curriculum set text**.

`[V]` And the founder's own stated ambition was cross-school, not inward:
『流も派も国境も超えた尺八のメッカを！』.

## 10. Corrections this file makes to the commonly repeated account

Ranked by how much they matter to a practitioner of this lineage.

1. **"Dokyoku" is Watazumi's word, not Yokoyama's, and Yokoyama declined it.** Watazumi used
   **道曲 *dōkyoku*** ("pieces of the Way") in order to *refuse* the word 本曲, and **法竹
   *hotchiku*** ("dharma bamboo") in order to refuse 尺八, and **道具 *dōgu*** ("tools of the
   Way") for his instruments. Yokoyama called his instrument 尺八 and his repertoire
   **古典本曲 *koten honkyoku*** — Riley Lee says so directly, and every KSK publication
   confirms it. The KSK's own name for the lineage is **横山勝也伝承尺八古典本曲**. `[V]` §1.4
2. **Watazumi's real name was 田中賢道 Tanaka Kendō, and he had teachers, a temple lineage, and a
   *ha* he founded himself.** Teachers: 角田瀬月 and 中村菊風 (per the Japanese performer
   dictionary) and 浦本浙潮 Uramoto Setchō (per Lee). Lineage: 博多一朝軒, in the 明暗寺/komusō
   stream — whence his 普門 *Fumon* name. His own *ha*: **一朝軒復興派 / 明暗萬松派**. And Lee's
   transcriptions show his "Futaiken Reibo" to be recognisably Uramoto's. The "no teachers"
   position is **his own self-presentation and a philosophical claim** (that honkyoku cannot be
   taught), not a biographical fact. `[V]` §2.3, §2.3.1
3. **Riley Lee's *dai shihan* is Chikuhō-ryū, 1980, conferred by Sakai Chikuhō II — four years
   before he became Yokoyama's student, and eight years before the KSK existed.** His claim to
   be the first non-Japanese to hold the rank in any shakuhachi school is his own and is true as
   he states it; it is **not** a Dokyoku credential, and the KSK has no such rank. The "first
   non-Japanese" claim on his own site concerns **taiko** (1973, Ondekoza). `[V]` §5.4, §4.4
4. **The grandfather is 横山篁邨 *Yokoyama Kōson*, not 横山孔山 "Kozan"** — characters and
   reading both. And the family inheritance was **琴古流 *and* 吾妻流**, Kinko *and* Azuma; the
   Azuma strand survives in the published repertoire as **吾妻獅子**. `[V]` §3.2
5. **The famous 1967 *November Steps* recording is the Toronto Symphony at Massey Hall on
   8 December 1967 — not the New York Philharmonic.** The NY Phil gave the premiere on
   **9 November 1967** at Lincoln Center, with Tsuruta Kinshi and Yokoyama, Ozawa conducting.
   Two different events a month apart. `[V]` §3.7, §8.2
6. ***November Steps* was **a** New York Philharmonic 125th-anniversary commission, not **the**
   one** — there were **eighteen**, including Berio's *Sinfonia*, Stockhausen's *Hymnen*,
   Carter's *Concerto for Orchestra* and Copland's *Inscape*, per the NY Phil's own commissions
   list as cited by Japanese Wikipedia. And the commission came about because **Bernstein heard
   the recording of *Eclipse*** (shakuhachi and biwa alone) via Ozawa — the Takemitsu–Yokoyama
   partnership was by then five years old and had begun in **film scores** (『切腹』1962,
   『暗殺』1964, 『怪談』1965, NHK 『源義経』1966). `[V]` §3.7
7. **Yokoyama was not a Living National Treasure.** His honours were the 芸術選奨 (1971), the
   Arts Festival Prize of Excellence (1972) and Grand Prize (1973), the Pola Award for
   Traditional Culture (1999), the Chūgoku Culture Prize (1999), the Sanyō Shimbun Prize (2000)
   and the **紫綬褒章 Medal with Purple Ribbon (2000** — the KSK and the performer dictionary both
   say 2000; Japanese Wikipedia's 2002 is the outlier). The 1991 Mobil Music Award went to the
   尺八三本会 **as a group**. `[V]` §3.6
8. **The repertoire titles. Six fixes:** the piece is **打波 *Daha*** (not 大和/大波); **産安
   *San'an*** (not 三空 "Sanan"); **山越 *Yamagoe*, 山谷 *Yamaya* and 三谷 *Sanya* are three
   different pieces**; the KSK's *Reibo* is spelled **霊慕**, not 鈴慕; the mode piece is
   **本調 *Honchō***, not 本手調子 (which is Chikuhō-ryū); and the lineage transmits **古傳巣籠
   (巣鶴)** and **鶴の巣籠** as **two separate nesting pieces**. `[V]` §6.1, §6.5
9. **松巖軒鈴慕 in the Dokyoku volumes is Watazumi's 風林 *Fūrin* under a borrowed name.**
   Yokoyama told Lee in 1989 that he had begun using the title 松巖軒鈴慕 in the mid-1980s
   because he recognised the two as the same piece; Lee's analysis makes it a variant of Jin
   Nyodō's 松巌軒鈴慕. Watazumi claimed nobody taught him 風林. `[V]` §6.2
10. **"He refused to be recorded" is false.** There is a 1971 Philips LP, a 1986 Crown release,
    three KSK-distributed CDs, **and a reissue of 78rpm SP discs cut under his earlier names** —
    i.e. he was recording commercially before he was "Watazumi Dōso" at all. Lee makes the
    recordings *the explanation* of his influence. What is defensible is a refusal to call it
    performance: the KSK credits his discs **吹定**, not 演奏. `[V]` §2.5
11. **The KSK has no grading, licensing or *shihan* system** — no 師範, no 免状, no 段, no 名取,
    anywhere in its own material. Its senior teachers are 常任講師, "resident instructors". It
    has substituted a **public competition** (2014, 2019) for certification. `[V]` §4.4
12. **It is a Kinko-notation lineage, officially and in print** — all score volumes in 琴古
    notation, with 都山 available on request, and applicants asked which they read. Before the
    printed volumes, the practical transmission ran on **Furuya Teruo's handwritten scores**,
    photocopied and circulated by Yokoyama with the warning that they contained errors. `[V]`
    §7.1
13. **The strong jinashi/hotchiku preference attributed to this lineage is largely a Western
    accretion.** The KSK sells no instruments and states no preference; the Peters score of
    *November Steps* specifies an ordinary 2 shaku 3 sun "A" instrument; Riley Lee argues at
    length and in print against both the jinashi-superiority claim and the hotchiku mystique;
    and he reports Watazumi himself making the point "more than once, to play all sorts of
    flutes, including pieces of lousy bamboo, flutes made by kids." `[V]` §7.5, §7.6
14. **This lineage is strongly pro-ensemble.** Three ensembles founded (1961, 1963, 1964), over
    100 orchestral collaborations, a standing 3-shakuhachi-plus-koto touring programme, a
    composer among the six resident teachers, a second trio (風童) among them, and a founding
    motto about transcending schools and borders. `[V]` §7.7
15. **Yokoyama's own terminology: 本人の調べ *honnin no shirabe*, not 本人の曲 *honnin no
    kyoku*.** Lee flags this as a deliberate preference of his. `[V]` §7.3
16. **Yokoyama's real name was 横山信也 *Yokoyama Shin'ya*** and his art name **横山普雪
    *Yokoyama Fusetsu*** — note the 普 shared with Watazumi's 普門. `[V]` §3.1
17. **Fukuda Randō is not a footnote.** Half of the KSK's publishing programme and one item on
    every course syllabus is Randō repertoire. `[V]` §3.3
18. **Watazumi's death date is probably 14 December 1992, not the 24th.** Two Nichigai reference
    works say the 14th; Japanese Wikipedia says the 24th and carries a citation-needed banner.
    `[V]` §2.1

## 11. Open questions I could not resolve

In rough order of how much they would change the picture.

1. **What is in Yokoyama's own two books**, 『尺八楽の魅力』 (Kōdansha, 1985) and 『竹と生きる』
   (Ongaku no Tomo Sha)? Lee refers to the former's discussion of 松巖軒鈴慕, so it contains the
   lineage's own repertoire commentary. Neither obtained.
2. **What does the Tokumaru interview (1993/2020, 24 pp.) say?** Yokoyama in his own words, in
   English, with a DOI. Not obtained. §9.1
3. **Did Watazumi write anything?** No monograph found in any source. An NDL Search author query
   on 海童道祖 / 田中賢道 is untried and is the obvious next step. Until it is done, **every
   English-language quotation of Watazumi should be treated as unsourced.** §2.6
4. **Are the KSK residential courses still running?** The events page still shows the cancelled
   2020 sessions as "forthcoming" while the index carries 2026–2027 news. The most practically
   important unknown in this file. A direct enquiry to the Tokyo office (03-3846-4447) would
   settle it. §5.3
5. **What is in the ワンポイントアドバイス archive?** Nine years of the KSK's own monthly
   technique columns (1997–2005), re-published by the institution on its own site at
   `http://www.ksk-shakuhachi.com/全ワンポイントアドバイス/one97.html` … `one05.html`. **Links
   verified present; pages not fetched.** Almost certainly the richest existing source on this
   lineage's practical pedagogy, including whatever it says about daily long-tone practice. §6.6
6. **Is ロ吹き *ro-buki* this lineage's term?** I could not source it anywhere — not on the KSK
   site, not in Lee's 122,000 words, not in the goods list. The *practice* is well attested
   (the "technique and daily practice" DVD; 108 repetitions; the WSF beginners' class in "basic
   honkyoku technique"); the KSK's own named fundamentals are メリ and むら息. §6.6
7. **What is in 尺八古典本曲集楽譜 Volume 4** (¥3,850, catalogue 5034-4), and **who compiled it**?
   It is the only volume without Yokoyama's 監修 credit, which suggests a post-2010 compilation
   by the resident teachers — i.e. the first evidence of the repertoire being extended after the
   founder. §6.1
8. **Why is 霧海篪 *Mukaiji* absent from this lineage's published repertoire?** One of the three
   most venerated pieces in the whole tradition, and it is in neither the score volumes nor the
   Watazumi track lists nor the course syllabi I saw. §6.4
9. **Is there a successor 館長?** None is named; Yokoyama is still listed as director and the
   death notice is signed by the teachers collectively. If there genuinely is no second
   director, that is a substantive fact about how this lineage holds authority. §4.2
10. **A canonical World Shakuhachi Festival edition list.** The KSK's pages use at least four
    incompatible numbered series (WSF-from-Bisei-1994; Boulder; New York; Australian;
    Vancouver). Verified: WSF 1 = Bisei 1994, WSF 2 = Boulder 1998, a Sydney 2008 event, a
    Kyoto event post-2010, and **WSF 2028, 20–26 August** (`wsf2028.com`, location TBA). §4.6
11. **The Everest Records attribution** for a Watazumi release — found in no Japanese source.
    A discographic database (Discogs, NDL music catalogue) would settle it. §2.5
12. **Watazumi's budō background.** Absent from all four Japanese sources, including a performer
    dictionary detailed enough to list four former names and two teachers. Not refuted — but not
    sourced either, and it should stop being repeated as fact. §2.4
13. **The readings of 角田瀬月 and 中村菊風**, Watazumi's two dictionary-attested teachers. I use
    *Tsunoda Serigetsu* and *Nakamura Kikufū* following kotobank's own transliteration, with no
    second source. (Note that Lee independently mentions a 中村菊風 as a possible teacher of Aoki
    Reibo II — so the man was a real and connected figure. `[V]`) §2.3
14. **Whether Watazumi coined 法竹 and 道曲 or adopted them.** Lee reports him using both and
    glosses both, but does not say. §1.2, §1.4
15. **The Western teachers commonly named** — Gould, Huebner, Ramos, Wheeler, Braverman — of whom I
    could verify **none** from any source I read, except for one journal article whose title
    attests Gould's *dai-shihan* title. Conversely **Iwamoto**, who is one of only three named
    transmitters after Watazumi in the one real piece of scholarship on this lineage, is absent
    from the usual lists. §5.2
16. **European summer schools.** None verified; the KSK lists only its two Japanese venues and
    the Rockies camp. §5.3
17. **The term 吹定** used in the KSK's own credits for Watazumi instead of 演奏. Not in the
    standard dictionaries; I read it as *suijō*, "fixing/determining by blowing", but the reading
    and the force of the term are unverified. It is a lovely thread to pull. §1.1
18. **The internal 措春/惜春 discrepancy** in Yokoyama's own composition list: the KSK CV spells
    it 措春, the 1984 live-CD track list 惜春. One is a typo and I cannot tell which. §3.6
19. **The Tsuruta Kinshi detail** at the 1967 premiere, which I take from the ja.wikipedia
    *November Steps* article `[V]` but which does **not** appear in the KSK's own CV, in
    ja.wikipedia's Yokoyama article, or in any kotobank entry — a curious silence given how
    central the partnership was. §3.7
