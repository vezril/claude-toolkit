# The Dokyoku Lineage

The shakuhachi line running **Uramoto Setchō → Watazumi Dōso → Yokoyama Katsuya → Iwamoto Yoshikazu and the KSK teachers**, which English-speaking players call **Dokyoku** and which the lineage itself calls **横山勝也伝承尺八古典本曲** — "Yokoyama Katsuya-transmission classical shakuhachi honkyoku."

For the instrument as an object and the wider school map see [[shakuhachi]]; for general technique and notation see [[shakuhachi-performance]]. This skill is the lineage.

## Start here: the name is not what the English-speaking world thinks

**道曲 *dōkyoku* is Watazumi's word, and Yokoyama declined it.**

Watazumi coined **道曲** ("pieces of the Way") off his own title 海童**道**祖, and used it precisely in order to **refuse** the word **本曲**. By the same move he used **法竹 *hotchiku*** ("dharma bamboo") to refuse **尺八**, and **道具 *dōgu*** ("tools of the Way", punning on the ordinary Japanese word for "tool") for his instruments. All three pivot on 道.

**Yokoyama did the opposite.** He called his instrument **尺八** and his repertoire **古典本曲 *koten honkyoku***. Riley Lee states this directly, and every KSK publication confirms it: the score volumes are 「尺八古典本曲集／横山勝也・監修」, the competition is 横山勝也伝承尺八古典本曲コンクール, and **the word "Dokyoku" appears nowhere in the lineage's own Japanese.**

So modern English **"Dokyoku" is a transfer of the teacher's private refusal-vocabulary onto the student who had explicitly declined to use it.** The candidate spellings 独奏曲 and 童曲 are both simply wrong. Two further distinctions English routinely collapses: **海童道 *Watazumidō*** is Watazumi's own named *path*, Zen-grounded and explicitly not a music school; and **海童道祖** is a **title** ("patriarch of the Watazumi Way"), not a given name.

None of this makes the lineage less real, and none of it is a reason to stop saying "Dokyoku" in English — it is the word that communicates. It is a reason to know what the lineage calls itself, and to notice that the English name encodes a disagreement between teacher and student.

## The corrections that matter most to a practitioner

- **Watazumi had teachers, a temple lineage, and a *ha* he founded himself.** Real name **田中賢道 Tanaka Kendō**. Teachers **角田瀬月** and **中村菊風** per the Japanese performer dictionary, and **浦本浙潮 Uramoto Setchō** per Lee. Lineage: **博多一朝軒**, in the Myōan-ji/komusō stream — whence the 普門 *Fumon* element in his names, which is a **lineage** name and not a religious one. His own *ha*: **一朝軒復興派 / 明暗萬松派**. And Lee **proves the Uramoto transmission musically**, by transcription analysis of their two "Futaiken Reibo" performances, *against Watazumi's own denial of it.* The honest formulation is not "Watazumi had no teachers" (false) but **"Watazumi held that honkyoku cannot be taught, and therefore declined to name teachers"** (true, and far more interesting).
- **"He refused to be recorded" is false.** A 1971 Philips LP, a 1986 Crown release, three KSK-distributed CDs, **and a reissue of 78rpm SP discs cut under his earlier names** — he was recording commercially before he was "Watazumi Dōso" at all, and Lee makes the recordings *the explanation* of his influence. What is defensible is a refusal to call it performance: the KSK credits his discs **吹定**, not 演奏.
- **Yokoyama was not a Living National Treasure.** His honours were the 芸術選奨 (1971), Arts Festival Prize of Excellence (1972) and Grand Prize (1973), the Pola Award (1999), the Chūgoku Culture Prize (1999), the Sanyō Shimbun Prize (2000) and the **紫綬褒章 (2000**, per both the KSK's own CV and the performer dictionary; Japanese Wikipedia's 2002 is the outlier). The seven shakuhachi 人間国宝 are all Kinko or Tozan players — see [[shakuhachi]].
- **The grandfather is 横山篁邨 *Kōson*, not 横山孔山 "Kozan"** — characters and reading both. And the family inheritance was **琴古流 *and* 吾妻流**, Kinko *and* Azuma; the Azuma strand survives in the published repertoire as **吾妻獅子**. Yokoyama's own real name was **横山信也 Shin'ya**, his art name **横山普雪 Fusetsu** — note the 普 shared with Watazumi's 普門.
- ***November Steps*: the premiere and the famous recording are two different events a month apart.* The **premiere** was the New York Philharmonic, **9 November 1967**, Lincoln Center, with Tsuruta Kinshi and Yokoyama, Ozawa conducting. The **famous recording** is the **Toronto Symphony at Massey Hall, 8 December 1967** (RCA BVCC-9383). It was also **a** 125th-anniversary commission, not **the** one — there were **eighteen**, including Berio's *Sinfonia* and Stockhausen's *Hymnen*. And the commission happened because **Bernstein heard the *Eclipse* recording** via Ozawa; the Takemitsu–Yokoyama partnership was five years old by then and had begun in **film scores** (『切腹』1962, 『暗殺』1964, 『怪談』1965, NHK 『源義経』1966).
- **Riley Lee's *dai shihan* is a Chikuhō-ryū grade, 1980, from Sakai Chikuhō II** — four years *before* he became Yokoyama's student and eight years before the KSK existed. His claim to be the first non-Japanese to hold the rank in any shakuhachi school is his own and true as he words it, but **it is not a Dokyoku credential, and the KSK has no such rank**. (The "first non-Japanese" claim on his own site is about **taiko**.)
- **The strong jinashi/hotchiku preference attributed to this lineage is largely a Western accretion.** The KSK sells no instruments and states no preference; the Peters score of *November Steps* specifies an ordinary **2 shaku 3 sun "A"** instrument; and Lee argues in print against both the jinashi-superiority claim and the hotchiku mystique, reporting Watazumi himself saying more than once to play all sorts of flutes, "including pieces of lousy bamboo, flutes made by kids."
- **This lineage is strongly pro-ensemble**, which cuts against the solitary-ascetic picture: three ensembles founded (1961, 1963, 1964), over 100 orchestral collaborations, a standing three-shakuhachi-and-koto touring programme, a composer and a second trio among the six resident teachers, and a founding motto about transcending schools and borders.
- **It is a Kinko-notation lineage, officially and in print** — all score volumes in 琴古 notation, Tozan available on request, applicants asked which they read. Before the printed volumes the practical transmission ran on **Furuya Teruo's handwritten scores**, photocopied and circulated by Yokoyama with a warning that they contained errors.
- **The KSK has no grading, licensing or *shihan* system at all** — no 師範, no 免状, no 段, no 名取. Its senior teachers are **常任講師**, "resident instructors." It has substituted a **public competition** (2014, 2019) for certification. Given how central licensing is to Kinko and Tozan, that absence is one of the most informative facts about the lineage.
- **Six repertoire title fixes**: the piece is **打波 *Daha*** (not 大和/大波); **産安 *San'an*** (not 三空); **山越 *Yamagoe*, 山谷 *Yamaya* and 三谷 *Sanya* are three different pieces**; the KSK's *Reibo* is **霊慕**, not 鈴慕; the mode piece is **本調 *Honchō***, not 本手調子 (which is Chikuhō-ryū); and **古傳巣籠 (巣鶴)** and **鶴の巣籠** are **two separate** nesting pieces.
- **Yokoyama's own terminology was 本人の調べ *honnin no shirabe*, not 本人の曲** — Lee flags it as a deliberate preference.
- **Watazumi's death date is probably 14 December 1992, not the 24th** — two Nichigai reference works give the 14th; Japanese Wikipedia gives the 24th with a citation-needed banner.

## What the evidence base actually is

**Riley Lee's thesis is the scholarship** — *Yearning for the Bell: a study of transmission in the shakuhachi honkyoku tradition*, University of Sydney, 1992, ~122,000 words, read in full. Watazumi appears 374 times in it, Yokoyama 285, and its central chapter is a ten-performance transcription comparison of "Reibo" in which their two versions are two of the ten. The Sydney repository is Cloudflare-blocked, but **Lee hosts it himself at `rileylee.net/phd`**.

Beyond that, **there is nothing peer-reviewed on the KSK as an institution or on present-day practice.** For those, the lineage's own account is the only account — and this skill labels it as such rather than presenting it as established fact. The KSK's live Japanese site (HTTP-only, Shift-JIS, maintained by Kakizakai and updated into 2026) is the primary institutional source, and its `KSK-Goods.pdf` yielded the canonical 22-piece repertoire across three Yokoyama-supervised volumes.

Two outstanding items would change the picture: **Tokumaru's 24-page interview with Yokoyama** (*Contemporary Music Review* 8:2, 1993, 53–76, DOI `10.1080/07494469400640051`) — the founder in his own words, still unread; and **Yokoyama's own two books**, 『尺八楽の魅力』 (Kōdansha, 1985) and 『竹と生きる』.
