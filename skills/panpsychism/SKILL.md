# Panpsychism

**SEP's definition, verbatim: panpsychism is "the view that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world."**

The literal reading — everything has a mind — is explicitly *not* contemporary usage. Chalmers:

> "Panpsychism, taken literally, is the doctrine that everything has a mind. In practice, people who call themselves panpsychists are not committed to as strong a doctrine. They are not committed to the thesis that the number two has a mind, or that the Eiffel tower has a mind… we can understand panpsychism as the thesis that **some fundamental physical entities have mental states.**"

A panpsychist need not hold that a rock has mental properties, only that its fundamental parts do.

## What it is not

- **Not animism.** Animism "attributes fully developed, intelligent spirits to things" and "comes more from mythology than philosophy" (IEP). The distinction that matters: panpsychism attributes *experience* to fundamental entities, not *agency, intelligence or spirit* to macroscopic ones. Panpsychists deliberately prefer "sentience," "experience," "feeling" and avoid "consciousness" and "cognition."
- **Not hylozoism** (everything is alive) — IEP: "Now considered less useful."
- **Not idealism.** Idealism does away with the physical world; panpsychism does not. ⚠️ But the boundary is genuinely porous — SEP describes Royce and Lotze as "idealist panpsychists," and cosmopsychism sits closest to idealism.
- **Not dualism.** Panpsychism is monist; dualism leaves "a radically disunified picture of nature."
- **Its relation to physicalism is the interesting case**, because Strawson's whole argument is that physicalism *entails* panpsychism. Chalmers shows the classification turns on whether quiddities count as physical properties, and his verdict is that constitutive Russellian panpsychism "falls into a… grey area" and the dispute is "**something of a verbal dispute**" — compatible with broad but not narrow physicalism.
- **Emergentism is a sub-family within panpsychism, not a rival.** The contrast that does real work is constitutive vs non-constitutive, and Strawson's anti-*brute*-emergence argument targets physicalist emergence, not every form of emergence.

## The taxonomy — three orthogonal axes

Chalmers' taxonomy is canonical, and **it is three independent axes, which is what most summaries get wrong.**

| Axis | The distinction |
|---|---|
| **Constitutive vs non-constitutive** | Does macroexperience obtain **in virtue of** microexperience? Constitutive says yes. Non-constitutive panpsychists are typically **emergent** panpsychists — and "nonconstitutive panpsychism inherits many of the problems of dualism, while it is constitutive panpsychism that offers hope for a Hegelian synthesis" |
| **Russellian vs non-Russellian** | Are some **quiddities** (the intrinsic properties playing microphysical roles) microphenomenal? Russellian panpsychism "addresses two metaphysical problems… and in effect answers both of them at once." Non-Russellian versions have microphenomenal properties that don't play microphysical roles, so they "face obvious problems with mental causation" |
| **Micropsychism vs cosmopsychism** | Is mentality fundamental at the **micro** or the **cosmic** level? Cosmopsychism draws on Schaffer's priority monism, and faces the **mirror-image problem** — decombination/individuation rather than combination |

**Chalmers' target throughout is constitutive Russellian panpsychism**, "perhaps the most important form of panpsychism, precisely because it is this form that promises to avoid the problems of physicalism and dualism."

### Which labels name distinct positions

| Label | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Panexperientialism** | **A label.** SEP equates it with panpsychism. Chalmers: "This thesis is sometimes called panexperientialism… **but I will simply call it panpsychism here.**" Coined by David Ray Griffin in the 1970s. Its real contrast class is *pancognitivism*, which essentially nobody defends |
| **Micropsychism / cosmopsychism** | **Genuinely distinct** — different level of priority, and different hard problems |
| **Constitutive / non-constitutive** | **Genuinely distinct, and the single most important division** — it determines whether you face the combination problem at all |
| **Russellian monism** | **Distinct and broader than panpsychism.** The conjunction of structuralism about physics, realism about quiddities, and quidditism about consciousness. It can be panpsychist, panprotopsychist, neutral-monist **or physicalist**. So Russellian panpsychism ⊂ Russellian monism |
| **Panprotopsychism** | **Distinct.** *Proto*-consciousness is fundamental; quiddities are "special properties that are not phenomenal" that can collectively constitute phenomenal ones. **It does not entail panpsychism** |
| **Panqualityism** | **Distinct.** Qualities are unexperienced in matter and become experienced through functional roles (Coleman) — a "middle way" |
| **Neutral monism** | **Distinct but overlapping.** Quiddities and structural properties are "neither physical nor mental but underlie both" |
| **Dual-aspect theory** | **A family, not a sharp position.** SEP gives no standalone definition; treat as a label |

## The motivating arguments

**1. The hard problem** (Chalmers 1995). Physicalism's unity is "arguably bought at the cost of explaining conscious experience," while dualism leaves nature disunified. Constitutive Russellian panpsychism is offered as the **Hegelian synthesis** — Chalmers' own term — which "promises to share the advantages of both materialism and dualism and the disadvantages of neither."

**Note the structure carefully: panpsychism is not offered as a *solution* to the hard problem. It is offered as a relocation of the explanandum that is supposed to make it tractable — which is exactly the opening the "relocates the mystery" objection exploits.**

**2. Evolutionary continuity** (Clifford, James). Evolution cannot introduce a wholly novel property at some point. Clifford's phrase: "the Amoeba which swims about in our own blood." James warns against "the irruption into the universe of a new nature." IEP traces the general form (*ex nihilo, nihil fit*) back through Epicurus, Telesio, Patrizi, Campanella, Fechner and Paulsen. Goff's sorites variant (2013): if consciousness has no vague boundaries, its first appearance would have to be triggered by an arbitrary precise micro-change.

**3. The intrinsic-nature argument** (Russell, *The Analysis of Matter*, 1927; Eddington, Gifford Lectures 1927). Physics gives only mathematico-nomic, *relational* descriptions — it tells us what the mass role *is* but not what property plays it. Russell's line: **"all the things in the world will merely be each other's washing."** Eddington says science describes the "background of 'pointer readings and similar indications'," not the nature of the entities, and calls the alternative "silly."

The second step is a **parsimony inference**: since brain matter is already known to be consciousness-involving, extending this to all matter is the simplest hypothesis. Goff's model for the inference form is Einstein over Lorentz — accepted "not because Einstein's theory accounted for any observations that the Lorentzian theory could not account for, but because Einstein provided a much simpler and more elegant explanation."

**4. Strawson against brute emergence.** "Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism," *JCS* 13(10–11):3–31, 2006. If physicalism is true, mental phenomena must be physical; **"Emergence can't be brute"**; therefore consciousness must come from more basic consciousness to be intelligible. Earlier ancestor: Nagel, "Panpsychism," in *Mortal Questions*, 1979.

**5. Mental causation.** Panpsychism lets consciousness be part of the causally closed physical system rather than an epiphenomenal add-on: "constitutive panpsychism allows that macroexperience can inherit causal relevance from microexperience… when entities at this level are constitutively connected, there need be no causal exclusion." **This is the Russellian panpsychist's best selling point — and it is exactly where Carroll attacks.**

Also: Mørch's argument from the experience of causation — experience reveals a necessary connection between, say, pain and trying to avoid it, which is evidence that phenomenality underlies causal powers.

## The combination problem

**It is the consensus hardest problem, and the consensus is that it is unsolved. That concession is in the SEP entry, written by Goff, panpsychism's leading defender:**

> "**It is generally agreed, both by its proponents and by its opponents, that the hardest problem facing panpsychism is what has become known as the 'combination problem'.**"
>
> "**The general consensus among panpsychists is that there is currently no wholly adequate solution to the combination problem.**"

**Provenance:** the term is Seager's (1995); the argument is James's, *Principles of Psychology* (1890), ch. 6 "The Mind-Stuff Theory":

> "Take a hundred of them [feelings], shuffle them and pack them as close together as you can… still **each remains the same feeling it always was, shut in its own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean**… Take a sentence of a dozen words, and take twelve men and tell to each one word. Then stand the men in a row or jam them in a bunch, and let each think of his word as intently as he will; **nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence**… **The private minds do not agglomerate into a higher compound mind.**"

⚠️ Kant, in *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*, was apparently the first to deploy it against panpsychism. And James himself later became a panpsychist, writing in 1909 that "the constitution of reality which I am making for is of the psychic type."

### Chalmers' decomposition

**Three sub-problems tracking the three aspects of phenomenal states, each with an "especially pressing aspect":**

| Sub-problem | The question | Especially pressing aspect |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject combination** | "how do microsubjects combine to yield macrosubjects?" | **the subject-summing problem** |
| **Quality combination** | "how do microqualities combine to yield macroqualities?" | **the palette problem** |
| **Structure combination** | "how does microexperiential structure combine to yield macroexperiential structure?" | **the structural mismatch problem** |

Plus four further aspects he insists on: the **unity**, **boundary**, **awareness** and **grain** problems.

**The subject-summing problem**, formalized:

> (1) If constitutive panpsychism is true, the existence of a number of microsubjects with certain experiences necessitates the existence of a distinct macrosubject.
> (2) It is never the case that the existence of a number of subjects with certain experiences necessitates the existence of a distinct subject.
> ∴ (3) Constitutive panpsychism is false.

Premise (2) is backed by a **conceivability argument**, and this generalizes to the **panpsychist zombie**: a microphysical and microphenomenal duplicate of us without consciousness. **The force of this is that it is exactly the argument panpsychists use against physicalism, turned on them** — and Chalmers says so: "conceivability/possibility claims can be rejected, but not without incurring substantial costs, and **panpsychists who have rejected physicalism in part on the basis of conceivability arguments are not in a good position to do so.**"

**The palette problem.** "There is a vast array of macroqualities… There is presumably only a limited palette of microqualities. **Especially if Russellian panpsychism is true, we can expect only a handful of microqualities, corresponding to the handful of fundamental microphysical properties.** How can this limited palette… yield the vast array of macroqualities?"

**The structural mismatch problem**, formalized as an apparently inconsistent tetrad — the most elegant statement of the problem in the literature:

> (1) Microphenomenal structure is isomorphic to microphysical structure. *[Russellian panpsychism]*
> (2) Microphenomenal structure constitutes macrophenomenal structure. *[constitutive panpsychism]*
> (3) Microphysical structure constitutes macrophysical structure. *[widely accepted]*
> (4) Macrophenomenal structure is **not** isomorphic to macrophysical structure. *[the datum]*

Chalmers on which is the live threat: "**if we are looking to either solve the combination problem or to prove it unsolvable, I think the structural mismatch problem is a promising place to focus.**"

### Chalmers' conclusion — the strongest statement of the difficulty, from a sympathizer

> "It is common for a proposed solution to address only one of these problems… **It should be stressed that a satisfactory solution must address all of these problems. This raises the bar for a solution, as it is far from clear that any single proposal can solve all the problems at once.**"
>
> "After a close analysis of the many aspects of the combination problem and the limited resources for solving them, **it is easy to be pessimistic about the prospects for a solution.**… The Russellian monist is constrained to find a theory whereby macroexperience is constituted by a tiny range of underlying primitive properties and without any further contingent fundamental laws. **This is a little like trying to juggle seven balls in the air with both hands tied behind one's back.**
>
> **It may be that the constraints imposed by the combination problem are so strong that the challenge cannot be answered.**"

### The strong form: summing is impossible, not merely unexplained

**Coleman, "The Real Combination Problem," *Erkenntnis* 79(1):19–44** — an argument that subject-summing is *incoherent*, because **each subject's point of view excludes all others**, so subjects cannot coherently combine. (Coleman's own positive view is panqualityism — he drops micro-*subjects* precisely to dodge this.)

### The attempted solutions, and their status

**None has gained widespread acceptance.** Phenomenal bonding (Goff); combinatorial infusion/fusion where micro-subjects *cease to exist* on fusing (Seager, Mørch); quantum holism; mereological identity (Roelofs); small qualitative palettes and principles of informational composition (Chalmers' own preferred candidates — and the point where this literature reaches toward something IIT-shaped); nihilism about aggregates (James's own move, which Chalmers rejects as proving too much); the cosmopsychist evasion (which inherits a decombination problem); and the *tu quoque* deflation that "panpsychism's combination problem is a problem for everyone" (Mendelovici).

## The other objections

**The incredulous stare is not an argument, and both sides concede it.** Goff's reply: "**most arguments for panpsychism start from a datum which is known with greater certainty than the data of observation and experiments: the existence of human consciousness**… **There are always an infinite number of theories consistent with the data, and we must choose between them on the basis of theoretical virtues, such as parsimony and simplicity.**" Chalmers concurs: "this reaction on its own is not a serious objection… **there is good reason to think that any view of consciousness must embrace some counterintuitive conclusions.**"

**But the substitute criterion is parsimony, and that is attackable on its own terms** — see Henry Taylor, "Is panpsychism simple?", *Analysis* 79(2):265–275.

**Searle's dismissal is the hardest published, and it bites on individuation:**

> "**Consciousness cannot be spread over the universe like a thin veneer of jam; there has to be a point where my consciousness ends and yours begins.**… Why the iPhone? Why not each part of it? Each microprocessor? Why not each molecule?… **The problem with panpsychism is not that it is false; it does not get up to the level of being false. It is strictly speaking meaningless because no clear notion has been given to the claim. Consciousness comes in units and panpsychism cannot specify the units.**"

**This is the hinge to [[integrated-information-theory]]**, because "specify the units" is exactly what IIT's exclusion postulate claims to answer — and Tononi & Koch quote the "thin veneer of jam" line themselves.

**Explanatory idleness.** Frankish: "**Panpsychism offers no distinctive predictions or explanations. It finds a place for consciousness in the physical world, but that place is a sort of limbo**"; it "**threatens to render it explanatorily idle**," and "**consciousness is an unlikely candidate for this fundamental property. For, so far as our evidence goes, it is a highly localised phenomenon that is specific not only to brains but to particular states of brains.**"

**And note that the strongest version of this objection was published by Goff himself, before he became panpsychism's leading defender** — "Why Panpsychism Doesn't Help us Explain Consciousness," *Dialectica* 63(3):289–311, 2009: "**panpsychism cannot help us to explain consciousness, at least not the kind of consciousness we have pre-theoretical reason to believe in.**"

**Carroll's physics-side version attacks the mental-causation selling point directly.** "Consciousness and the Laws of Physics," *JCS* 28(9):16–31, 2021:

> "either your theory of consciousness keeps the dynamics of the Core Theory intact within its domain of applicability, or it doesn't. There aren't any other options!… **many non-physicalists are weirdly cagey about whether they're going to violate the Core Theory.** Philip suggested that one could rely on 'strong emergence'… **You can't.**"
>
> "the purported 'consciousness' that is central to Ψ-world is **playing no explanatory role whatsoever**… **by sequestering off 'consciousness' from playing any causal role in the world, you've turned it into something very different from what we were originally trying to explain.**"

Carroll's claim is that panpsychism does *not* secure mental causation — the phenomenal quiddity rides along without doing anything. This matches the Russellian-monism objection that quiddities may be "just along for the causal ride" (Howell).

**Also catalogued:** Amy Kind's "unmotivated" charge that Russellian monism leaves us "essentially back where we started"; Stoljar on whether the structural/non-structural distinction can be explicated as required; and Tallis's review verdict on the combination problem — "**Here we seem to have replaced one explanatory gap with another at least as wide.**"

## Testability

**The panpsychists' own position is that it is not directly testable, and they say so plainly.** Goff, asked directly whether panpsychism can be tested:

> "You can't look inside an electron to see whether or not it is conscious… **As soon as you start to answer this question, you move beyond what can be, strictly speaking, tested, simply because consciousness is unobservable. We have to turn to philosophy.**"

The substitute standard is **inference to the best explanation plus theoretical virtues**, with panpsychism framed as a *research programme* rather than a finished theory: "it was only many decades after Darwin and Wallace formulated the principle of natural selection that modern genetics developed… If it does eventually produce a perfectly adequate account… this will no doubt only be after many decades or centuries of serious inter-disciplinary work."

**Critics who treat untestability as fatal:** Frankish ("a sort of limbo"), Searle ("does not get up to the level of being false"), Carroll ("no explanatory role whatsoever"), Pigliucci ("not even wrong"), Seth ("an instructive example of how targeting the hard problem, rather than the real problem, can slow down or even stop experimental progress"), and — pointedly — **Tononi & Koch**: "**panpsychism's beauty has been singularly barren. Besides claiming that matter and mind are one thing, it has little constructive to say and offers no positive laws explaining how the mind is organized and works.**"

**Nothing found, after dedicated search, claims panpsychism *per se* generates novel empirical predictions. Every source reached that addresses the question — including the panpsychists — says the opposite.**

## Honest status

**A live minority position with genuine institutional infrastructure, emphatically not fringe in the sense of being outside the discipline — but a small minority, and more popular with non-experts than experts.**

**The 2020 PhilPapers survey** (Bourget & Chalmers, *Philosophers' Imprint* 23:11). The question offered five options: dualism, eliminativism, functionalism, identity theory, panpsychism.

| Option | % accepting or leaning |
|---|---|
| Functionalism | 33.0 |
| Other | 22.7 |
| Dualism | 22.0 |
| Identity theory | 13.3 |
| **Panpsychism** | **7.5** (6.1 exclusively) |
| Eliminativism | 4.5 |

**Three things to know about that number:**

1. **There is no 2009 comparison, because there was no consciousness question in 2009.** Goff's gloss is the right one: "**panpsychism did not even feature in the 2009 survey: it's gone from being a view that hardly anyone took seriously… to being one of the standard options to consider.**"
2. **The bias correction moves it *up*, from 6.2 to 8.0** — so the raw figure is not inflated by enthusiast self-selection; if anything the reverse.
3. **⚠️ And here is the tension worth flagging.** On a separate question in the *same* survey, "**only 2% accepted/leaned towards particles being conscious**" — a roughly 4× gap. Goff's own explanation: people answering the "other minds" question considered the neural-correlates evidence, while those answering the "consciousness" question gave their view on the best solution to the hard problem. **So "panpsychism" appears to be selected partly as a label for non-physicalism-that-isn't-dualism — plausibly including Russellian monists, who had no separate option — rather than as the literal micro-experience claim.**

⚠️ Specialist sub-groups, from a secondary source: philosophers of mind **10%**; philosophers of cognitive science **2%**. That split points the same way as the general finding that non-experts rate these theories higher than experts do.

**Also note:** physicalism's apparent decline is a population artefact. The headline 51.9% looks like a drop from 2009's 56.5%, but the paper warns against the comparison, and the like-for-like "comparable departments" figures are **56.5 → 57.4** — essentially flat.

## Always-apply

1. **State which panpsychism.** "Panpsychism" without the constitutive/Russellian/micro-vs-cosmo qualifiers is too coarse to evaluate — the positions face different objections.
2. **Do not say panpsychism claims rocks have minds.** It is the view that *fundamental* entities have experience.
3. **Lead with the combination problem**, and quote Goff's own concession rather than a critic's. It is stronger evidence and it is in the field's reference work.
4. **Do not treat the incredulous stare as an argument** — attack the parsimony claim instead, which is where the real dispute is.
5. **Distinguish "not testable" from "meaningless."** Panpsychists concede the first and dispute the second, and their substitute standard (inference to the best explanation) is a real epistemology, not an evasion.
6. **Keep Russellian monism separate.** It is broader than panpsychism and can be physicalist; conflating them makes the survey numbers and the literature unreadable.
7. **When linking to IIT, use Searle's "specify the units" demand** — it is the precise point of contact, and IIT's exclusion postulate is the answer on offer.
8. **Cite the 7.5% with its caveats**, especially the 2% particles gap.
