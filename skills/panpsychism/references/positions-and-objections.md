## Definition, and the boundaries

SEP: panpsychism is **"the view that mentality is fundamental and ubiquitous in the natural world."**

Chalmers' sharper version ("Panpsychism and Panprotopsychism," the 2013 Amherst Lecture; in Alter & Nagasawa eds., *Consciousness in the Physical World*, OUP 2015, pp. 246–276), verbatim:

> *"Panpsychism, taken literally, is the doctrine that everything has a mind. In practice, people who call themselves panpsychists are not committed to as strong a doctrine. They are not committed to the thesis that the number two has a mind, or that the Eiffel tower has a mind, or that the city of Canberra has a mind… we can understand panpsychism as the thesis that **some fundamental physical entities have mental states**. For example, if quarks or photons have mental states, that suffices for panpsychism to be true, even if rocks and numbers do not… we can read the definition as requiring that **all members of some fundamental physical types** (all photons, for example) have mental states."*

**The boundaries:**

- **vs. animism.** IEP is the better source: animism *"attributes fully developed, intelligent spirits to things"* and *"comes more from mythology than philosophy."* SEP does not engage animism at all except via Thales' "ubiquity of animation." **The distinction that matters: panpsychism attributes *experience* (phenomenality) to fundamental entities, not *agency, intelligence, or spirit* to macroscopic ones.** Panpsychists deliberately prefer "sentience," "experience," "feeling" and avoid "consciousness" and "cognition" (IEP).
- **vs. hylozoism** (everything is *alive*). IEP: *"Now considered less useful."*
- **vs. idealism.** Idealism does away with the physical world; panpsychism does not. ⚠️ **But the boundary is genuinely porous**: SEP describes Royce and Lotze as *"idealist panpsychists,"* for whom matter is a form of mind, and Sprigge's view as idealism-based. **Cosmopsychism sits closest to idealism.**
- **vs. dualism.** SEP: dualism leaves a *"radically disunified picture of nature"* and makes mind–brain interaction hard to explain. Panpsychism is monist.
- **vs. physicalism — the interesting case**, because **Strawson's whole argument is that physicalism *entails* panpsychism.** Chalmers shows the classification question turns on whether **quiddities** count as physical properties, and distinguishes **narrowly physical** properties (microphysical role properties) from **broadly physical** ones. His verdict: constitutive Russellian panpsychism *"falls into a … grey area"* and the dispute is *"something of a verbal dispute"*; **it is compatible with broad physicalism but not narrow physicalism.**
- **vs. emergentism.** SEP treats emergentism as a **sub-family within** panpsychism, not a rival: *layered emergentism* (Rosenberg, Brüntrup) has micro and emergent minds coexisting; *fusionism* (Seager, Mørch) has micro-subjects fusing. **The contrast that does real work is constitutive vs. non-constitutive**, and the anti-*brute*-emergence argument targets physicalist emergence, not every form of emergence.

## The taxonomy — three orthogonal axes

**Chalmers' taxonomy is the canonical one and it is three *independent* axes, which is the thing most summaries get wrong.**

### Axis 1 — constitutive vs. non-constitutive

Verbatim (Chalmers 2015): *"**Constitutive panpsychism** is the thesis that macroexperience is (wholly or partially) **grounded in** microexperience… macrophenomenal truths obtain in virtue of microphenomenal truths."* And: *"**non-constitutive panpsychism**, which holds that there is microexperience and macroexperience, but the microexperience does not ground the macroexperience. Nonconstitutive panpsychists will typically be **emergent panpsychists**, holding that macroexperience is strongly emergent from microexperience and/or from microphysics."*

His assessment: *"nonconstitutive panpsychism inherits many of the problems of dualism, while it is constitutive panpsychism that offers hope for a Hegelian synthesis."*

**Sub-split:** **type-A** constitutive panpsychism (a priori entailment from micro- to macrophenomenal truths) vs. **type-B** (a posteriori necessary entailment). Type-B *"inherits many of the problems of type-B materialism,"* **so type-A is the live option.**

### Axis 2 — Russellian vs. non-Russellian

Verbatim: *"**Russellian panpsychism** is the view that some quiddities are microphenomenal properties… The Russellian panpsychist addresses two metaphysical problems—what is the place of phenomenal properties in nature, and what are the intrinsic properties underlying physical structure?—and **in effect answers both of them at once**."* Non-Russellian panpsychism has microphenomenal properties that do *not* play microphysical roles, and so *"faces obvious problems with mental causation."*

**Chalmers' target throughout is therefore constitutive Russellian panpsychism**, *"perhaps the most important form of panpsychism, precisely because it is this form that promises to avoid the problems of physicalism and dualism."*

### Axis 3 — micropsychism vs. cosmopsychism

SEP: constitutive **micropsychism** grounds all facts in micro-level consciousness; constitutive **cosmopsychism** grounds them in cosmic-level consciousness, drawing on **Jonathan Schaffer's priority monism** ("Monism: The Priority of the Whole," 2010). **Cosmopsychism's problem is the mirror image of the combination problem — a *decombination* or individuation problem.**

### Which labels name genuinely distinct positions

| Label | Verdict |
|---|---|
| **Panexperientialism** | **Effectively a label.** SEP *equates* it with panpsychism (*"the claim that conscious experience is fundamental and ubiquitous"*). Chalmers: *"This thesis is sometimes called panexperientialism, to distinguish it from other varieties of panpsychism (varieties on which the relevant entities are required to think or reason, for example), **but I will simply call it panpsychism here**."* Coined by **David Ray Griffin in the 1970s** (IEP) in the Whiteheadian process tradition. Its contrast class is **pancognitivism** (thought, not experience, is fundamental) — **which essentially nobody defends** |
| **Micropsychism / cosmopsychism** | **Genuinely distinct** — different level of ontological priority, and **different hard problems** (combination vs. decombination) |
| **Constitutive / non-constitutive** | **Genuinely distinct, and the single most important division** — it determines whether you face the combination problem at all and whether you inherit dualism's causal problems |
| **Russellian monism** | **Distinct and broader than panpsychism.** Per SEP (Alter & Pereboom), the conjunction of three theses: **structuralism about physics, realism about quiddities, and quidditism about consciousness.** It holds that *"a single set of properties underlies both consciousness and the most basic entities posited by physics."* It can be panpsychist, panprotopsychist, neutral-monist, **or physicalist.** So **Russellian panpsychism ⊂ Russellian monism** |
| **Panprotopsychism** | **Distinct.** *Proto*-consciousness, not consciousness, is fundamental; quiddities are *"special properties that are not phenomenal"* (Chalmers) but can collectively constitute phenomenal ones. **It does not entail panpsychism.** SEP notes a definitional problem: **standard physicalism counts as panprotopsychism on a loose definition**, so a stricter definition requiring *transparent* explanation is needed |
| **Panqualityism** | **Distinct.** A *"middle way"* between panpsychism and physicalism: qualities are unexperienced in matter and become experienced through functional roles (Coleman) |
| **Neutral monism** | **Distinct but overlapping.** Per SEP Russellian Monism: both quiddities and structural properties are *"neither physical nor mental but underlie both."* SEP panpsychism says James's neutral monism *"can be read as dual-aspect and so as a kind of panpsychism, but it goes beyond that by adding mind-like elements"* |
| **Dual-aspect theory** | **A family, not a sharp position.** SEP gives no standalone definition; Spinoza is its reference point. SEP claims only that *"a dual-aspect neutral monism, as in Spinoza's philosophy, might count as panpsychism in its own right."* **Treat as a label** |

## The motivating arguments, with primary citations

### 1. The hard problem

**Chalmers, "Facing Up to the Problem of Consciousness," *JCS* 2(3):200–219, 1995**; expanded in *The Conscious Mind*, OUP 1996. SEP's framing: physicalism's simplicity and unity is *"arguably bought at the cost of explaining conscious experience,"* while dualism leaves nature disunified. Constitutive Russellian panpsychism is offered as the **Hegelian synthesis** — Chalmers' own term — which *"promises to share the advantages of both materialism and dualism and the disadvantages of neither. In particular, it can respect both the epistemological intuitions that motivate dualism and the causal intuitions that motivate physicalism."*

**Note the structure carefully: panpsychism is not offered as a *solution* to the hard problem. It is offered as a *relocation* of the explanandum that is supposed to make it tractable — which is exactly the opening the "relocates the mystery" objection exploits.**

### 2. The genetic / evolutionary-continuity argument

**Clifford and James.** Evolution cannot introduce a wholly novel property at some point, so something consciousness-like must run through lower organisms. Clifford's phrase: *"the Amoeba which swims about in our own blood."* James warns against *"the irruption into the universe of a new nature."* IEP's version, the **Argument from Non-Emergence**, traces it to the Greek *ex nihilo, nihil fit* and finds versions in **Epicurus, Telesio, Patrizi, Campanella, Fechner and Paulsen.**

Related: **Goff's sorites argument (2013)** — if consciousness has no vague boundaries, then its first appearance would have to be triggered by an arbitrary precise micro-change.

### 3. The intrinsic-nature argument (Russell, Eddington, 1920s)

**Russell, *The Analysis of Matter*, Kegan Paul, 1927** (also *An Outline of Philosophy*, 1927; *The Analysis of Mind*, 1921). **Eddington, Gifford Lectures 1927, published as *The Nature of the Physical World*, 1928.** SEP: in 1927 the two *"independently proposed closely similar ideas,"* which were then *"almost completely forgotten about for much of the twentieth century."*

The argument: **physics gives only mathematico-nomic, dispositional, *relational* descriptions. It tells us what the mass *role* is but not what property plays it.** Dispositional essentialism faces a vicious regress — Russell, *Analysis of Matter* p. 325: **"all the things in the world will merely be each other's washing."** Per SEP Russellian Monism, Russell says physics gives *"certain equations giving abstract properties of their changes,"* but *"as to this, physics is silent"* about what changes; Russellian monism *"can be seen as breaking that silence."* Eddington: science describes the *"background of 'pointer readings and similar indications',"* not the nature of the entities; he suggests atoms might have *"something of spiritual nature of which a prominent characteristic is *thought*,"* reasoning from his own brain's pointer readings, which he takes to be attached to consciousness. **Eddington calls the alternative "silly."**

**The second step is a simplicity/parsimony inference**: since brain matter is already known to be consciousness-involving, extending this to all matter is the most parsimonious hypothesis. Goff's analogy for the inference *form* (Aeon 2017): Einstein's special relativity supplanted the Lorentzian theory *"not because Einstein's theory accounted for any observations that the Lorentzian theory could not account for, but because Einstein provided a much simpler and more elegant explanation."*

### 4. Strawson against brute emergence

**Galen Strawson, "Realistic Monism: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism," *Journal of Consciousness Studies* 13(10–11):3–31, 2006.** Reprinted in Strawson, *Real Materialism and Other Essays*, OUP 2008, pp. 53–74, doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199267422.003.0003 (Crossref cited-by 47). The 2006 symposium volume: Strawson et al., *Consciousness and its Place in Nature: Does Physicalism Entail Panpsychism?*, ed. **Anthony Freeman**, Imprint Academic, 2006, with Strawson's reply "Panpsychism? Reply to Commentators with a Celebration of Descartes," JCS 13(10–11):184–280.

The argument, as IEP states it: if physicalism is true, mental phenomena must be physical; brute emergence is impossible; therefore everything is experiential. Strawson's key claim, verbatim per SEP: **"Emergence can't be brute"** — so consciousness must come from more basic consciousness to be intelligible. IEP quotes Wright: *"Emergence of mind from no mind at all is sheer magic."*

**Earlier ancestor: Thomas Nagel, "Panpsychism," in *Mortal Questions*, CUP 1979**, which rests on four premises: material composition, realism about mental states, no radical emergence, non-reductionism.

⚠️ **Citation hazard.** The SEP *panpsychism* entry's bibliography gives Strawson 2006a as "Realistic **Materialism**: Why Physicalism Entails Panpsychism." **Both the SEP Russellian Monism entry and Chalmers' own bibliography give "Realistic Monism," and Crossref confirms it. Use "Realistic Monism."** The same entry prints the editor as "Anthony **Freedman**" (correct: **Freeman**), and gives Strawson 2006b's page range as 184–208 while the Russellian Monism entry gives 184–280 — **an internal SEP inconsistency.**

### 5. Mental causation / causal closure

Panpsychism lets consciousness be part of the causally closed physical system rather than an epiphenomenal add-on. Chalmers' development is the clearest: *"constitutive panpsychism allows that macroexperience can inherit causal relevance from microexperience… when entities at this level are constitutively connected, there need be no causal exclusion."* **This is the Russellian panpsychist's best selling point — and it is exactly where Carroll attacks.**

### 6. Mørch's argument from the experience of causation

Experience reveals a necessary connection between, e.g., pain and trying to avoid it; **this is evidence that phenomenality underlies causal powers.** Hedda Hassel Mørch, "The Argument for Panpsychism from Experience of Causation," in Seager (ed.), *Routledge Handbook of Panpsychism*; developed from her 2014 Oslo PhD, "Panpsychism and Causation: A New Argument and a Solution to the Combination Problem."

## The historical lineage

**Ancient / medieval.** Thales (c. 624–545 BCE; the lodestone has a soul), Anaximenes (*pneuma*), Heraclitus (fire), Anaxagoras (c. 500–425 BCE; *nous*), Empedocles (elements ensouled), Plato (*Sophist*, *Philebus*, *Timaeus*, *Laws*), Aristotle (**qualified — IEP says not strictly a panpsychist**), Epicurus, Lucretius, the Stoics. **The contrast class is Democritus** (c. 460–370 BCE), the reductionist who relegated qualities to convention.

**Renaissance.** Cardano, Telesio (c. 1586; "pansensism"), **Francesco Patrizi (1591) — who coined the word "panpsychism"** (IEP), Bruno (1584), Campanella (c. 1620).

**Early modern.** **Spinoza (1632–77)** and **Leibniz (1646–1716)**. Leibniz's monads are mentalistic in nature; Whitehead keeps Leibniz's distinction between unities and mere aggregates (*"The echoes of Leibniz are not accidental,"* SEP), and Leibniz is among the roots of the intrinsic-nature argument. **Leibniz also addressed the combination problem in the late 1600s via the "dominant monad."**

⚠️ **On whether Spinoza counts — the honest answer is that SEP does not resolve it.** The entry says only that the world-soul view has *"obvious echoes of Spinoza"* and that *"a dual-aspect neutral monism, as in Spinoza's philosophy, might count as panpsychism in its own right."* It states no verdict and does not discuss the dispute. IEP lists Spinoza among 17th-century panpsychists without argument. **Flag this as contested; do not assert either way on the strength of these two sources.** The structural issue: Spinoza's thought-attribute applies to every mode under the attribute of extension, which looks panpsychist, but Spinoza's "ideas" are not obviously *experiences*, and his substance monism makes him at least as naturally read as a cosmopsychist or a dual-aspect theorist.

**19th century.** Fechner (1801–1887), Lotze (1817–1881), Wundt (1832–1920), **W. K. Clifford (1845–1879; "mind-stuff")**, **William James (1842–1910)**, Royce (1855–1916), Schopenhauer (1788–1860), Paulsen (1846–1908), Prince (1854–1929), von Hartmann (1842–1906), Schiller (1864–1937), Haeckel (1834–1919), Mach, Peirce.

**James specifically — his trajectory matters, because he is both the source of the strongest objection and (later) a panpsychist.** SEP: he began with neutral monism, then moved toward panpsychism, writing in 1909 that *"the constitution of reality which I am making for is of the psychic type."* SEP judges his final commitment *"quite clear,"* while conceding it *"remains somewhat controversial"* because of his *Principles of Psychology* attack on mind-dust. IEP: *"In 1890, William James raised it against Clifford's mind-stuff view. By 1909 he no longer treated it as an insurmountable hurdle."* ⚠️ **And Kant, in *Dreams of a Spirit-Seer*, was apparently the first to deploy the combination problem against panpsychism** (IEP).

**20th century.** Russell (1921, 1927), Eddington (1927/28), **Whitehead (1861–1947)** — events and processes replace the matter/space/time triad; his "occasions" have mentality *"in some—often extremely attenuated—sense,"* described through creativity, spontaneity, perception. Then Hartshorne ("psychicalism"), Sprigge (1983), Bergson, Dewey, Teilhard de Chardin, Bateson (1972), Dyson (1979), Bohm (1986), **Griffin (1998)**.

**The eclipse and the revival.** SEP: *"relatively little interest in panpsychism in Western philosophy"* from **the 1930s to the late twentieth century**, attributed to the dominance of physicalism and to hostility toward metaphysics up to the 1970s. Exceptions: Sprigge, Nagel 1979, Griffin 1998, Chalmers 1996. **The revival is attributed to metaphysics regaining acceptance from the 1970s on, and to "the continuing failure of physicalists to come up with a satisfying account of consciousness."**

## The combination problem in full

**It is the consensus hardest problem, and the consensus is that it is unsolved.** Goff, in the SEP entry he himself wrote, verbatim:

> *"**It is generally agreed, both by its proponents and by its opponents, that the hardest problem facing panpsychism is what has become known as the 'combination problem'. This term comes from William Seager (1995)**, although in the contemporary literature the problem itself is generally traced back to William James ([1890] 1981)."*

> *"**The general consensus among panpsychists is that there is currently no wholly adequate solution to the combination problem.**"*

**Provenance.** William Seager, "Consciousness, information, and panpsychism," *JCS* 2(3):272–288, 1995 — **the coinage.** William James, *The Principles of Psychology*, 1890, ch. 6 "The Mind-Stuff Theory" — **the argument.** ⚠️ Chalmers cites the 1895 edition; SEP cites [1890] 1981: 160 — **flag the edition discrepancy.** The passage, verbatim as Chalmers quotes it:

> *"Take a hundred of them [feelings], shuffle them and pack them as close together as you can (whatever that may mean); still **each remains the same feeling it always was, shut in its own skin, windowless, ignorant of what the other feelings are and mean**. There would be a hundred-and-first feeling there, if, when a group or series of such feelings were set up, a consciousness belonging to the group as such should emerge. And **this 101st feeling would be a totally new fact**; the 100 original feelings might, by a curious physical law, be a signal for its creation, when they came together; but **they would have no substantial identity with it, nor it with them, and one could never deduce the one from the others**, or (in any intelligible sense) say that they evolved it.*
>
> *Take a sentence of a dozen words, and take twelve men and tell to each one word. Then stand the men in a row or jam them in a bunch, and let each think of his word as intently as he will; **nowhere will there be a consciousness of the whole sentence**… **The private minds do not agglomerate into a higher compound mind.**"*

Goff's SEP gloss on why it bites specifically: *"At least on the face of it we have no problem with the idea of bricks forming a house, or mechanical parts forming a car engine. **But the idea of many minds forming some other mind is much harder to get your head around.**"*

### Chalmers' decomposition

**Chalmers, "The Combination Problem for Panpsychism," in Brüntrup & Jaskolla (eds.), *Panpsychism: Contemporary Perspectives*, OUP 2016, pp. 179–214**, doi:10.1093/acprof:oso/9780199359943.003.0008 (**Crossref cited-by 92**). Based on his opening presentation to **"Panpsychism on the Reef," Lady Elliot Island, July 2012**; the structural-mismatch material developed at **the Oslo panpsychism conference, August 2013.** He credits **Goff (2009)** with *"an especially sharp formulation."*

⚠️ **The structure is often misreported. It is three sub-problems, each with an "especially pressing aspect."** The three sub-problems track the three aspects of phenomenal states; the pressing aspects are the sharp arguments. Verbatim:

> *"The combination problem can be broken down into at least three subproblems, reflecting three different aspects of phenomenal states: their **subjective character** (they are always had by a subject), their **qualitative character** (they involve distinctive qualities), and their **structural character** (they have a certain complex structure). These three aspects yield what we might call the **subject combination problem**, the **quality combination problem**, and the **structure combination problem**."*

| Sub-problem | Statement (verbatim) | Especially pressing aspect |
|---|---|---|
| **Subject combination** | *"how do microsubjects combine to yield macrosubjects?"* | **the subject-summing problem** |
| **Quality combination** | *"how do microqualities combine to yield macroqualities?"* | **the palette problem** |
| **Structure combination** | *"how does microexperiential structure (and microphysical structure) combine to yield macroexperiential structure?"* | **the structural mismatch problem** |

**Plus four further aspects he insists on putting "on the table explicitly":**

- the **unity problem** (unified consciousness);
- the **boundary problem** (Rosenberg 1998 — bounded consciousness);
- the **awareness problem** (awareness of qualities);
- the **grain problem** (Maxwell 1979; Lockwood 1993) — *"how do microexperiences come together to yield homogeneous macroexperiences, such as a homogeneous experience of red, instead of an enormous jagged array of distinct qualities?"*

Dainton (2011) calls the palette problem the **"derivation problem."**

### The subject-summing problem

Verbatim: *"Given 101 subjects, it seems that the existence of the first 100 does not necessitate the existence of the 101st. More generally, given any group of subjects and any further subject, it seems possible in principle for the first group of subjects to exist without the further subject. If so, then no group of microsubjects necessitates the existence of a macrosubject."*

Formalized (following Goff 2009):

> (1) If constitutive panpsychism is true, the existence of a number of microsubjects with certain experiences necessitates the existence of a distinct macrosubject.
> (2) It is never the case that the existence of a number of subjects with certain experiences necessitates the existence of a distinct subject.
> ∴ (3) Constitutive panpsychism is false.

**Premise (2) is the key, and Chalmers backs it with a conceivability argument**: for any group of subjects, it is conceivable that those subjects exist with their experiences and no other subjects exist; conceivability entails possibility; therefore possible. **His assessment of the panpsychist's position here is pointed:** *"conceivability/possibility claims can be rejected, but not without incurring substantial costs, and **panpsychists who have rejected physicalism in part on the basis of conceivability arguments are not in a good position to do so**."*

**This generalizes to the panpsychist zombie argument.** Let PP = all microphysical and microphenomenal truths, Q = "some macroscopic entity is conscious." Then: `PP & ¬Q` is conceivable; if conceivable, metaphysically possible; if possible, constitutive panpsychism is false. *"Such a world is populated by **panpsychist zombies**, which are microphysical and microphenomenal duplicates of us without consciousness."* **Goff (2009) calls these "micro-experiential zombies."** **The force of this is that it is exactly the argument panpsychists use against physicalism, turned on them.**

### The palette problem

Verbatim: *"There is a vast array of macroqualities, including many different phenomenal colors, shapes, sounds, smells, and tastes. There is presumably only a limited palette of microqualities. **Especially if Russellian panpsychism is true, we can expect only a handful of microqualities, corresponding to the handful of fundamental microphysical properties.** How can this limited palette of microqualities combine to yield the vast array of macroqualities?"*

Chalmers' earlier phrasing: *"How is it that the richly painted canvas of human experiences is produced from such a small palette of paints?"* **The dilemma he sets out:** *small-palette* solutions are *"very much subject to the previous problem of how quality combination works"*; *large-palette* solutions either multiply fundamental properties implausibly or are *"stuck with either a form of"* the same problem.

### The structural mismatch problem

Verbatim: *"Macrophysical structure (in the brain, say) seems entirely different from the macrophenomenal structure we experience. Microexperiences presumably have structure closely corresponding to microphysical structure (this is especially clear on a Russellian view), and we might expect a combination of them to yield something akin to macrophysical structure. How do these combine to yield macrophenomenal structure instead?"*

**Formalized as an apparently inconsistent tetrad — the most elegant statement of the problem in the literature:**

> (1) Microphenomenal structure is isomorphic to microphysical structure. *[a commitment of Russellian panpsychism]*
> (2) Microphenomenal structure constitutes macrophenomenal structure. *[a commitment of constitutive panpsychism]*
> (3) Microphysical structure constitutes macrophysical structure. *[widely accepted]*
> (4) Macrophenomenal structure is **not** isomorphic to macrophysical structure. *[the datum]*

**Chalmers' own view on which is the live threat:** *"if we are looking to either solve the combination problem or to prove it unsolvable, **I think the structural mismatch problem is a promising place to focus**."* And: *"it is clear that the structural mismatch argument is a significant challenge that all Russellian monists must answer."*

### The introspection argument (via Lockwood 1993, Goff 2006)

> (1) The nature of consciousness is revealed to us in introspection.
> (2) If constitutive panpsychism is correct, consciousness is constituted by a vast array of microexperiences.
> (3) Whatever constitutes consciousness is part of its nature.
> (4) A vast array of microexperiences is not revealed to us in introspection.
> ∴ (5) Constitutive panpsychism is incorrect.

Chalmers: *"Premise (1) is not compulsory, and most materialists will deny it. But… **For panpsychists who argue in this way, it is an uncomfortable premise to deny.** Premises (2) and (4) are also hard to deny."* **The dialectical shape is the same as the subject-summing argument: the panpsychist's own epistemology (revelation through introspection) is what traps them.**

### Chalmers' conclusion — the strongest available statement of the difficulty, from a sympathizer

> *"It is common for a proposed solution to the combination problem to address only one of these problems: most often the subject combination problem and occasionally the quality combination problem. **It should be stressed that a satisfactory solution to the combination problem must address all of these problems. This raises the bar for a solution, as it is far from clear that any single proposal can solve all the problems at once.** One might appeal to separate proposals for solving the problems one at a time, but then it is far from clear that these proposals will be compatible with each other."*
>
> *"After a close analysis of the many aspects of the combination problem and the limited resources for solving them, **it is easy to be pessimistic about the prospects for a solution**. … It is hard enough to find a theory of consciousness that works on dualist terms, where we are allowed to take macrosubjects and macrophenomenal properties as primitive and appeal to numerous contingent psychophysical laws. The Russellian monist is constrained to find a theory whereby macroexperience is constituted by a tiny range of underlying primitive properties and without any further contingent fundamental laws. **This is a little like trying to juggle seven balls in the air with both hands tied behind one's back.**"*
>
> *"**It may be that the constraints imposed by the combination problem are so strong that the challenge cannot be answered.** Or it may just be that trying to satisfy the constraints will point someone toward the correct form for a fundamental theory of consciousness."*

### The strong form: summing is impossible, not merely unexplained

**Sam Coleman, "The Real Combination Problem: Panpsychism, Micro-Subjects, and Emergence," *Erkenntnis* 79(1):19–44**, doi:10.1007/s10670-013-9431-x (**Crossref cited-by 81**). ⚠️ Crossref publication date 2013-01-20; SEP dates it 2014 — **the standard online-first/issue split.**

Per SEP §4.3: *"In its stronger form the subject-summing problem takes the form of an **argument to the conclusion that subject-summing is incoherent or impossible**, and hence that panpsychism—or at least constitutive micropsychism—must be false. Sam Coleman (2014), for example, …"* **Coleman's core move, per SEP: each subject's point of view *excludes* all others, so subjects cannot coherently combine.** (Coleman's own positive view is **panqualityism** — he drops micro-*subjects* precisely to dodge this.) Also Coleman, "Mental Chemistry: Combination for Panpsychists," *Dialectica* 66(1):137–166, 2012.

### Where panprotopsychism is and isn't better off

Chalmers: *"The structure and quality combination problems seem just as hard in this guise. The subject combination problem will take a different form, one that perhaps makes it slightly easier. Although microexperiences presumably have subjects, protoexperiences need not."* **But it incurs a *new* problem:** *"**how can nonexperiences constitute experiences?**"* His own assessment is unusually even-handed: *"Sometimes it is flatly asserted that this is impossible… I do not think that this is obviously correct… Still, there is at least a significant challenge for the panprotopsychist here."* **Net verdict: *"I conclude that both panpsychism and panprotopsychism suffer from serious combination problems."***

**All of these are live proposals; none has "gained widespread acceptance" (SEP).**

## The objection catalogue

### The incredulous stare — is it an argument?

The phrase is borrowed from **David Lewis, *On the Plurality of Worlds*, 1986**, where Lewis concedes the incredulous stare is the main reaction to modal realism and argues it is not a refutation. ⚠️ **The page reference was not verified; treat the attribution as standard but unchecked.** Goff's SEP §4.1 is titled "The Incredulous Stare."

**The panpsychist reply, verbatim from SEP (Goff):**

> *"Presumably these scientific theories are taken so seriously in spite of their strangeness because they are supported by empirical evidence. However, **most arguments for panpsychism start from a datum which is known with greater certainty than the data of observation and experiments: the existence of human consciousness**… **There are always an infinite number of theories consistent with the data, and we must choose between them on the basis of theoretical virtues, such as parsimony and simplicity**… Similarly, **we should assess panpsychism on the grounds of its theoretical virtue and explanatory power**, rather than the fact that many people find it strange."*

Chalmers concurs: *"Panpsychism is sometimes dismissed as a crazy view, but this reaction on its own is not a serious objection. While the view is counterintuitive to some, **there is good reason to think that any view of consciousness must embrace some counterintuitive conclusions.**"* And: *"It is true that we do not have much direct evidence for panpsychism, but we also do not have much direct evidence against it, given the difficulties of detecting the presence or absence of consciousness in other systems. **And there are indirect reasons, of a broadly theoretical character, for taking the view seriously.**"*

**Verdict: no, the stare is not an argument, and essentially everyone on both sides concedes this.** Strawson, reviewing *Galileo's Error* in the *Guardian*, 27 Dec 2019: *"'First, you know, a new theory is attacked as absurd,' William James once remarked… **Panpsychism is not a new theory, but it is newly popular, and it is still widely held to be 'absurd'. It remains to be seen whether it will ever advance to 'obvious'.**"*

**But note the real dispute underneath: the panpsychist's substitute criterion is *parsimony*, and that is attackable on its own terms** — see **Henry Taylor, "Is panpsychism simple?", *Analysis* 79(2):265–275**, doi:10.1093/analys/any049, which goes directly at the simplicity argument. ⚠️ Crossref cited-by = 1 — **low uptake; flag it.**

**A structurally similar but sharper objection, since it *is* an argument rather than a stare:** Aaronson's "paradigm cases" argument against IIT is the general form — **if a theory's verdicts on the paradigm cases are wrong, the theory has failed at the only task that could have validated it, and the right response is to conclude the theorist means something else by the word.** That argument applies to panpsychism as readily as to IIT, and **it is the respectable version of the incredulous stare.**

### "It doesn't explain anything / it relocates the mystery"

⚠️ **The strongest version of this objection was published by Philip Goff — against panpsychism — before he became its leading defender.** **Goff, "Why Panpsychism Doesn't Help us Explain Consciousness," *Dialectica* 63(3):289–311, 2009**, doi:10.1111/j.1746-8361.2009.01196.x (**Crossref cited-by 61**). Abstract, verbatim:

> *"This paper starts from the assumption that panpsychism is counterintuitive and metaphysically demanding. A number of philosophers, whilst not denying these negative aspects of the view, think that panpsychism has in its favour that it offers a good explanation of consciousness. **In opposition to this, the paper argues that panpsychism cannot help us to explain consciousness, at least not the kind of consciousness we have pre-theoretical reason to believe in.**"*

**Goff's later answer to himself:** "How Exactly Does Panpsychism Help Explain Consciousness?", *JCS* 31(3):56–82, 2024, doi:10.53765/20512201.31.3.056. ⚠️ Citation verified; content not obtained. Also "Experiences don't sum," *JCS* 13(6):53–61, 2006; "Against Constitutive Panpsychism," in Alter & Nagasawa 2015:370–400.

**Keith Frankish**, "Why panpsychism fails to solve the mystery of consciousness," ***Aeon*, 20 September 2016** (republished by *The Atlantic* the same day as "Why Panpsychism Is Probably Wrong"; ⚠️ that Atlantic URL is Cloudflare-blocked — quote the Aeon original). The prongs, verbatim:

> *"**Panpsychism offers no distinctive predictions or explanations. It finds a place for consciousness in the physical world, but that place is a sort of limbo.**"*
> *"panpsychism gives consciousness a curious status. It places it at the very heart of every physical entity yet **threatens to render it explanatorily idle**. For the behaviour of subatomic particles and the systems they constitute promises to be fully explained by physics and the other physical sciences."*
> *"consciousness is an unlikely candidate for this fundamental property. For, so far as our evidence goes, **it is a highly localised phenomenon that is specific not only to brains but to particular states of brains**… It appears to be a specific state of certain highly complex information-processing systems, not a basic feature of the Universe."*
> *"**Consciousness is indeed a hard nut to crack, but I think we should exhaust the other options before we take a metaphysical sledgehammer to it.**"*

Frankish also gives the combination problem in its most vivid popular form: *"If billions of humans organised themselves to form a giant brain, each person simulating a single neuron and sending signals to the others using mobile phones, it seems unlikely that their consciousnesses would merge to form a single giant consciousness. Why should something similar happen with subatomic particles?"* Plus a **boundary prong**: *"Are there any intermediate-level conscious subjects (molecules, crystals, plants?)… It's hard to see why subjecthood should be restricted to just subatomic particles and higher animals, but equally hard to think of any non-arbitrary way of extending the category."*

**Amy Kind — the "unmotivated" charge.** **Kind, "Pessimism about Russellian Monism," in Alter & Nagasawa (eds.) 2015, pp. 401–421.** As stated in the SEP Russellian Monism entry: Russellian monism *"fails to resolve the most central issue in the debate: whether consciousness is a fundamental component of the universe. On Russellian panpsychism consciousness is a fundamental component of the universe, whereas on Russellian panprotopsychism it is not. Thus, Russellian monism leaves us '**essentially back where we started**' (Kind 2015: 420)."* **The reply (Alter & Coleman 2020a):** the charge *"misunderstands Russellian monism's aims… the theory is meant to provide a **framework** in which that issue (and others) can be more productively addressed."* ⚠️ **The "back where we started" quote is sourced via SEP, not from Kind's chapter, which could not be obtained.** Earlier Kind: "Panexperientialism, Cognition, and the Nature of Experience," *Psyche* 12, 2006.

**Sean Carroll — the physics-side version, and the strongest form of the causal objection.** **Carroll, "Consciousness and the Laws of Physics," *JCS* 28(9):16–31, 2021**, doi:10.53765/20512201.28.9.016 (cited-by 24), written for the Goff & Moran symposium on *Galileo's Error*. Blog version: "The Zombie Argument for Physicalism (Contra Panpsychism)," Preposterous Universe, 17 Nov 2021. Verbatim:

> *"the dilemma facing the non-physicalist about consciousness is the following: either your theory of consciousness keeps the dynamics of the Core Theory intact within its domain of applicability, or it doesn't. There aren't any other options! I emphasize this because **many non-physicalists are weirdly cagey about whether they're going to violate the Core Theory**. In our discussion, Philip suggested that one could rely on 'strong emergence' to create new kinds of behavior without really violating the CT. **You can't.** The fact that the CT is a local effective field theory completely rules out the possibility…"*
> *"when we sit down to compare P-world and Ψ-world, we realize that the purported 'consciousness' that is central to Ψ-world is **playing no explanatory role whatsoever**. It might be there, ineffably in the background, but it has no impact at all on what human beings do or say. As Keith put it in our conversation, **it offers no 'differential' explanatory power**…"*
> *"by sequestering off 'consciousness' from playing any causal role in the world, **you've turned it into something very different from what we were originally trying to explain.**"*
> On the "introspection is data" reply: *"**there is no 'data' at all, in the ordinary sense**… The lesson is that this kind of introspection is completely unreliable."*

**Note that Carroll's argument is a direct hit on the Russellian panpsychist's *best* selling point — that unlike dualism, panpsychism secures mental causation. Carroll's claim is that it doesn't: the phenomenal quiddity rides along without doing anything.** This matches a Russellian-monism objection SEP catalogues as **Howell (2015)**: quiddities may be *"just along for the causal ride."*

**John Searle — the hardest published dismissal, and the one that bites hardest on *individuation*.** **Searle, "Can Information Theory Explain Consciousness?", *The New York Review of Books*, 10 January 2013 issue** (a review of Christof Koch, *Consciousness: Confessions of a Romantic Reductionist*). Verbatim:

> *"My second objection is that the theory implies panpsychism, and pansychism is absurd for a reason I can explain briefly."* *(sic)*
> *"**Consciousness cannot be spread over the universe like a thin veneer of jam; there has to be a point where my consciousness ends and yours begins.** For people who accept panpsychism, who attribute consciousness, as Koch does, to the iPhone, the question is: Why the iPhone? Why not each part of it? Each microprocessor? Why not each molecule? Why not the whole communication system of which the iPhone is a part? **The problem with panpsychism is not that it is false; it does not get up to the level of being false. It is strictly speaking meaningless because no clear notion has been given to the claim. Consciousness comes in units and panpsychism cannot specify the units.**"*

⚠️ **Note: this is Searle's *Koch review*, not the 1997 Chalmers exchange. That Searle's 1997 NYRB exchange with Chalmers addresses panpsychism is unverified.**

**This is the single most useful quotation for linking panpsychism to IIT**, because the "specify the units" demand is *exactly* what IIT's exclusion postulate claims to answer — **and Tononi & Koch quote the "thin veneer of jam" line themselves and cite it as the problem IIT solves.**

**Tononi & Koch's version, from the IIT side.** *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. B* 370(1668):20140167, 2015, doi:10.1098/rstb.2014.0167. Verbatim:

> *"Unlike idealism, which does away with the physical world, or dualism, which accepts both in an uneasy marriage, **panpsychism is elegantly unitary**… **But panpsychism's beauty has been singularly barren. Besides claiming that matter and mind are one thing, it has little constructive to say and offers no positive laws explaining how the mind is organized and works.**"*
> *"**Unfortunately, panpsychism is mute when it comes to explaining the way any one conscious experience feels** — why the perception of red feels different from that of blue…"*
> *"by identifying consciousness with integrated information, IIT can account for why it evolved, **another aspect about which panpsychism has nothing to say**."*

**A named published review making the "replaced one gap with another" charge directly:** **Raymond Tallis, review of *Galileo's Error*, *Philosophy Now*, Issue 135** ⚠️ (Dec 2019/Jan 2020 — the cover date was not confirmed on the page):

> *"One manifestation of this puzzle is the so-called 'combination problem': 'How do you get from little conscious things… to big conscious things, like human brains?' **Here we seem to have replaced one explanatory gap with another at least as wide.**… He hints that quantum entanglement might provide a model for such unification, but is not able to indicate what is or might be distinctive about the brain that it uniquely makes use of such entanglement. **So long as this 'emergentist' model lacks details, it is only a promissory note.**"*

Tallis also presses an **internal-inconsistency charge** about the final chapter: appealing to *"objective, quantitative science to demonstrate the presence of consciousness is at odds with the very thesis on which his argument hinges… **To think so is to fall victim to the very Galilean error that provides the title of his book.**"* His overall verdict is mixed, not hostile: *"this lucid, reader-friendly book is highly recommended. You may, like me, disagree with its conclusion…"*

**Daniel Stoljar — verified citations, but the argument itself was not read.** Stoljar, "Two Conceptions of the Physical," *PPR* 62(2):253–281, 2001, doi:10.1111/j.1933-1592.2001.tb00056.x — **Chalmers credits it directly:** *"I owe a special debt to Daniel Stoljar whose related work in 'Two Conceptions of the Physical' greatly influenced this article."* The one to cite for his critique is **Stoljar, "Russellian Monism or Nagelian Monism?", in Alter & Nagasawa 2015:324–345.** Also "Four Kinds of Russellian Monism," in Kriegel (ed.) 2014:17–39; *Ignorance and Imagination*, OUP 2006. ⚠️ **His text was not obtained — the citations and SEP's characterization are all that is verified.**

**Also catalogued in SEP Russellian Monism:** the **revenge problem** for panprotopsychism (parallel considerations may undermine the claim that protophenomenal + structural truths a priori entail all phenomenal truths); and objections that the structural/non-structural distinction cannot be explicated as Russellian monism requires.

⚠️ **Further SEP-catalogued objections, names only (arguments not obtained):** Albahari's "Inner-Outer Gap Problem"; Aleksiev's "Missing Entities Problem"; Schneider and Dainton on time and photons; Blaesi's moral parody argument; Papineau's causal argument against emergentist versions.

### Testability

**The panpsychists' own position is that it is not directly testable, and they say so plainly.** The cleanest statement: Gareth Cook interviewing Philip Goff, "Does Consciousness Pervade the Universe?", ***Scientific American*, 14 January 2020** (print: *SA Mind* 31(2), March 2020, p. 13, doi:10.1038/scientificamericanmind0320-3). Verbatim:

> **Q:** *"Do you foresee a scenario in which panpsychism can be tested?"*
> **Goff:** *"You can't look inside an electron to see whether or not it is conscious, just as you can't look inside someone's head and see their feelings and experiences. We know that consciousness exists only because we are conscious. Neuroscientists correlate certain kinds of brain activity with certain kinds of experience… what we ultimately want from a science of consciousness is an explanation of those correlations. Why is a particular feeling correlated with a particular pattern of brain activity? **As soon as you start to answer this question, you move beyond what can be, strictly speaking, tested, simply because consciousness is unobservable. We have to turn to philosophy.** Science gives us correlations between brain activity and experience. We then have to find the philosophical theory that best explains those correlations. In my view, the only theory that holds up to scrutiny is panpsychism."*

**The substitute standard is inference to the best explanation plus theoretical virtues**, with the Einstein-over-Lorentz analogy as the model of a theory choice made on simplicity rather than new data. Goff's Aeon 2017 headline thesis: *"**I maintain that there is a powerful simplicity argument in favour of panpsychism**… Panpsychism is crazy. But it is also highly likely to be true."* (Philip Goff, "Panpsychism is crazy, but it's also most probably true," *Aeon*, **1 March 2017** — Goff's own retrospective note says this essay led directly to *Galileo's Error* two years later.)

**The secondary move is to frame panpsychism as a *research programme*, not a finished theory.** SEP (Goff), verbatim:

> *"**The general consensus among panpsychists is that there is currently no wholly adequate solution to the combination problem. However, this is not obviously a reason to think the panpsychist research project is not worth pursuing.** Compare: it was only many decades after Darwin and Wallace formulated the principle of natural selection that modern genetics developed… **It is only recently that panpsychism is once again being taken seriously by contemporary philosophers and some neuroscientists (e.g., Tononi & Koch 2015). If it does eventually produce a perfectly adequate account of human consciousness and of the nature of matter, this will no doubt only be after many decades or centuries of serious inter-disciplinary work.**"*

SEP also points to **Roelofs (2021)** for responses to the "panpsychism is unscientific" intuition. IEP states the objection and reply compactly: the "Not Testable / No Signs" objection grants *"an epistemological gap but denies that it proves an ontological one."*

**Critics who treat untestability as fatal.** Frankish (*"no distinctive predictions or explanations… a sort of limbo"*), Searle (*"does not get up to the level of being false… strictly speaking meaningless"*), Carroll (*"no explanatory role whatsoever"*), Tononi & Koch (*"singularly barren… no positive laws"*), plus:

- **Massimo Pigliucci**, *Figs in Winter* (Substack): *"Sabine also wrote a no-nonsense rebuttal of a recent pernicious fashion in philosophy, the **entirely not empirically verifiable notion of 'panpsychism'**"* ("I very much doubt the universe thinks," **13 March 2023**); and *"if a theory or hypothesis about any aspect of the world is not empirically verifiable then it is **not even wrong**, in the sense that it is useless in terms of advancing human knowledge. **Examples would include dualism in metaphysics, panpsychism**, and of course any invocation of the supernatural"* ("Five allegedly bad 'isms' – Part II," **16 July 2026**).
- **Anil Seth**, "The hard problem of consciousness is a distraction from the real one," *Aeon*, 2016: *"Tononi… argues that consciousness simply *is* integrated information. This is an intriguing and powerful proposal, but it comes **at the cost of admitting that consciousness could be present everywhere and in everything, a philosophical view known as panpsychism**. The additional mathematical contortions needed also mean that, in practice, integrated information becomes impossible to measure for any real complex system. **This is an instructive example of how targeting the hard problem, rather than the real problem, can slow down or even stop experimental progress.**"* Also relevant: Gómez-Marin & Seth, "A science of consciousness beyond pseudo-science and pseudo-consciousness," *Nature Neuroscience* 28(4):703–706, 2025, doi:10.1038/s41593-025-01913-6.
- **The 2023 IIT open letter is also, explicitly, an anti-panpsychism document.** "The Integrated Information Theory of Consciousness as Pseudoscience," PsyArXiv, **16 September 2023**, doi:10.31234/osf.io/zsr78: *"These claims have been widely considered untestable, unscientific, 'magicalist', or a 'departure from science as we know it'. **Given its panpsychist commitments, until the theory as a whole—not just some hand-picked auxiliary components trivially shared by many others or already known to be true—is empirically testable, we feel that the pseudoscience label should indeed apply.**"* Signatories include **Patricia S. Churchland, Daniel C. Dennett, Keith Frankish, Peter Carruthers, Hakwan Lau, Stephen M. Fleming, Chris D. Frith, Joseph LeDoux, Yoshua Bengio.** First ten drafting authors: Fleming, Frith, Goodale, Lau, LeDoux, Lee, Michel, Owen, Peters, Slagter. ⚠️ **Anil Seth is NOT a signatory.** The letter self-describes as *"intended as a 'Letter to the Editor'-type of article… this is not the final version."*

**Nothing found, after dedicated search, claims panpsychism *per se* generates novel empirical predictions. Every source reached that addresses the question — including the panpsychists — says the opposite.**

**Population and response.** Fielded **15 October – 16 November 2020**. Target population **7,685 philosophers**: 6,112 in group (1), *"all regular faculty members (tenure-track or permanent) in BA-granting philosophy departments with four or more members"* in Australia, Canada, Ireland, New Zealand, the UK and the US; 1,573 in group (2), *"English-publishing philosophers in BA-granting philosophy departments with four or more English-publishing faculty members"* elsewhere. **"Of the main target population of 7685 philosophers, 1785 (23%) completed the survey."** Of those: 522 completed exactly 50 questions, 925 all 100, 338 answered 51–99; a further 487 consented but did not complete. Every member was emailed an invitation plus up to two reminders.

**Structure.** 40 main questions asked of everyone; **60 additional questions, each asked of one-sixth of participants as part of their mandatory 50**, with the rest optional. Per the paper: *"Of the 60 additional questions, one-sixth of the 1785 respondents, or about 300 respondents, were presented with the question as part of their mandatory 50 questions. Typically, another 800 respondents (45%) were presented with the question by answering additional questions, for a total of around 1100 respondents (62%)."* **Consciousness was an *additional*, not a main, question.** Separate **"2009-comparable departments"** group: 100 departments (PhD-granting, 2017–18 Philosophical Gourmet Report score ≥1.9, plus two leading MA departments and selected European departments), **2,407 philosophers, 648 (27%) completed.** Ethics approval: The Western University Non-Medical Research Ethics Board.

⚠️ **Specialist sub-groups, secondary source only:** philosophers of mind (n=95) **panpsychism 10%**; philosophers of cognitive science (n=43) **panpsychism 2%**. These come from LessWrong's transcription of the survey website, because `survey2020.philpeople.org` returns Cloudflare 403. **The 10%-vs-2% split is striking and worth repeating only with that caveat.** ⚠️ Option order may or may not have been randomised per respondent — not confirmable.

**Mind: physicalism vs non-physicalism.** 2020, Table 1 (broad population): physicalism **900 / 51.9%**; non-physicalism **556 / 32.1%**; other 276 / 15.9%. **But the paper warns against comparing these to 2009** (§3.1), for three reasons: the 2020 population is much broader; multiple endorsement was easier; and the 2009 published figures included skips and "insufficiently familiar." **The like-for-like comparison is Table 18, "Comparable departments" (09% → 20%): physicalism 56.5 → 57.4; non-physicalism 27.1 → 25.8**; other 16.4 → 16.8. "Same people": physicalism 61.1 → 59.3; non-physicalism 24.3 → 24.9. **So physicalism is essentially flat; the 51.9% headline is a population effect, not a trend.** ⚠️ The up/down arrow glyphs in Table 18 did not survive HTML-to-text conversion; read direction off the pairs.

**Largest swings 2009→2020 overall (Table 8) do not involve mind/consciousness**: knowledge claims invariantism −7.5, logic non-classical +6.7, knowledge claims contextualism +5.1, moral motivation externalism +4.4, a priori knowledge yes +4.3, laws of nature Humean +4.3, free will compatibilism +3.5.

**2009: there was no panpsychism option and no consciousness question.** Citation: Bourget & Chalmers, "What do philosophers believe?", *Philosophical Studies* **170(3):465–500, 2014**, doi:10.1007/s11098-013-0259-7. **The complete 30-question list contains no consciousness question; the word "conscious" does not appear in it or in the Springer appendix.** The nearest datum is Mind: physicalism **56.5 ± 1.5%** (accept 34.6, lean 21.9) / non-physicalism **27.1 ± 1.1%** (accept 14.2, lean 12.9) / other 16.4%, from **931 target faculty participants across 99 departments**. ⚠️ **The number *invited* in 2009 (commonly cited as 1,972) is unverified from any primary source. Don't assert it.**

**The best quotable gloss on what the number means, from a panpsychist.** Philip Goff, "Materialism Remains the Majority View…But Only Just!", *Conscience and Consciousness*, **1 Nov 2021**:

> *"**panpsychism did not even feature in the 2009 survey: it's gone from being a view that hardly anyone took seriously option to being one of the standard options to consider.**"* *(sic — ungrammatical in the original)*
> *"Among those who accept/lean towards non-physicalism, approximately 3/4 are dualists and 1/4 are panpsychists."*
> *"the only view of consciousness less popular than panpsychism was eliminitivism, the view of Daniel Dennett and others that consciousness doesn't really exist."* *(sic)*

⚠️ **And the tension worth flagging prominently.** Goff, same post: on the separate "Other Minds" question, *"**only 2% accepted/leaned towards particles being conscious**, a much lower percentage than accept/lean towards panpsychism in the 'Consciousness' question. I suspect this is because when addressing the 'Other Minds' question, people were generally considering what is suggested by the empirical data concerning the neural correlates of consciousness, whereas when addressing the 'Consciousness' question, people are giving their opinion on the best solution to the hard problem."*

**So: 7.5% endorse "panpsychism" as a position on consciousness, but only 2% will say particles are conscious. That is a ~4× gap in the same survey, and it suggests "panpsychism" is being selected partly as a label for *non-physicalism-that-isn't-dualism* — plausibly including Russellian monists, who had no separate option — rather than as the literal micro-experience claim.** ⚠️ The 2% is from Goff's blog, secondary; the survey site was unreachable.

## Live or fringe? The bibliometric and institutional evidence

**Verdict: a live minority position with genuine institutional infrastructure, emphatically not fringe in the sense of being outside the discipline — but a small minority, and more popular with non-experts than experts.**

**For "live":**

- Its own **SEP** entry (rev. 2022) and a separate SEP entry on **Russellian Monism** (rev. 2023). SEP's opening line: panpsychism *"has a long and venerable history… and **has recently enjoyed a revival in analytic philosophy**."*
- Major-press and university-press books: Goff 2017 (OUP, 254 citations), Goff 2019 (Pantheon/Rider), Seager (ed.) *Routledge Handbook* (2019), Brüntrup & Jaskolla (eds.) (OUP 2016, 60 citations), Alter & Nagasawa (eds.) (OUP 2015), Roelofs *Combining Minds* (OUP 2019, 63 citations), Mørch's Cambridge Element (2023).
- A dedicated **JCS special issue** (28(9–10), 2021), the 2006 Strawson JCS symposium plus replies volume, and a dedicated **JCS critical symposium on IIT** (26(1–2), 2019).
- **Conference infrastructure documented in Chalmers' own footnotes:** *"Panpsychism on the Reef,"* Lady Elliot Island, **July 2012**; the **Oslo** panpsychism conference, August 2013; the **Munich** conference on panpsychism and emergence, June 2011; the **2013 Amherst Lecture in Philosophy.**
- ⚠️ **NDPR reviewers' own framing** (search-result first sentences only; the review pages returned 403 to every user agent tried, so **reviewer names are unknown** for both): *"**Panpsychism is hot stuff.** Since Strawson's (2006) provocative argument (accompanied by a swathe of high-profile replies)…"* and *"**Panpsychism is trending.** If you're not a panpsychist, you might find this puzzling…"*
- **Frankish, a critic, concedes the standing:** panpsychism *"might sound like New Age mysticism, but some hard-nosed analytic philosophers have suggested it might be how things are, and **it's now a hot topic in philosophy of mind**"*; *"**there is a lively ongoing discussion of the problems it faces** and the best way to respond to them in contemporary philosophical books and journals."*

**Bibliometric evidence.** Crossref, `query.title=panpsychism`, year facet, retrieved 2026-11-11; **401 total results**:

⚠️ **Caveat: `query.title` is a fuzzy relevance query, not an exact title-contains filter, so this is indicative of a trend, not a census.** The step change is nonetheless sharp — **roughly 3–4× the pre-2016 baseline** — **with the 2019 peak (36) coinciding with the Routledge Handbook and *Galileo's Error*.**

**Crossref cited-by counts, retrieved 2026-11-11:** Goff *Consciousness and Fundamental Reality* (2017) = **254**; Chalmers "The Combination Problem" (2016) = **92**; Coleman "The Real Combination Problem" (2013) = **81**; Goff "Why Panpsychism Doesn't Help Us Explain Consciousness" (2009) = **61**; Roelofs *Combining Minds* (2019) = **63**; Brüntrup & Jaskolla (2016) = **60**; Strawson "Realistic Monism" (2008 reprint) = **47**; Seager (ed.) *Routledge Handbook* = **24**; Carroll "Consciousness and the Laws of Physics" (2021) = **24**; **Taylor "Is panpsychism simple?" = 1.**

**For "dismissed":** Searle (the hardest), Frankish, Carroll, Pigliucci, Seth, Tononi & Koch, Taylor on the simplicity argument — plus the collective signal of the 2023 IIT letter, which bundles panpsychism into a pseudoscience charge and carries Churchland's, Dennett's and Frankish's signatures.

**And the datum that cuts hardest against any "leading theory" framing.** **Michel M. et al., "An Informal Internet Survey on the Current State of Consciousness Science," *Frontiers in Psychology* 9:2134, 2018**, plus **Yeung, Cushing & Lee, "A bibliometric evaluation of the impact of theories of consciousness in academia and on social media," *Consciousness and Cognition* 100:103296, 2022.** Cushing et al. summarize: *"**non-experts view IIT as the theory of consciousness most likely to be true, while it ranks near the bottom among experts in the field.**"* **That is about IIT, not panpsychism, but the 10%-among-philosophers-of-mind vs 2%-among-philosophers-of-cognitive-science split points the same way.**
