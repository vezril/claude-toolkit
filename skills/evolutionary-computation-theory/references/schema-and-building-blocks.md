### 1. HOLLAND'S SCHEMA THEOREM

### PRIMARY SOURCE

John H. Holland, *Adaptation in Natural and Artificial Systems*, University of Michigan Press 1975; 2nd ed. MIT Press 1992. (Book; no DOI. Bibliographic details are standard and corroborated by every source below; I did not read the book itself — flag page references as coming from secondary sources.)

DEFINITIONS (verified against two independent sources — Altenberg 1995 FOGA 3, and the notation section of White's schema-theory survey, arXiv:1401.2651):
- A schema H is a string of length l over {0,1,*}, where * is a "don't care" wildcard. A bitstring x is an *instance* of H (x in H) iff it matches H in every non-* position. A schema denotes a hyperplane / subset of {0,1}^l.
- Order o(H) = the number of fixed (non-*) positions.
- Defining length d(H) = the distance between the first and last fixed positions = the number of one-point-crossover cut points that can disrupt H. The ratio d(H)/(l-1) is sometimes called the schema's *fragility*.
- m(H,t) = the number of instances of H in the population at generation t.
- f(H,t) = the average observed fitness of the population members matching H at generation t. fbar(t) = the average fitness of the whole population at t.
- Counts: there are 3^l schemata but 2^(2^l) subsets of the search space, so most subsets are NOT schemata. One string of length l matches 2^l schemata; a population of n strings has instances of between 2^l and n*2^l distinct schemata.

### THE INEQUALITY

Two forms circulate and both are legitimate; the difference is whether mutation survival is kept exact or linearised.

(a) Multiplicative / exact-mutation form (verified verbatim, Theorem 1 of White, "An Overview of Schema Theory", arXiv:1401.2651, which states it is "slightly more general than Holland's original version because it factors in mutation fully rather than assuming p_m is approximately zero"):

  E[m(H,t+1)] >= m(H,t) * f(H,t)/fbar(t) * [1 - p_c*d(H)/(l-1)] * (1 - p_m)^o(H)

(b) Linearised form, as usually quoted from Goldberg 1989 (first-order expansion of (1-p_m)^o(H) ~= 1 - o(H)*p_m, valid for small p_m):

  E[m(H,t+1)] >= m(H,t) * f(H,t)/fbar(t) * [1 - p_c*d(H)/(l-1) - o(H)*p_m]

CONDITIONS, stated precisely. Altenberg 1995 (FOGA 3, p. 24) gives Holland's classical version as holding "In a genetic algorithm using a proportional selection algorithm and single point crossover occurring with probability p_c". The full list:

1. Fitness-proportionate (roulette-wheel) selection — this is what produces the f(H,t)/fbar(t) factor. The theorem does not hold in this form for tournament, rank, or truncation selection.

2. One-point crossover applied with probability p_c, with the cut point uniform over the l-1 internal positions. This gives the disruption probability p_c*d(H)/(l-1).

3. Independent per-bit mutation with probability p_m; survival of all o(H) fixed bits has probability (1-p_m)^o(H).

4. Non-overlapping generations, random mating, and (in the dynamical-systems version) infinite population. Altenberg's "canonical GA" is explicitly "discrete, non-overlapping generations, frequency-independent selection, and infinite population size".
5. f(H,t) is the *observed* average fitness of H's representatives in the current population, not the static average of H over the whole search space. This distinction is load-bearing and is where most misreadings happen.

6. The >= is there because Holland counted only *destructive* effects of crossover and mutation and pessimistically assumed every crossover inside the defining length destroys H. It ignores *constructive* effects (crossover creating new instances of H). White: "The proof is an exercise in elementary probability. The result is an inequality because Holland only factored in the destructive effects of crossover".

### WHAT IT IS AND IS NOT

It is a one-generation expectation bound, a lower bound on an expected count. It is NOT a long-run dynamical result. The standard criticisms (White attributes this list to Whitley 1993; the published source in the same vein is Darrell Whitley, "Deception, dominance and implicit parallelism in genetic search", Annals of Mathematics and AI 5(1):49-78, 1992, DOI 10.1007/BF01530780 — VERIFIED via Crossref):
- It cannot be iterated recursively: it is an inequality over an expectation, so compounding it over t generations loses information at every step and the bound degrades to vacuity.
- f(H,t) is itself a random variable that changes as selection biases the population, so the right-hand side is only meaningful for the first few generations. "The average fitness of a schema is only relevant in the first few generations of a run."
- Exact versions exist and make the equality precise. VERIFIED: Theorem 3 ("Exact Schema Theorem for Genetic Algorithms") in White's survey: E[m(H,t+1)] = n*alpha(H,t) where
    alpha(H,t) = (1-p_c)*p(H,t) + (p_c/l) * SUM_{i=0}^{l-1} p(L(H,i),t)*p(R(H,i),t)
  with p(H,t) the selection probability of H and L(H,i), R(H,i) the left/right truncations of H at position i. Credit: Christopher R. Stephens & Henri Waelbroeck, "Schemata Evolution and Building Blocks", Evolutionary Computation 7(2):109-124, 1999, DOI 10.1162/evco.1999.7.2.109 (arXiv nlin/0006048) — VERIFIED via OpenAlex; and Riccardo Poli's exact schema theorems for GP, 2000-2001. Also Alden H. Wright, "The Exact Schema Theorem", arXiv:1105.3538, 2011 — VERIFIED via OpenAlex.
- The theorem extends to *arbitrary subsets* of the search space, not just Holland schemata, and for arbitrary operators — which destroys the idea that schemata are privileged objects. VERIFIED in Altenberg 1995, who credits Grefenstette 1989 (FOGA 1), Radcliffe 1991 ("Equivalence class analysis of genetic algorithms", Complex Systems 5(2):183-205) and Vose 1991 ("Generalizing the notion of schema in genetic algorithms", Artificial Intelligence 50(3):385-396). Altenberg's own Theorem 4 ("Schema, Covariance Form") is explicitly stated for "any subset H of the search space".

### 2. IMPLICIT PARALLELISM AND THE O(n^3) CLAIM

TERMINOLOGY, VERIFIED: Holland originally called it "intrinsic parallelism" (Holland 1975, p. 74) and later renamed it "implicit parallelism" (Holland 1992). Source: Keki Burjorjee, "The Fundamental Problem with the Building Block Hypothesis", arXiv:0810.3356, 2008, which cites the page — I read this paper in full.

### THE CLAIM

A population of n strings can "usefully process" on the order of n^3 schemata per generation, i.e. that many schemata are present in enough copies to be statistically sampled and are short/low-order enough not to be disrupted. The argument, VERIFIED in White's survey (Proposition 1 and the paragraph after it):
- Let phi be the number of instances of a schema required before you call it "sampled" (a statistician's confidence parameter), and let theta be the highest schema order represented at that frequency. Then theta = log2(n/phi), and the number of schemata of order theta present in the population is 2^theta * C(l,theta) >= n^3.
- STATED RANGE OF VALIDITY (the crucial and usually omitted part): "This result holds when 64 <= n <= 2^20 and l >= 64." It is an arithmetic coincidence over a parameter window, not an asymptotic theorem.

### STATUS

contested; present the n^3 figure as folklore with a narrow numerical justification, not as a theorem. Key references, all VERIFIED bibliographically:

### 3. THE BUILDING-BLOCK HYPOTHESIS

### PRIMARY SOURCE

David E. Goldberg, *Genetic Algorithms in Search, Optimization, and Machine Learning*, Addison-Wesley, 1989. ISBN 0-201-15767-5. (Crossref has only the Choice Reviews record, DOI 10.5860/choice.27-0936; the book itself has no DOI.)
*** FLAG: I could NOT obtain the book and therefore could NOT verify Goldberg's exact wording. *** Do not present a verbatim Goldberg quote on my authority. The widely quoted p. 41 sentence — "Short, low order, and highly fit schemata are sampled, recombined, and resampled to form strings of potentially higher fitness" — is UNVERIFIED in this session.

What I CAN give verified:
- The standard formal statement, verbatim from White (arXiv:1401.2651): "Definition 2. A building block is a short, low order schema with above average fitness." / "Hypothesis 1 (Building Block Hypothesis). Good genetic algorithms combine building blocks to form better solutions."
- Mitchell, Holland & Forrest's own statement, VERIFIED verbatim from the NIPS paper I read in full (see section 5): "The Building Block Hypothesis (Holland, 1975/1992) states that the GA works well when instances of low-order, short schemas ('building blocks') that confer high fitness can be recombined to form instances of larger schemas that confer even higher fitness."
- The stronger version the critical literature actually attacks, verbatim from White: "Hypothesis 2 (Static Building Block Hypothesis). Given any schema partition, a genetic algorithm is expected to converge to the class with the best static average fitness." This formulation is Grefenstette's (1993) and is the one deception is defined against. FLAG: verified only from White's secondary account, not from Grefenstette's own text.
- Holland's metaphysical backing, VERIFIED verbatim via Burjorjee: Holland's "building block thesis" "holds that most of what we know about the world pivots on descriptions and mechanisms constructed from elementary building blocks", with building blocks being parts that "(i) must be easy to identify (once they've been discovered or picked out), and (ii) must be readily recombined to form a wide variety of structures".

### 4. THE CRITICISMS

### 4a. GREFENSTETTE, "DECEPTION CONSIDERED HARMFUL". VERIFIED via Crossref and OpenAlex: John J. Grefenstette, "Deception Considered Harmful" (with a footnote "With apologies to Edsger Dijkstra (Dijkstra, 1968)"), in L. Darrell Whitley (ed.), *Foundations of Genetic Algorithms 2* (FOGA 2), pp. 75-91, Morgan Kaufmann, 1993. DOI 10.1016/B978-0-08-094832-4.50011-8. 137 citations (OpenAlex). Also DTIC technical report ADA294072, DOI 10.21236/ada294072. Content UNVERIFIED — Elsevier, DTIC and OpenAlex all withheld the text; no abstract deposited. The argument, corroborated by the secondary literature (White, Burjorjee): the formal notion of "deception" presupposes the Static Building Block Hypothesis, which is false, because a GA does not sample schemata uniformly — it samples from a biased, evolving distribution. Consequently deception is neither necessary nor sufficient for GA-hardness: deceptive problems can be easy and non-deceptive problems can be hard. FLAG the "neither necessary nor sufficient" phrasing as my synthesis of the secondary literature, not a verified quotation.

### 4d. ALTENBERG AND PRICE'S THEOREM — the strongest single critique, and I have the full text.
VERIFIED: Lee Altenberg, "The Schema Theorem and Price's Theorem", in Darrell Whitley & Michael Vose (eds.), *Foundations of Genetic Algorithms 3* (FOGA 3), pp. 23-49, Morgan Kaufmann, San Francisco, 1995. No DOI. Full text at https://dynamics.org/Altenberg/FILES/LeeSTPT.pdf (fetched 2026-11-11; the reprint notes proof details for Theorem 5 were added to the appendix on 15 August 2001). Citation confirmed from Altenberg's own publication list at dynamics.org/Altenberg/PAPERS/.

Abstract, load-bearing sentence (verbatim): "dissenting arguments are reviewed and elaborated upon, explaining why the Schema Theorem has no implications for how well a GA is performing."

The argument, verbatim where quoted:
- The central claim: "The Schema Theorem, in short, does not address the search component of genetic algorithms on which performance depends, and cannot distinguish genetic algorithms that are performing well from those that are not."
- The hidden assumption: "The common interpretation of the Schema Theorem implicitly assumes that any member of an above-average schema is likely to produce offspring of above-average fitness, i.e. that there is a correlation between membership in an above-average schema and production of fitter offspring. But the existence of such correlations is logically independent of the validity of the Schema Theorem." He also skewers the usual gloss: "the phrase 'promising regions of the search space' is a construct through which hidden assumptions are introduced which are not implied by the Schema Theorem."
- THE NEEDLE-IN-A-HAYSTACK COUNTEREXAMPLE (verbatim, condensed): one genotype (the needle) has high fitness, all others are equally low. Once the needle is in the population it increases by selection while recombination "will most likely generate more 'hay'". "The Schema Theorem will still be seen to operate, in that short schemata with above-average fitness (those schemata containing the needle) will increase in frequency, even though the fitness of new instances of the schemata (more hay) will not be any more likely to have the high fitness of the needle."
- A second, sharper counterexample: take the fitness distribution to be the one-sided stable distribution of index 1/2 (Feller 1971), which generates needles in haystacks on all length scales. "A GA with this fitness function will generically have schemata that obey (10), even though it is still random search."
- THE TECHNICAL CONTRIBUTION. He uses Price's Covariance and Selection Theorem (George R. Price, 1970, 1972) as the general one-generation machinery, with a *measurement function* extracting a macroscopic population statistic. Choosing a schema indicator function yields the Schema Theorem; choosing a fitness-threshold indicator function yields what he calls the Local Performance Theorem (his Theorem 2), which governs the upper tail of the fitness distribution. His key structural point, verbatim: "the covariance term (from (13)), Cov[I(x,H), f(x)], and the covariance term (from (7)), Cov[T(...), f(x)], are independently defined. So conditions that produce growth in the frequencies of different schemata are independent of conditions that produce growth in the upper tails of the fitness distribution." Therefore: "the inability of the Schema Theorem to distinguish GA performance can be seen simply as the consequence of the measurement function that was chosen."
- Price-form schema theorem (his Corollary 4 / Theorem 4): the change in the frequency of any subset H decomposes into (i) linkage disequilibrium and (ii) the covariance between parental fitness and the proportion of offspring landing in H; and is bounded below by [Delta(H) + Cov(I(.,H), f)/fbar]*(1 - p_c*epsilon), where epsilon bounds schema disruption. "Therefore, if Cov(I(.,H), f)/fbar > ... then schema H will increase in frequency." He notes the two contributing sources explicitly: "1. linkage disequilibrium ... and 2. covariance between parental fitnesses and the proportion of their offspring in the schema."
- "SCHEMA PROCESSING IS NOT A REQUIREMENT": "In the process of deriving results that relate the parent-offspring correlations to the performance of the GA under a generalized transmission function, schemata disappear as pertinent entities. Therefore, 'schema processing' is not a requirement for performance in evolutionary algorithms in general."
- THE CONSTRUCTIVE HALF — the "missing" schema theorem (his Theorem 5, "Evolution of the fitness distribution under recombination"), derived using Hilda Geiringer's (1944) recombination-distribution representation. Schemata *reappear* when you specialise to recombination operators, and the theorem makes the BBH's real content explicit: "(1) that the recombination operator determines which schemata are being recombined; and (2) that there needs to be a correlation between complementary schemata of high fitness and the fitness distributions of their recombinant offspring in order for the GA to increase the chance of sampling fitter individuals." He describes this as giving "a quantitative expression to the Building Blocks Hypothesis". This matters rhetorically: Altenberg is not an anti-schema partisan; he reconstructs what the BBH would have to assert to be true.
- His positive proposal: use *evolvability* — "the ability to produce individuals fitter than any existing" — and the transmission function in the fitness domain, as the right performance statistic. Introduced in Altenberg 1994, "Emergent Phenomena in Genetic Programming", in Sebald & Fogel (eds.), *Proc. 3rd Annual Conference on Evolutionary Programming*, pp. 233-241, World Scientific. (No DOI; PDF at dynamics.org/Altenberg/FILES/Altenberg_EPIGP_1994.pdf.)

### 5. THE ROYAL ROAD STORY — the empirical refutation

This is the single best-documented failure of the BBH as a predictive claim, and I have the primary text.

R1, DEFINED EXACTLY (verbatim from the NIPS paper, Figure 1 and the text): l = 64. Eight order-8 schemata s_1..s_8, where s_i has ones in positions 8(i-1)+1 .. 8i and * everywhere else; each has coefficient c_i = 8. R1(x) = SUM_i c_i*delta_i(x) where delta_i(x) = 1 if x in s_i and 0 otherwise. "if x is an instance of exactly two of the order-8 schemas, R1(x) = 16. Likewise, R1(111...1) = 64."

THE EXPECTATIONS, in their own words: "Given this hypothesis, we initially expected that the building-block structure of R1 would lay out a 'royal road' for the GA to follow to the optimal string. We also expected that simple hill-climbing schemes would perform poorly since a large number of bit positions must be optimized simultaneously in order to move from an instance of a lower-order schema ... to an instance of a higher-order intermediate schema .... **However both these expectations were overturned** (Forrest & Mitchell, 1993)."

### THE ALGORITHMS

The GA: fitness-proportionate selection with sigma scaling, single-point crossover, point mutation. RMHC (random mutation hill climbing, a zero-temperature Monte Carlo method, suggested to them by Richard Palmer): pick a random string, evaluate; mutate one uniformly chosen locus; "If the mutation leads to an equal or higher fitness, the new string replaces the old string"; iterate. Also SAHC (steepest-ascent hill climbing) and NAHC (next-ascent hill climbing).

THE NUMBERS, verbatim, 200 runs on R1, counting function evaluations to find the optimum:
- "while the GA found the optimum on R1 in an average of **61,334** function evaluations, neither SAHC nor NAHC ever found the optimum within the maximum of **256,000** function evaluations."
- "However, RMHC found the optimum on R1 in an average of **6179** function evaluations — **nearly a factor of ten faster than the GA**."
- Their own reaction: "This striking difference on landscapes originally designed to be 'royal roads' for the GA underscores the need for a rigorous answer to the question posed earlier."

THE DIAGNOSIS — HITCHHIKING: "the simple GA ... optimized R1 quite slowly, at least in part because of 'hitchhiking': once an instance of a higher-order schema is discovered, its high fitness allows the schema to spread quickly in the population, with 0s in other positions in the string hitchhiking along with the 1s in the schema's defined positions. This slows down the discovery of schemas in the other positions, especially those that are close to the highly fit schema's defined positions. Hitchhiking can in general be a serious bottleneck for the GA." And: "One major impediment was hitchhiking, which limited implicit parallelism by fixing certain schema regions suboptimally."

### THE ANALYSIS

For N adjacent blocks of K ones each (R1: N = K = 8):
- RMHC time to find one block: a Markov-chain analysis (credited to Richard Palmer, personal communication) gives E(K,1) "slightly larger than 2^K, converging slowly to 2^K from above as K -> infinity"; for K = 8, E(K,1) = 301.2.
- Once a block is found, mutations inside it are wasted; the fraction of useful mutations is (KN-K)/KN. Hence
    E(K,N) = E(K,1)*[N/N + N/(N-1) + ... + N/1] = E(K,1)*N*(1 + 1/2 + ... + 1/N) ~= E(K,1)*N*(ln N + gamma).
  For K = N = 8 this gives **6549**, against the measured 6179 — "which agrees reasonably well with the expected value."
- THE IDEALIZED GA (IGA), a *lower bound* on what any GA could do on R1. It knows the target schemata, samples a fresh uniform random string each step, sequesters the first string containing a desired schema, and instantaneously crosses in each newly discovered schema. With p = 1/2^K the probability a random string contains a given block,
    E_N = SUM_t t*([1-(1-p)^t]^N - [1-(1-p)^(t-1)]^N) ~= (1/p)*SUM_{n=1}^N 1/n ~= **2^K(log N + 1)**.
  Detailed solution credited to Holland, SFI WP 93-10-062 (1993).
- THE PUNCHLINE, verbatim: "The major point is that the IGA gives an expected time that is on the order of **2^K log N**, where RMHC gives an expected time that is on the order of **2^K N log N**, a factor of N slower." The mechanism: "A primary reason is that the IGA perfectly implements implicit parallelism: each new string is completely independent of the previous one, so new samples are given independently to each schema region. In contrast, RMHC moves in the space of strings by single-bit mutations from an original string, so each new sample has all but one of the same bits as the previous sample. Thus each new string gives a new sample to only one schema region." And: "The IGA 'cheats' on each function evaluation, since it knows exactly the desired schemas, but in this way it gives a lower bound on the number of function evaluations that the GA will need on this problem."
- The four design conditions they extract for a real GA to approximate the IGA: (i) independent samples — large enough population, slow enough selection, enough mutation that no locus fixes; (ii) sequestering — selection strong enough to preserve found schemata but slow enough to prevent hitchhiking; (iii) effectively instantaneous crossover relative to discovery time; (iv) N large enough for the factor-N speedup to matter. They note these are not mutually compatible: "high mutation works against sequestering schemas".

THE PARTIAL REHABILITATION (the story does not end in defeat). They engineered R3 (R2's schemata separated by length-24 *introns*, with fitness 1 + u*level, u = 0.2) and then R4 (same level structure, 16 base schemata over **128-bit** strings, no introns; population 500, mutation rate 0.005, multipoint crossover with the number of crossover points drawn from a Poisson with mean 2.816). Result on R4, 37 runs, max 10^6 evaluations: "the time to reach level one is comparable for the two algorithms, but **the GA is much faster at reaching levels 2 and 3. Further, the GA discovers level 3 approximately twice as often as RMHC.**" Neither algorithm reached level 4. Their own framing: "Our modified Royal Road landscape R4, like R1, is not meant to be a realistic example of a problem to which one might apply a GA."

Finally, the modern rigorous resolution of the "does crossover assemble building blocks?" question — Dirk Sudholt, "How Crossover Speeds up Building Block Assembly in Genetic Algorithms", Evolutionary Computation 25(2):237-274, 2017, DOI 10.1162/evco_a_00171 (conf. version GECCO 2012, pp. 689-702, DOI 10.1145/2330163.2330260). VERIFIED with full abstract. Headline: on royal-road functions and OneMax, "using crossover makes every (mu+lambda) genetic algorithm at least twice as fast as the fastest evolutionary algorithm using only standard bit mutation, up to small-order terms and for moderate mu and lambda." Mechanism: "crossover is able to repair the disruptive effects of mutation in later generations", which "makes multibit mutations more useful" and shifts the optimal OneMax mutation rate upward. So there IS a rigorous, provable sense in which crossover assembles building blocks — but the constant is a factor of ~2, not the dramatic speedup the BBH rhetoric promised, and it was established 28 years after Goldberg 1989.
