### 2. THE FRAMEWORK AND PRECONDITIONS, VERBATIM

Every one of these is a genuine precondition, all stated explicitly in section II. I quote the paper directly because the preconditions are where the misuse happens.

(a) FINITE SEARCH SPACE. "We restrict attention to combinatorial optimization in which the search space X, though perhaps quite large, is finite."

(b) FINITE CODOMAIN. "We further assume that the space of possible 'cost' values Y is also finite. These restrictions are automatically met for optimization algorithms run on digital computers where typically Y is some 32 or 64 bit representation of the real numbers." (This is the clause that kills the common claim that NFL applies to continuous optimization: it applies to the *floating-point* discretisation, which is a different object from R^n. See section 7.)

(c) THE FUNCTION CLASS IS EVERYTHING. "An optimization problem f ... is represented as a mapping f: X -> Y and F = Y^X indicates the space of all possible problems. F is of size |Y|^|X| — a large but finite number." The uniform average is over all of F.

(d) NON-REPEATING, AND PERFORMANCE IS MEASURED ON THE SEQUENCE OF VISITED POINTS. The most frequently dropped condition. "we have elected to circumvent the problem entirely by comparing algorithms based on the number of **distinct** function evaluations they have performed. ... we compare algorithms by counting only their number of distinct calls to the oracle." A sample is "a time-ordered set of m distinct visited points", and d_m^y denotes "the ordered set of cost values". An algorithm is "a mapping from previously visited sets of points to a single new (i.e., previously unvisited) point in X. Formally, a: d in D -> {x | x not in d^x}."
Two crucial riders, both stated explicitly:
  - The sample must include everything the oracle was asked, not just what the algorithm kept: "because a sample contains all the previous points at which the oracle was consulted, it includes the Y values of all the neighbors of the current point, and not only the lowest cost one that the algorithm moves to. This must be taken into account when counting the value of m." (I.e. steepest-ascent hill climbing pays for every neighbour it evaluated.)
  - Performance must be a function of d_m^y alone: "any measure of the performance of an algorithm after m iterations is a function of the sample d_m^y. ... **Note that measures of performance based on factors other than d_m^y (e.g., wall clock time) are outside the scope of our results.**"

(e) WHICH ALGORITHMS ARE COVERED, AND WHICH ARE NOT. "our definition of an algorithm includes all common black-box optimization techniques like simulated annealing and evolutionary algorithms. (**Techniques like branch and bound are not included since they rely explicitly on the cost structure of partial solutions.**)"

(f) DETERMINISTIC/STOCHASTIC EQUIVALENCE. "As defined above, a search algorithm is deterministic; every sample maps to a unique new point. ... Nonetheless, it is worth noting that all of our results are extensible to nondeterministic algorithms, where the new point is chosen stochastically from the set of unvisited points." The mechanism (made explicit in Droste/Jansen/Wegener's reproof, section 3) is that a randomised strategy is a probability distribution over the finitely many deterministic strategies, so its expected cost is a weighted average of identical costs.

(g) THE PRIOR P(f). The probabilistic framing is explicit and is the interpretive hinge: "A crucial factor in the probabilistic framework is the distribution P(f). This distribution, defined over F, gives the probability that each f in F is the actual optimization problem at hand. ... P(f) is thus a prior specification of the class of the optimization problem at hand." NFL is the statement about the *uniform* P(f).

(h) THE THEOREM ITSELF. Section III sets up: "The precise question addressed in this section is: 'How does the set of problems F_1 for which algorithm a_1 performs better than algorithm a_2 compare to the set F_2 for which the reverse is true?' To address this question we compare the sum over all f of P(d_m^y | f, m, a_1) to the sum over all f of P(d_m^y | f, m, a_2)." Its own one-line gloss: "P(d_m^y | f, m, a) **is independent of a when averaged over all cost functions**."

Theorem 1 (FLAG: the displayed equations in the PDF are Type-3 glyphs my text extraction dropped, so I am reconstructing the formula from the surrounding prose, which I DID verify verbatim — the prose unambiguously fixes it): for any pair of algorithms a_1 and a_2,

  SUM_{f in F} P(d_m^y | f, m, a_1) = SUM_{f in F} P(d_m^y | f, m, a_2)

i.e. the uniform-average distribution over the sequence of observed cost values is identical for the two algorithms. The corollary is explicit and verbatim: "An immediate corollary of this result is that for any performance measure Phi(d_m^y), the average over all f of P(Phi(d_m^y) | f, m, a) is independent of a. **The precise way that the sample is mapped to a performance measure is unimportant.**"

Theorem 2 covers time-dependent cost functions (f shifted by an operator T between iterations) and gives an analogous NFL result.

Beyond the two NFL theorems the paper also contains: a geometric interpretation ("an inner product between the optimization algorithm and the distribution of optimization problems likely to be encountered fixes the distribution over likely performances" — performance is set by "how aligned [the algorithm's vector] is with P(f)"); information-theoretic applications; and *a priori* head-to-head minimax distinctions between algorithms — "distinctions that result **despite** the NFL theorems' enforcing of a type of uniformity over all algorithms." Worth flagging: even Wolpert & Macready's own paper contains results showing algorithms are NOT all equivalent under non-average comparisons.

### 3. THE SHARPENED NFL: CLOSURE UNDER PERMUTATION

The most important refinement, and the thing that converts NFL from a slogan into a usable criterion.

### 3a. SCHUMACHER, VOSE & WHITLEY — the "iff". VERIFIED bibliographically (verbatim from the reference list of Igel & Toussaint, arXiv:cs/0108011): **C. Schumacher, M. D. Vose & L. D. Whitley, "The no free lunch and description length", in L. Spector et al. (eds.), *Genetic and Evolutionary Computation Conference (GECCO 2001)*, pp. 565-570, Morgan Kaufmann, 2001.** No DOI (pre-ACM-DL GECCO; not in Crossref — I searched). Note Igel & Toussaint's reference list spells the first author "C. Schuhmacher"; the usual spelling is Schumacher. FLAG the spelling.
THEIR RESULT, verified via Igel & Toussaint's statement of it: "a sharpened version of the NFL theorem has been proven that states that NFL results hold for any subset F of the set of all possible functions **if and only if** F is closed under permutation [c.u.p.]." This is an iff — necessary *and* sufficient — which is what makes "is this class c.u.p.?" the right question to ask of any claimed NFL application.

### 3b. DEFINITION, verbatim (Igel & Toussaint): "Let pi: X -> X be a permutation (i.e. bijective function) of X. ... A set F subset of F is said to be closed under permutation (c.u.p.) if for any pi in P(X) and any function f in F the function f o pi is also in F."
The sharpened theorem, verbatim as Igel & Toussaint state it (their Theorem 1): "For any two algorithms a and b, any value k in R, and any performance measure c: SUM_{f in F} delta(k, c(Y(f,m,a))) = SUM_{f in F} delta(k, c(Y(f,m,b))) **iff** F is c.u.p." (delta = Kronecker delta.) Consequence, verbatim: "for any two algorithms a and b and any function f_a in F, where F is c.u.p., there is a function f_b in F on which b has the same performance as a on f_a."

### 3c. THE DROSTE/JANSEN/WEGENER REPROOF, with a clean generalised statement. VERIFIED, FULL TEXT OBTAINED: **Stefan Droste, Thomas Jansen & Ingo Wegener, "Optimization with randomized search heuristics — the (A)NFL theorem, realistic scenarios, and difficult functions", *Theoretical Computer Science* 287(1):131-144, 2002. DOI 10.1016/S0304-3975(02)00094-4.** 135 citations. Tech-report version read in full: Universitaet Dortmund SFB 531, 2001, hdl.handle.net/2003/5394, DOI 10.17877/DE290R-5645, file ci91.pdf.
Their formalisation of the original setting, verbatim: "**Scenario 1 (No Free Lunch Scenario).** The sets A and B are finite and B is completely ordered. The class F_{A,B} contains all functions f: A -> B and the aim is maximization. The function f is drawn uniformly from F_{A,B}."
Their cost measure (it matches Wolpert & Macready's distinct-evaluations convention): "we measure the resources spent by an algorithm H for the function f by the (expected) number of different inputs a in A such that f(a) has been evaluated until f is evaluated for an optimal input a*. Many popular search heuristics evaluate certain points more than once but this can be avoided by using a dictionary."
**Theorem 1 (Generalized NFL Theorem), verbatim:** "Let H be an arbitrary (randomized or deterministic) search heuristic for functions f in F subset of F_{A,B} where F is closed under permutations. Let r(H) be the average (under the uniform distribution on F) of the expected runtimes of H on F. Then r(H) is a value independent of H, i.e., r(H) is the same for all H."
Their proof is a short induction on |A| plus the randomised-to-deterministic averaging argument (citing Motwani & Raghavan 1995), and they say so: "The simplicity of the proof also implies that **'there is no secret or myth' behind the NFL theorem**."
Their one-sentence intuition, verbatim and worth quoting: "The generalized NFL theorem is by no means surprising. **If a class of functions does not change by any permutation on the input space, there is no structure which can be used for search.** Hence, all search strategies show the same behavior."

### 4. WHAT NFL DOES *NOT* IMPLY

### 4a. IT DOES NOT APPLY TO PRACTICALLY RELEVANT FUNCTION CLASSES — the quantitative version. VERIFIED, FULL TEXT: **Christian Igel & Marc Toussaint, "On classes of functions for which No Free Lunch results hold", *Information Processing Letters* 86(6):317-321, 2003. DOI 10.1016/S0020-0190(03)00222-9.** 104 citations. Preprint arXiv:cs/0108011 (21 Aug 2001) — the version I read in full.
THE COUNTING RESULT, verbatim:
- "There exist 2^(|Y|^|X|) - 1 non-empty subsets of F."
- "**Theorem 2.** The number of non-empty subsets of Y^X that are c.u.p. is given by 2^C(|X|+|Y|-1, |X|) - 1."
- Therefore the fraction of non-empty subsets that are c.u.p. is [2^C(|X|+|Y|-1,|X|) - 1] / [2^(|Y|^|X|) - 1]. "The fraction decreases for increasing |X| as well as for increasing |Y|. **Already for small |X| and |Y| the fraction almost vanishes, e.g., for a Boolean function f: {0,1}^3 -> {0,1} the fraction is << 10^-170.**" (Three bits in, two values out. That is the whole argument in one number.)
Proof machinery: they define a Y-histogram h: Y -> N_0 with SUM_y h(y) = |X|, and show (Lemma 1) that there are exactly C(|X|+|Y|-1, |X|) pairwise disjoint "basis classes" B_h partitioning F; that "Two functions f, g in F are h-equivalent iff there exists a permutation pi of X such that f o pi = g"; and that each B_h is exactly the permutation orbit of any function with that histogram. So the c.u.p. subsets are precisely the unions of basis classes — hence 2^(number of basis classes).

### 4b. STRUCTURE DESTROYS CLOSURE — the constructive half of the same paper. Quote this at anyone who says "NFL means all algorithms are equally good on my problem."
- "**Theorem 3.** A non-trivial neighborhood on X is not invariant under permutations of X." (A neighbourhood is a symmetric n: X x X -> {0,1}; non-trivial means some distinct pair is adjacent and some distinct pair is not.) Proof is two lines: pick a non-adjacent pair and an adjacent pair and permute one onto the other.
- "**Corollary 1.** If the maximum steepness s_max(f) of every function f in a non-empty subset F subset of F is constrained to be smaller than the maximal possible max_{f in F} d_max(f), then F is not c.u.p." (s_max(f) = max over adjacent x_i, x_j of d_Y(f(x_i), f(x_j)); d_max(f) = max over all pairs. "Steepness" is their formalisation of *strong causality*.)
- "**Corollary 2.** If the number of local minima of every function f in a non-empty subset F subset of F is constrained to be smaller than the maximal possible max_{f in F} l_max(f), then F is not c.u.p." With the concrete bound: "consider pseudo-Boolean function {0,1}^n -> R and let two points be neighbored iff they have Hamming-distance one. Then the maximum number of local minima is 2^(n-1)."
- Their conclusion, verbatim, the money quote for this whole section: "Based on the results in [Schumacher, Vose & Whitley], we have shown that the statement '**I'm only interested in a subset F of all possible functions, so the NFL theorems do not apply**' is true with a probability close to one (if F is chosen uniformly and Y and X have reasonable cardinalities). Further, the statements 'In my application domain, functions with maximum number of local minima are not realistic' and 'For some components, the objective functions under consideration will not have the maximal possible steepness' lead to scenarios where NFL does not hold."

### 4c. THE NON-UNIFORM-PRIOR VERSION. VERIFIED bibliographically, two Crossref records for the same paper (a DOI change): **Christian Igel & Marc Toussaint, "A No-Free-Lunch theorem for non-uniform distributions of target functions", *Journal of Mathematical Modelling and Algorithms* 3(4):313-322.** DOIs: **10.1007/s10852-005-2586-y** (Crossref year 2005) and **10.1023/B:JMMA.0000049381.24625.f7** (Crossref year 2004). 128 citations. Both records give vol. 3, no. 4, pp. 313-322; the year is cited as 2004 or 2005 depending on which record a citer picked. Content UNVERIFIED — no abstract deposited, no OA copy reached. The result (the field's standard reading, NOT verified text — FLAG): NFL generalises from the uniform distribution over a c.u.p. class to any distribution over target functions that is *invariant under permutations of the search space*, and conversely non-permutation-invariant priors break NFL. Do not attribute a specific formula to it on my authority.

### 4d. KOLMOGOROV COMPLEXITY / DESCRIPTION LENGTH, AND THE "FREE APPETIZER". VERIFIED, full text, from the Droste/Jansen/Wegener tech report. They set up four scenarios and argue NFL is irrelevant to all the realistic ones:
- "**Scenario 2 (One Shot Scenario).** One function f: A -> B has to be maximized." Their comment: "This is the real life situation. However, in black box optimization we do not know f. Theory in the one shot scenario is not possible, since there is an algorithm with runtime 1 (the difficulty is to find this algorithm)."
- "**Scenario 3 (Fixed Function Type Scenario).** It is known that f is chosen from some class of functions sharing some properties." — "this is more knowledge than we can expect to have in black box optimization."
- "**Scenario 4 (Restricted Black Box Optimization).** The scenario is the same as the NFL scenario with the only exception that F_{A,B} is replaced by some subset F'_{A,B} of functions whose complexity (in a sense to be specified) is restricted." Three instantiations: **4.1 Time Restricted** (bound t on the number of steps to evaluate f); **4.2 Size Restricted** (bound s on the representation size, e.g. circuit size); **4.3 Kolmogorov Complexity Restricted** (bound b on the Kolmogorov complexity of f) — they spell it "Kolmogoroff".
- THE SCALE ARGUMENT, verbatim: "The class of functions F_{A,B} contains |B|^|A| functions. The situation of A = {0,1}^100 is not unusual. Assuming, e.g., that B contains the set of 20-bit integers, the number of functions equals 2^(20*2^100) and, for each coding, only a tiny fraction of all functions can be described or evaluated with available resources. **Search heuristics are meaningless if it is too expensive to evaluate the given function.**"
- THEIR CONJECTURE AND ITS HONEST LIMIT, verbatim: "Our conjecture is that no such scenario where the restriction is not a trivial one allows an NFL theorem. **The problem with a proof is that nobody is able to discuss the class of functions with bounds like t(n) = n, s(n) = n, c(n) = n, or even c(n) = O(log(n)) (Kolmogoroff complexity) for functions on n Boolean variables. There are no average case results known for such classes of functions.**" What they could do: "The only possibility is to consider very small classes of functions where a complete case inspection is possible. Droste, Jansen, and Wegener (1999) have performed such considerations for different complexity measures. For all non-trivial situations considered they have proved that there is no NFL theorem. However, the advantages of the better algorithms are small (also because of the small sets A and B). **This has led to the claim that there is at least a 'free appetizer' in restricted black box optimization.**"
  - The "appetizer" paper, VERIFIED bibliographically (from Igel & Toussaint's reference list, verbatim): **Stefan Droste, Thomas Jansen & Ingo Wegener, "Perhaps not a free lunch but at least a free appetizer", in W. Banzhaf et al. (eds.), *Proceedings of the Genetic and Evolutionary Computation Conference (GECCO '99)*, pp. 833-839, Morgan Kaufmann, 1999.** No DOI.

### 4e. THE ANFL THEOREM — the result that cuts the *other* way. The one people forget, and the honest answer to "so I can just restrict the function class and win."
VERIFIED verbatim from the Droste/Jansen/Wegener tech report:
"**Theorem 2 (Almost No Free Lunch (ANFL) Theorem).** Let H be a randomized search strategy and f: {0,1}^n -> {0,1,...,N-1}. Then there exist at least N^(2^(n/3)) - 1 functions f*: {0,1}^n -> {0,1,...,N} which agree with f on all but at most 2^(n/3) inputs such that H does find the optimum of f* within 2^(n/3) steps with a probability bounded above by 2^(-n/3). Exponentially many of these functions have the additional property that their evaluation time, circuit size representation, and Kolmogoroff complexity is only by an additive term of O(n) larger than the corresponding complexity of f."
The proof idea in one line (verified): partition {0,1}^n into the 2^(2n/3) subcubes S_b indexed by the first 2n/3 bits; since H can evaluate at most 2^(n/3) points in 2^(n/3) steps, the pigeonhole principle gives a subcube b* with q*(b*) <= 2^(n/3)/2^(2n/3) = 2^(-n/3); hide a new optimum anywhere in S_{b*}.
THE MORAL, verbatim: "The ANFL theorem implies that **a search strategy has to pay for its success for some functions f with its bad behavior on many functions which are not much more complex than f. Hence, each search strategy bears some intuition in mind how functions for optimization look like.** One such assumption is that inputs a with large f(a) are most likely close to other inputs with large f-values."
Their three worked applications (all verbatim-sourced):
  - Needle in a haystack: "The proof of the ANFL theorem implies that each search heuristic fails on most of the haystack functions."
  - Trap, and the demolition of a cute trick: "The function Trap_n differs from OneMax_n only on the all zero string 0^n which is optimal for Trap_n .... This function is claimed to be difficult for search heuristics. However, this statement works in a one shot scenario and has to be wrong. A search heuristic may start by evaluating the function 0^n. **Whitley (1997) has discussed for each search heuristic H the variant H* defined by the following rule. If H evaluates f on a, then H* does the same for a and its bitwise complement abar. If H is efficient on OneMax_n then H* is efficient (losing at most a factor of 2) on Trap_n.** It is more meaningful to consider the class of trap functions Trap_{n,b} .... The proof of the ANFL theorem implies that each search heuristic fails on most of the trap functions. **This proves that Whitley's idea only helps in very special situations.**" (The Whitley reference is to the Gray-vs-binary free-lunch line — VERIFIED citation from Igel & Toussaint: D. Whitley, "A free lunch proof for gray versus binary encodings", GECCO '99, vol. 1, pp. 726-733, Morgan Kaufmann, 1999. Also Whitley, "Functions as Permutations: Regarding No Free Lunch, Walsh Analysis and Summary Statistics", PPSN VI, LNCS, pp. 169-178, 2000, DOI 10.1007/3-540-45356-3_17 — VERIFIED via Crossref.)
  - They then construct "a non-artificial and simple function ... which is a polynomial of degree 3" that is provably hard for simulated annealing and EAs — hardness does not require pathological description length. (Their section 5; I read the heading and opening but not the full construction. FLAG the details as unverified.)

### 6. THE COEVOLUTIONARY FREE LUNCH

VERIFIED with full abstract (Crossref + OpenAlex): **David H. Wolpert & William G. Macready, "Coevolutionary Free Lunches", *IEEE Transactions on Evolutionary Computation* 9(6):721-735, 2005. DOI 10.1109/TEVC.2005.856205.** 239 citations.
The result, in their own words (verbatim from the abstract):
- The framework: "it is now known that an inner product between the optimization algorithm and the distribution of optimization problems likely to be encountered fixes the distribution over likely performances in running that algorithm."
- Scope: "In addition to the optimization scenarios addressed in the NFL results, this framework covers multiarmed bandit problems and evolution of multiple coevolving players. As a particular instance of the latter, it covers 'self-play' problems. In these problems, the set of players work together to produce a champion, who then engages one or more antagonists in a subsequent multiplayer game."
- **THE POSITIVE RESULT, verbatim: "In contrast to the traditional optimization case where the NFL results hold, we show that in self-play there are free lunches: in coevolution some algorithms have better performance than other algorithms, averaged across all possible problems."**
- **THE CRUCIAL CAVEAT, verbatim: "However, in the typical coevolutionary scenarios encountered in biology, where there is no champion, the NFL theorems still hold."**
So the free lunch is specifically about *self-play producing a champion who is then externally evaluated*, not about coevolution in general. Anyone citing "coevolution escapes NFL" without the champion/no-champion distinction is overclaiming.
Companion on the bandit side, VERIFIED: W. G. Macready & D. H. Wolpert, "Bandit problems and the exploration/exploitation tradeoff", IEEE TEC 2(1):2-22, 1998, DOI 10.1109/4235.728210.
Later Wolpert retrospective, VERIFIED: David H. Wolpert, "What Is Important About the No Free Lunch Theorems?", in *Black Box Optimization, Machine Learning, and No-Free Lunch Theorems*, Springer Optimization and Its Applications, pp. 373-388, 2021, DOI 10.1007/978-3-030-66515-9_13. ### 7. THE CONTINUOUS-DOMAIN ESCAPE, AND THE NFL-AS-LICENCE FALLACY

Two things that matter for a reference document, verified via the Camacho-Villalon/Dorigo/Stuetzle critique literature (details in Part 4):
- NFL does not hold in the continuous domain. VERIFIED as their claim with citations: they state the theorems "do not hold for multi-objective problems or continuous domains", citing **Auger & Teytaud, "Continuous lunches are free!" (2007) and their 2010 follow-up**. FLAG: I verified that Camacho-Villalon et al. make this claim and cite Auger & Teytaud; I did NOT independently fetch the Auger & Teytaud papers, so treat their exact statement and venue as unverified. (The commonly cited version is Anne Auger & Olivier Teytaud, "Continuous Lunches Are Free Plus the Design of Optimal Optimization Algorithms", Algorithmica 57(1):121-146, 2010 — FLAG this DOI/pagination as unverified in this session.)
- THE "NFL LICENSES NEW ALGORITHMS" FALLACY — NFL's single largest real-world harm, worth a paragraph in any reference doc. Camacho-Villalon, Dorigo & Stuetzle (ITOR 30(6), Part 4) quote the offenders verbatim: Mirjalili et al. 2014 (Grey Wolf Optimizer), pp. 46-47 — "Obviously, the No Free Lunch theorem makes this field of study highly active which results in enhancing current approaches and proposing new meta-heuristics every year. This also motivates our attempts to develop a new meta-heuristic with inspiration from grey wolves."; and Mirjalili 2015 (Antlion Optimizer), p. 81 — "...the so-called No Free Lunch theorem allows researchers to propose new algorithms." Their own abstract names this: "we discuss the rationale often presented by the authors of metaphor-based algorithms as their motivation to propose more algorithms of this type, **which is based on a wrong understanding of the no-free-lunch theorems for optimization.**" Note the irony: three of the six algorithms they dissect are continuous optimisers, where NFL as stated does not even apply.
- A third framing, VERIFIED as quoted in Bartz-Beielstein et al. (arXiv:2007.03488), who quote Culberson (1998): "In the context of search problems, the NFL theorem strictly only applies if arbitrary search landscapes are considered, while the instances of basically any search problem of interest have compact descriptions and therefore cannot give rise to arbitrary search landscapes." (Verified as Bartz-Beielstein et al.'s quotation of Culberson, not from Culberson directly.)

### SUMMARY OF WHAT NFL DOES AND DOES NOT LICENSE
DOES: for a uniform prior over a permutation-closed function class, with non-repeating black-box algorithms whose performance is scored on the sequence of distinct cost values visited, all algorithms have identical average performance. Also: head-to-head minimax distinctions survive (Wolpert & Macready's own section V-A), and self-play coevolution has genuine free lunches.
DOES NOT: say anything about any class that is not permutation-closed — essentially every class with a neighbourhood structure, a steepness bound, a bound on the number of local optima, or a bounded description length (Igel & Toussaint: the c.u.p. fraction is << 10^-170 already for {0,1}^3 -> {0,1}). Does not apply to continuous domains, to multi-objective problems, to algorithms that exploit partial-solution cost structure (branch and bound is explicitly excluded by Wolpert & Macready), or to performance measures outside d_m^y (e.g. wall-clock time). And does not license inventing a new metaheuristic — but the ANFL theorem also denies you the opposite comfort: whatever your heuristic is good at, there are exponentially many barely-more-complex functions on which it fails with probability 1 - 2^(-n/3).
