# Gender identity

The job is to explain gender terms the way the people who use them mean them, keep the layers
straight, and never hand someone a verdict about who they are. The per-term detail (definitions,
microlabels, coinage, flags) is in `references/glossary.md`. Load it when a question names a
specific term or flag.

## Keep the layers apart

Most confusion comes from collapsing different things into one word. Name the layer before you
answer.

| Layer | What it describes | Example terms |
|---|---|---|
| Sex characteristics | Body: chromosomes, hormones, anatomy. Assigned at birth from a glance at anatomy | AFAB, AMAB, intersex |
| Gender identity | The internal sense of one's own gender, or of having none | woman, man, non-binary, agender, demigirl |
| Gender expression | How someone presents: clothes, voice, mannerisms, roles | feminine, masculine, androgynous, femme, masc |
| Gender modality | How identity relates to the sex assigned at birth | cisgender, transgender |
| Orientation | Who someone is attracted to | lesbian, gay, bi, asexual, demisexual |

The confusions this table prevents:

- **Androgynous is a look, non-binary is an identity.** An androgynous cis man exists. So does a
  very feminine non-binary person.
- **Gender non-conforming is about expression and roles, not identity.** Cis and trans men and
  women can all be GNC. A feminine trans man is as non-conforming as a feminine cis man.
- **Intersex is about sex characteristics.** Intersex people aren't automatically non-binary, and
  non-binary people aren't intersex. Someone can be both.
- **Demigender is not demisexual.** They share a prefix and nothing else: one is identity, the
  other orientation.
- **Agender is not gender apathy.** An agender person has no gender, or a neutral one. A gender-apathetic person has a gender,
  often cis, and doesn't think about it.
- **Feminine and masculine aren't identities by default.** They're culturally variable sets of
  traits and presentations that anyone can carry. *Femme* and *masc* are different: they're
  intentional, queer-coded uses of those expressions.

## How the umbrellas nest, and where they leak

Roughly: **transgender** contains **non-binary**, which contains **agender**, **demigender**,
**genderfluid** and **xenogender**, among many others. **Genderqueer** overlaps non-binary heavily
but is broader and more political. **Transfeminine** and **transmasculine** cut across all of it
by direction: predominantly feminine or masculine, in identity, expression or both.

Every umbrella leaks, and saying so is part of being accurate:

- Some non-binary people don't call themselves trans, and a few call themselves cisgender.
- Some agender people reject "non-binary" because it still implies having a gender.
- Some transfeminine and transmasculine people don't identify as women or men at all.
- Whether transfem/transmasc require a particular assigned sex (AMAB/AFAB) is itself
  disputed.

So give the common nesting, then say it's a map, not a rule.

## Rails

1. **A label means what its user says it means.** Give the common definition, then note that
   usage varies by person, community and region. Never correct someone's account of their own
   label. When a third party is involved, the answer is "ask them what it means to them".
2. **Never assign a label.** If someone is questioning, describe what each candidate term
   captures and offer questions to sit with. "Which of these feels closer?" works; "you're
   probably demigender" doesn't. Say plainly that no label is required, that labels can change,
   and that a change isn't proof the earlier one was fake. That last point matters for
   genderfluid people in particular, who hear "it's a phase" constantly.
3. **Dysphoria is not the entry test.** In community use it's the distress of a mismatch between
   gender and body or social treatment, and it can be physical, social, mild or severe. Many trans
   and non-binary people have little or none. Many describe *gender euphoria*, the good feeling
   of being seen correctly, as the clearer signal. For the **clinical diagnosis**, defer to the
   `dsm-gender-dysphoria` skill. Don't list the DSM criteria here: they're APA-copyrighted and
   that skill handles the diagnosis properly.
4. **Medical and legal specifics are out of scope.** They change fast and differ by country.
   The source wiki's description of UK services, for example, predates the 2024 closure of the
   Tavistock GIDS clinic. Point to current sources: WPATH Standards of Care 8, the person's own
   clinicians, and local legal aid or trans organisations. Don't state treatment
   pathways or waiting rules as fact.
5. **Treat microlabels evenly.** Terms like genderfae, catgender or demiflux are mostly
   community-coined, many on Tumblr in 2014–2017, and used by small numbers. In the 2025 Gender
   Census, a self-selected survey, about 1.1% of respondents wrote in "xenogender". Describe them
   accurately and without mockery. Don't inflate how widespread they are, and don't dismiss them
   as fake either.
6. **Present live debates as debates.** Give each side's actual argument and the evidence status.
   Don't take a side unless asked, and don't preach either way.
7. **Don't infer anyone's gender from their appearance, voice or name.** That's exactly the
   expression-versus-identity mistake the layers table exists to stop.
8. **Distress outranks the glossary.** If someone sounds in crisis, respond to that first. In
   Canada and the US, call or text **988**. **Trans Lifeline**, peer support run by trans people,
   is at 877-565-8860.

## Live debates (rail 6)

- **Xenogender inclusion.** Xenogender people describe genders through animals, plants, elements
  or concepts. Supporters say this fills a real lexical gap, may connect to synaesthesia-like
  perception, and fits the history of new identity words like *butch* and *boi*, which were once
  new too. Critics within trans communities argue that xenogender identities aren't targeted the
  way trans people are, and that folding them into trans politics hurts a movement fighting for
  legal recognition and safety. Both positions are held in good faith by people inside the
  community.
- **Transmedicalism.** This is the view that being trans requires dysphoria, sometimes a
  diagnosis. Opponents ("tucute" is the transmedicalists' term for them) say it gatekeeps and
  excludes many non-binary people. The mainstream clinical direction has moved away from requiring
  distress. ICD-11 reclassified the condition as "gender incongruence", outside the mental
  disorders chapter.
- **Rapid-onset gender dysphoria (ROGD).** This is a hypothesis that some adolescents' dysphoria
  spreads socially. It was proposed in a 2018 PLOS ONE paper based on surveys of parents, and the
  paper was corrected in 2019. ROGD isn't a diagnosis in the DSM-5-TR or ICD-11. Several
  professional bodies have cautioned against using it clinically, and a later study of clinical
  data from trans adolescents found no support for it. Some clinicians still argue it deserves
  research. Report that state accurately; don't present it either as settled science or as
  unaskable.

## Pronouns and neutral language

- **English:** ask, then use what you're given.
  - *They/them* has been used for single people for over 600 years, and Merriam-Webster added
    the non-binary sense in 2019.
  - *Mx* (pronounced "mix") is the neutral honorific, first recorded in 1977.
  - Some people use neopronouns (xe/xem, ze/hir, fae/faer). Some use no pronouns, only their
    name. Some use different pronouns at different times, which is common for genderfluid
    people.
  - Gender-neutral job titles (firefighter, flight attendant) serve everyone.
- **French** (relevant in Quebec):
  - *iel* (plural *iels*) is the most common neutral pronoun, and Le Robert's online
    dictionary added it in 2021.
  - Other forms: *toustes*, *celleux*, the median dot (*étudiant·e·s*), and épicène wording,
    which avoids gender altogether (*le personnel enseignant*, *la communauté étudiante*).
  - The OQLF favours épicène wording and is cautious about néopronoms like *iel* in texts for a
    general audience. Check its Banque de dépannage linguistique for the current wording before
    advising on a formal text.
  - Common French identity terms: *non-binaire*, *genre fluide*, *agenre*, *genderqueer* (also
    used in French) and *transféminin/transmasculin*.
- **Respectful defaults:**
  - Say "assigned female/male at birth" (AFAB/AMAB), not "biologically female/male".
  - Write *trans woman* as two words: trans is an adjective. Avoid "transgendered" and
    "a transgender".
  - *Enby* is an affectionate nickname some non-binary people like and some don't. In writing,
    abbreviate non-binary as **NBi**, because NB is also used for "non-Black".

## Flags

When asked about a flag, give its stripes, what they're said to mean, the designer and the
date, and say so when the creator or the meaning is unknown, which is common. The table and the
disputed attributions are in `references/glossary.md`.

## Scope

This skill covers the terms its sources cover, framed broadly enough to answer adjacent
questions. It doesn't cover Two-Spirit, which is a culturally specific Indigenous identity with
its own meaning; it's not a synonym for non-binary and not for non-Indigenous people to claim. It
also doesn't cover other culturally specific roles (hijra, fa'afafine, muxe). Name those as
distinct, and don't fold them into Western categories.
